6.1.1 · SRAM and DRAM

A seesaw that stays, a cup that leaks

Priya has two kinds of cubbies. The fancy ones are a little seesaw: they only like left or right, and they stay there as long as the lights stay on. That is SRAM — six transistors per bit, used for cache.

The cheap ones are a tiny cup of charge. One transistor, one capacitor. They forget in 10 to 100 milliseconds unless you pour the bit back in. That refill is called refresh. That is DRAM, used for main memory.

Try it: flip the SRAM seesaw — it stays. Watch the DRAM cup drain, then press Refresh. Switch to RAS / CAS and step a read of supercell (2, 1).

What the book says

Figure 6.2: SRAM uses 6 transistors per bit, relative access time 1×, is persistent while powered, is not sensitive to disturbance, and costs about 1,000× as much as DRAM. DRAM uses 1 transistor per bit, access 10×, is not persistent, is sensitive, and costs 1×. SRAM is for cache memory; DRAM is for main memory and frame buffers.

“Due to its bistable nature, an SRAM memory cell will retain its value indefinitely, as long as it is kept powered.” DRAM stores charge on a capacitor of about 30 femtofarads. Leakage empties it in around 10 to 100 milliseconds, so the memory system must refresh every bit by reading it out and rewriting it.

Figure 6.3 is a 16 × 8 DRAM: 16 supercells, 8 bits each, 4 rows and 4 columns. To read supercell (2, 1) the controller sends RAS = 2, which copies row 2 into an internal row buffer, then CAS = 1, which returns those 8 bits (Figure 6.4). A 64 MB DIMM can be eight 8M × 8 chips that together return a 64-bit word.

6.1.2 · rotating disks

Slide the arm, wait for the groove, then pour

Omar’s disk is a stack of platters. Each surface is rings called tracks, cut into sectors of typically 512 bytes. The arm seeks to the right track, then the platter spins until the sector arrives, then the bits stream out.

Almost all the wait is the first two steps. The leftover bytes in that sector are nearly free. A 64-bit word in SRAM is about 4 ns; the same idea on disk is about 10 ms — tens of thousands of times longer.

Try it: press Step access. Watch seek (9 ms), rotation (about 4 ms), then transfer (0.02 ms) on the book’s 7,200 RPM disk.

What the book says

Figure 6.9–6.10: platters, surfaces, tracks, sectors, gaps, cylinders, a spindle at 5,400 to 15,000 RPM, and a read/write head on an actuator arm. Average seek is typically 3 to 9 ms (max about 20 ms). Average rotation is half a turn. Transfer of one sector is a tiny slice of a turn.

Worked example: 7,200 RPM, Tavg seek = 9 ms, 400 sectors/track. Tavg rotation ≈ 4 ms, Tavg transfer ≈ 0.02 ms, Taccess = 13.02 ms. “The time to access the 512 bytes in a disk sector is dominated by the seek time and the rotational latency.” Twice the seek time is a handy estimate.

A 64-bit word is roughly 4 ns in SRAM and 60 ns in DRAM. Reading a 512-byte block is about 256 ns (SRAM) or 4,000 ns (DRAM). A disk access of roughly 10 ms is about 40,000 times greater than SRAM and 2,500 times greater than DRAM. The OS sees a sequence of logical blocks; the disk controller maps them to (surface, track, sector). After the CPU writes a command to a memory-mapped port, the controller can DMA the sector into memory and interrupt.

Seek moves the arm. Rotation waits for the sector. Transfer is the short part.
6.1.3 · solid state disks

Write a page; to change it, erase the chapter

Mei’s flash chip is not a spinning platter. It is pages grouped into blocks. She can read a page quickly. She can write a page only if that whole block was just erased — every bit set to 1. A used page cannot be patched in place.

So a random rewrite copies the neighbours to a fresh block, then erases the old one. Erase takes on the order of 1 ms. A block dies after roughly 100,000 writes. Reads stay faster than writes.

Try it: write an erased page (fast). Then write the same page again and watch the block erase first.

What the book says

Figure 6.13: an SSD plugs into a disk slot (USB or SATA). Flash chips plus a flash translation layer pretend to be logical disk blocks. Pages are typically 512 bytes to 4 KB; a block is 32–128 pages (16 KB to 512 KB). Data move in page units. “A page can be written only after the entire block to which it belongs has been erased.” “A block wears out after roughly 100,000 repeated writes.”

Figure 6.14 (Intel SSD 730): sequential read 550 MB/s, write 470 MB/s; random 89,000 / 74,000 IOPS; average sequential access 50 μs read, 60 μs write (4 KB). Random writes are slower because erase is about 1 ms and because a write to a live page must copy sibling pages to a new erased block.

Wear-leveling spreads erasures. “SSDs are about 30 times more expensive per byte than rotating disks,” with no moving parts, less power, and much faster random reads. Intel’s endurance example for that drive is about 128 petabytes of writes.

6.2 · locality

Come back soon, or knock on the next door

Aisha adds up a row of lockers. She keeps the running total in her hand and looks at it every time — same address, again and again. That is temporal locality. She also opens locker 0, then 1, then 2. Neighbours. That is spatial locality.

Walking a C array along a row is stride-1: the next integer is the next address. Walking down a column hops a whole row each step. Same additions, worse spatial locality.

Try it: step sumvec. Then switch to rows and to cols and watch the visit order on the book’s 2 × 3 picture.

What the book says

“In a program with good temporal locality, a memory location that is referenced once is likely to be referenced again multiple times in the near future.” Spatial locality is the neighbour rule: if you touch an address, you are likely to touch a nearby one soon.

Figure 6.17 sumvec: scalar sum has good temporal locality and no spatial locality; vector v (N = 8) is a stride-1 scan — good spatial, poor temporal (each element once). Figure 6.18 sumarrayrows (M = 2, N = 3) matches row-major layout. Figure 6.19 sumarraycols is stride-N. Loops also give the instruction stream both kinds of locality; code is rarely written at run time.

On the authors’ Core i7, matrix-multiplication kernels that do the same arithmetic but differ in locality can vary by a factor of almost 40.

        6.3 · the memory hierarchy
        
Each shelf is a cache for the one below

        
Luca’s desk holds today’s page. His locker holds this week’s books. The library holds the town. The warehouse holds everything. Go down and storage gets bigger, slower, and cheaper per byte. Go up and it shrinks and speeds up.

        
A cache is just a small fast shelf that keeps copies of blocks from the bigger shelf under it. Ask the desk first. If the page is there, that is a hit. If not, a miss — fetch the block, maybe evict a victim, then try again.

        
Try it: tap a level to read its job and latency. Press Fetch from disk and watch a block climb, leaving a copy on every shelf.

        

          What the book says
          

            
Figure 6.21: L0 registers; L1–L3 SRAM caches; L4 DRAM main memory; L5 local disks; L6 remote disks (AFS, NFS, Web servers). “The overall effect is a large pool of memory that costs as much as the cheap storage near the bottom of the hierarchy but that serves data to programs at the rate of the fast storage near the top of the hierarchy.”

            
Figure 6.22: level k + 1 is blocks 0–15; level k holds four of them (4, 9, 14, 3). Transfers use the block size of that pair of levels — words between L0 and L1, tens of bytes between the SRAM caches, hundreds or thousands of bytes between disk and memory. Hardware caches often place block i in slot (i mod n). Figure 6.23: registers 0 cycles; L1 4; L2 10; L3 50; virtual memory / buffer cache 200; disk cache 100,000; network / browser on the order of 10,000,000.

            
Registers are managed by the compiler; L1–L3 by hardware; main memory as a disk cache by the OS plus MMU; a local disk as an AFS cache by client software. SSDs sit between DRAM and rotating disk. Some sites add tape under disk.

        6.4.1–6.4.2 · (S, E, B, m)
        
Middle bits pick the room; the tag checks the name

        
Noor’s mailroom has S rooms (sets). Each room has E cubbies (lines). Each cubby holds a B-byte packet (the block), a valid bit, and a tag. The whole cache’s data size is C = S × E × B.

        
An address splits into tag, set index, and block offset. The index picks the room. The tag asks “is this my packet?” The offset picks a byte inside. One line per room is a direct-mapped cache — replacement is “kick whoever is already there.”

        
Try it: step the book’s 4-bit toy: reads of 0, 1, 13, 8, then 0 again. Or tap an address 0–15.

        

          What the book says
          

            
Figure 6.25–6.26: S = 2s sets, E lines per set, B = 2b bytes per block, m address bits, t = m − (s + b) tag bits, C = B × E × S (valid and tag bits not counted). Finding a word is set selection, line matching (valid and tag), then word extraction from the offset.

            
Worked cache (S, E, B, m) = (4, 1, 2, 4). Figure 6.30 enumerates addresses 0–15. Reads: 0 miss (load block 0 into set 0); 1 hit; 13 miss (block 6 into set 2); 8 miss (block 4 replaces block 0 in set 0); 0 miss again — a conflict miss. Middle index bits keep adjacent blocks in different sets (Figure 6.31).

            
Figure 6.39, Intel Core i7: L1 i-cache and d-cache each 32 KB, E = 8, B = 64 B, S = 64, 4 cycles; L2 unified 256 KB, E = 8, S = 512, 10 cycles; L3 shared 8 MB, E = 16, S = 8,192, 40–75 cycles. A 2-way cache (Figure 6.32) searches E tags in a set; a fully associative cache is one set (used for small things such as a TLB).

        6.3.1 · 6.4.2 · kinds of misses
        
Empty fridge, two jars, or a picnic that will not fit

        
Soren opens the fridge. If it is empty, the first grab always misses — a cold (compulsory) miss. After a few visits the fridge is warm.

        
Two jars can keep stealing the same shelf even when other shelves are free. That is a conflict miss. And if the picnic is simply bigger than the fridge, you get capacity misses: the working set does not fit.

        
Try it: run Cold (first fill), then Conflict (addresses 0 and 8 fight for set 0), then Capacity (five blocks in a four-line cache).

        

          What the book says
          

            
“An empty cache is sometimes referred to as a cold cache, and misses of this kind are called compulsory misses or cold misses.” A conflict miss happens when the cache is large enough but placement maps the live blocks onto the same line — in Figure 6.22, ping-ponging blocks 0 and 8 into slot (i mod 4). A capacity miss means the working set is larger than the cache.

            
The book’s (4, 1, 2, 4) run of address 8 then 0 is a conflict: set 0 has room in the cache as a whole, but only one line. dotprod of two 8-float vectors that start 32 bytes apart, with a 32-byte two-set cache and 16-byte blocks, thrashes on every x[i] and y[i]. Padding x to 12 floats separates the sets. LRU or LFU can choose a victim in associative caches; random is the simple policy.

        6.4.5 · writes
        
Tell the warehouse now, or pin a dirty note

        
Yara changes a number that is already in the cubby. Write-through also shouts the new value down the hall right now — simple, but every store uses the bus. Write-back sticks a dirty bit on the cubby and only tells the hall when that cubby is evicted.

        
On a write miss, write-allocate fetches the block first (like a read miss), then writes. No-write-allocate skips the cache and writes the word downstairs. Write-through caches are typically no-write-allocate; write-back caches are typically write-allocate.

        
Try it: write the cached word both ways. Then evict. Only write-back sends a block downstairs at eviction.

        

          What the book says
          

            
Write-through updates the next lower level on every write hit and so makes bus traffic. Write-back waits until eviction and needs a dirty bit per line. Write-allocate loads the block on a write miss; no-write-allocate writes through to the next level and leaves the cache alone.

            
The authors tell you to assume write-back, write-allocate when you write cache-friendly code: lower levels already use write-back (virtual memory uses it exclusively), modern chips use write-back at every level, and that policy is symmetric with reads — it tries to exploit locality. Write-through can sit behind a write buffer; write-back leaves more bus bandwidth for DMA.

          

        

      

      

        
One word, two policies

        

        

        
Write-through updates memory at once. Write-back waits for eviction.

      

    

  


  

    

      

        6.5 · writing cache-friendly code
        
Walk the hallway, do not hop between floors

        
Hiro’s grid is stored row by row. A cache block is four ints wide. Walking a row is miss, hit, hit, hit — three free neighbours after you pay for the block. Walking a column jumps a whole row each time. If the grid is bigger than the cache, every hop misses.

        
Two rules: put the hot counters in registers, and scan memory in the order it is laid out (stride-1). On the authors’ desktop, sumarrayrows ran 25 times faster than sumarraycols for large arrays.

        
Try it: walk by rows, then by columns, on a 4 × 4 grid with 16-byte blocks and a one-block cache.

        

          What the book says
          

            
Focus on inner loops of core functions. A stride-k scan (k in words) averages min(1, (word size × k)/B) misses per iteration — best at k = 1. For sumvec with 4-byte words, B = 16 bytes, and a cold cache: miss, hit, hit, hit, repeat — three of four hit.

            
sumarrayrows is the same pattern per row. sumarraycols is stride-N. If the array fits, the miss rate can still fall to 1/4 after warmup; if it does not, “each and every access of a[i][j] will miss.” On their desktop machine, sumarrayrows runs 25 times faster than sumarraycols for large array sizes.

            
A block is the packet that moves; a line is the container (valid, tag, block); a set is one or more lines. People often say “line size” for block size.

        6.6 · caches and program performance
        
A mountain of speed, and a 40-times multiply

        
Tomás times how fast the machine can read. Small working set, small stride: he is on a high ridge. Big set, huge stride: he is in a valley. That picture is the memory mountain. On a Core i7 Haswell it runs from over 14 GB/s down to 900 MB/s.

        
Six ways to multiply matrices do the same arithmetic. The inner loop that walks B and C along rows (kij / ikj) makes 0.50 misses per iteration. The worst pair makes 2.00. For large n the fastest pair is almost 40 times faster.

        
Try it: slide size and stride on the mountain. Then open Multiply and compare the three inner-loop classes.

        

          What the book says
          

            
Figure 6.40–6.41: read throughput versus size (16 KB to 128 MB) and stride (1 to 12 eights of a long int) on a 2.1 GHz Core i7 Haswell with 32 KB L1 d-cache, 256 KB L2, 8 MB L3, 64 B blocks. L1 peak over 14 GB/s; main-memory floor 900 MB/s — more than an order of magnitude. A stride-1 ridge stays near 12 GB/s even past L1 and L2 because of hardware prefetch. At stride 8 (one 64-byte line per access) the L3 slope goes flat. MB/s here means 106 bytes/s.

            
Figure 6.44–6.46: six loop orders, doubles, B = 32. Class AB (ijk, jik) 1.25 misses/iter; AC (jki, kji) 2.00; BC (kij, ikj) 0.50. “For large values of n, the fastest version runs almost 40 times faster than the slowest version, even though each performs the same number of floating-point arithmetic operations.” Miss rate beat raw access count. Prefetch keeps the best pair flat even when the arrays dwarf SRAM.

Words to know
The chapter’s grown-up words, in plain language.

      

        
SRAM
Six-transistor bistable cell. Fast, expensive, no refresh. Cache.

        
DRAM
Charge on a capacitor. Dense, cheap, must refresh every 10–100 ms.

        
supercell
The book’s word for one DRAM array element of w bits.

        
seek / rotation / transfer
The three waits to read a disk sector. The first two dominate.

        
SSD
Flash pages in erasable blocks, with a translation layer that looks like a disk.

        
temporal locality
Use the same address again soon.

        
spatial locality
Use a nearby address next. Stride-1 is the friendly case.

        
cache line
A slot: valid bit, tag, and one block.

        
set / block
A set is E lines. A block is the B-byte packet that moves.

        
cold / conflict / capacity
Empty cache; same set fights; working set too big.

        
write-through / write-back
Update downstairs now, or later when the dirty line is evicted.

        
miss rate
Misses divided by references. Hit rate is 1 minus that.

        
miss penalty
The extra time a miss costs — about 10, 50, or 200 cycles from L2, L3, or DRAM.

        
memory mountain
Read throughput versus working-set size and stride.
