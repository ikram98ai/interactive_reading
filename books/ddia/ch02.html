Opening · functional and nonfunctional requirements

What it does, and how well it does it

Ola is planning a photo app. The list starts with what it must do: an upload button, an album screen, a share link. Those are its functional requirements — the screens, the buttons, and what each one is supposed to do.

Then come the things nobody writes down because they seem obvious: it should be quick, it shouldn’t lose photos, it should keep strangers out, follow the law, and be easy to fix next year. Those are nonfunctional requirements. They matter just as much: an app that is unbearably slow or unreliable might as well not exist.

This chapter takes four of them — performance, reliability, scalability and maintainability — and gives you the words to pin each one down.

Try it: each card is one line from Ola’s list. Press Functional or Nonfunctional. A nonfunctional card then drops into the part of the chapter that covers it — or into “not in this book.”

What the book says

If you are building an application, you will be driven by a list of requirements. At the top is most likely the functionality the application must offer: what screens and what buttons you need, and what each operation is supposed to do. These are your functional requirements.

You probably also have nonfunctional requirements: for example, the app should be fast, reliable, secure, legally compliant and easy to maintain. They might not be explicitly written down because they seem somewhat obvious, but they are just as important as the app’s functionality: “an app that is unbearably slow or unreliable might as well not exist.”

Many nonfunctional requirements, such as security, fall outside the scope of the book. The chapter looks at defining and measuring the performance of a system; what it means for a service to be reliable — continuing to work correctly, even when things go wrong; allowing a system to be scalable by having efficient ways of adding computing capacity as load grows; and making it easier to maintain a system in the long term.

Case study · representing users, posts and follows

The timeline that is cooked fresh on every visit

Picture a social network where people post short messages and follow each other. Everything lives in a relational database with three tables: users, posts, and follows — who follows whom.

What people read most is their home timeline: the newest posts from everyone they follow. One SQL query builds it: find whom I follow, fetch their posts, sort by time, keep the newest 1,000.

Posts should show up within five seconds, so the simple plan is for every phone to run that query again every five seconds. That’s polling. With 10 million people online, it’s 2 million queries a second — and each one gathers posts from about 200 people, so 400 million lookups a second.

Try it: pick whose timeline to build and press Run query. Watch the database walk the follows table, collect posts and sort them, one line of SQL at a time. Then, in the second machine, set how many people are online and how often their phones poll.

What the book says

The case study imagines a social network in the style of X (formerly Twitter), where users post messages and follow other users — “a huge simplification of how such a service actually works.” Assume users make 500 million posts per day, or 5,800 posts per second on average, occasionally spiking to as high as 150,000 posts per second. The average user follows 200 people and has 200 followers, though the range is very wide: most people have only a handful of followers, and a few celebrities, such as Barack Obama, have over 100 million.

All the data lives in a relational database (Figure 2-1): one table for users, one for posts, one for follow relationships. The main read operation is the home timeline — recent posts by people the user is following, ignoring ads and suggested posts. The query joins posts to follows and users, filters on the current user as follower, orders by timestamp descending and keeps the most recent 1,000.

Followers should see a post within five seconds. If each client repeats the query every five seconds while online (polling) and 10 million users are online, that’s 2 million queries per second. Following 200 people, each query fetches recent posts from 200 accounts and merges them: 400 million lookups per second — and that’s the average case. For users who follow tens of thousands of accounts, the query is very expensive and difficult to make fast.

Case study · materializing and updating timelines

Deliver every post to every mailbox

Instead of everybody coming to ask, do the work once, at the moment of posting. Give each user a ready-made home timeline, like a mailbox. When someone posts, look up their followers and drop a copy into each follower’s mailbox. Reading becomes instant: just open your mailbox. And instead of polling, the phone subscribes to its mailbox, so new posts are pushed to it.

The bill moves to writing. One post turns into as many timeline writes as the author has followers — that multiplication is called fan-out. At 5,800 posts a second and 200 followers each, that’s just over a million timeline writes a second: a lot, but far fewer than 400 million lookups.

Working out a query’s answer in advance and keeping it up to date is materialization; the timeline cache is a materialized view. When posts suddenly spike, deliveries can wait in a queue: posts take a little longer to appear, but timelines still open fast.

Try it: in each mode, press Post and Read timeline a few times and compare the work counters. Then, with Fan out on write, press Spike: ten posts at once. The delivery queue grows, but reads stay fast.

What the book says

Two improvements: instead of polling, the server should actively push new posts to any followers who are currently online; and the query results should be precomputed so a request for the home timeline can be served from a cache.

For each user, store a data structure containing their home timeline. Every time a user posts, look up all their followers and insert the post into the home timeline of each — “like delivering a message to a mailbox.” A user who logs in simply gets the precomputed timeline, and the client subscribes to the stream of posts being added to it. The downside is more work on every post, because home timelines are derived data that must be updated (Figure 2-2). When one initial request results in several downstream requests, fan-out describes the factor by which the number of requests increases.

At 5,800 posts per second with a fan-out factor of 200, that is just over 1 million home timeline writes per second — a lot, but a significant saving compared with 400 million per-sender post lookups per second. If the rate of posts spikes, deliveries can be enqueued, accepting that posts temporarily take a bit longer to show up; timelines remain fast to load, since they are served from a cache.

Precomputing and updating the results of a query is called materialization; the timeline cache is an example of a materialized view. It speeds up reads, but in return we have to do more work on writes.

Case study · materializing timelines: the extreme cases

The superfan and the superstar

Fan-out is cheap for most people. Two kinds of account strain it. Ravi follows 30,000 accounts that post all day, so Ravi’s mailbox is flooded with writes. But nobody reads 30,000 accounts’ worth of posts, so it’s fine to drop some of those writes and show Ravi a sample.

Then there’s Lumi, a pop star with millions of followers. One post from Lumi means millions of mailbox writes — and dropping some isn’t OK, because Lumi’s fans want every post. The trick is not to deliver star posts at all. Keep them in their own small pile, and when a fan opens their timeline, merge the stars’ newest posts in at read time.

Try it: in Superfan, drag how many accounts Ravi follows and press Run a minute, then turn Sample on. In Superstar, drag Lumi’s followers up and press Lumi posts, first with Fan out to all, then with Merge on read.

What the book says

The cost of writes for most users is modest, but a social network also has to consider some extreme cases.

If a user follows a very large number of accounts, and those accounts post a lot, that user will have a high rate of writes to their materialized timeline. But that user is not likely reading all the posts in their timeline, so it’s OK to simply drop some of their timeline writes and show only a sample of the posts from the accounts they follow.

When a celebrity account with a very large number of followers posts, inserting the post into the home timelines of millions of followers is a lot of work, and dropping some of those writes is not OK. One solution is to handle celebrity posts separately: store them separately and merge them with the materialized timeline when it is read. Despite such optimizations, handling celebrities on a social network can require a lot of infrastructure.

Describing performance · response time and throughput

One barista on a busy Saturday

A café has one barista, and a coffee takes about a minute. At ten customers an hour, nobody waits. At fifty an hour, a line forms, and most of your wait is spent standing in it rather than watching your coffee being made.

Two numbers describe this. Throughput is how much gets done per second (or per hour): coffees served, requests handled. Response time is how long one customer waits, from asking to having the answer. As throughput gets close to the most the barista can manage, response time doesn’t creep up — it shoots up. The reason is queueing.

Users feel response time. Throughput decides how many baristas — or servers — you must pay for. A system is scalable if adding resources raises its maximum throughput a lot.

Try it: press Open the doors, then drag Requests per second up slowly. Watch the dot climb the curve: past about 80% of capacity, the line explodes. Press Add a server and the whole curve moves right.

What the book says

Most discussions of software performance consider two main types of metric. Response time is the elapsed time from the moment a user makes a request until they receive the requested answer, measured in seconds (or milliseconds, or microseconds). Throughput is the number of requests per second, or the data volume per second, that the system is processing; for a given allocation of hardware there is a maximum throughput that can be handled, and its unit is “somethings per second”.

In the case study, posts per second and timeline writes per second are throughput metrics, whereas the time it takes to load the home timeline and the time until a post is delivered to followers are response time metrics.

Throughput and response time are often related (Figure 2-3): the service has a low response time when throughput is low, but response time increases with load because of queueing — a request arriving on a highly loaded system must wait while the CPU finishes an earlier request. As throughput approaches the maximum the hardware can handle, queueing delays increase sharply.

Response time is usually what users care about the most, whereas throughput determines the required computing resources (how many servers you need) and hence the cost. A system is said to be scalable if its maximum throughput can be significantly increased by adding computing resources.

Describing performance · when an overloaded system won’t recover

Everybody ask again! (the retry storm)

The café is packed. People at the back give up after five minutes — and immediately rejoin the end of the line, because they still want coffee. Now the line holds the original crowd plus everyone asking again, and the barista even makes drinks for people who already left. The line grows faster than it shrinks.

In a server this is a retry storm: responses slow down, clients time out and resend, and the request rate climbs further. Worse, even after the rush is over, the system can stay stuck in overload until someone reboots it. That is a metastable failure.

Defences come from both sides. Clients wait longer and longer, with a little randomness, between retries (exponential backoff), and stop calling a service that keeps failing (a circuit breaker or token bucket). Servers turn away extra work early (load shedding) or ask clients to slow down (backpressure).

Try it: with Naive retries, press Rush hour and watch useful work collapse — and stay collapsed after the rush has ended. Press Reboot. Then pick each defence in turn and run the same rush.

What the book says

If a system is close to overload, with throughput pushed close to the limit, it can enter a vicious cycle where it becomes less efficient and hence even more overloaded. With a long queue, response times may increase so much that clients time out and resend their requests, so the rate of requests increases further — a retry storm. Even when the load is reduced again, such a system may remain overloaded until it is rebooted or otherwise reset. This is a metastable failure, and it can cause serious outages in production systems.

To avoid retries overloading a service, clients can increase and randomize the time between successive retries (exponential backoff) and temporarily stop sending requests to a service that has returned errors or timed out recently (a circuit breaker or token bucket algorithm).

The server can detect when it is approaching overload and start proactively rejecting requests (load shedding), or send back responses asking clients to slow down (backpressure). The choice of queueing and load-balancing algorithms can also make a difference.

Describing performance · latency and response time

Where did all my time go?

You post a letter and the reply arrives eleven days later. Your friend spent ten minutes writing it. The rest of the time was the post van, the letter sitting in their in-tray, and the reply’s trip back. Only those ten minutes were spent actually working on your letter.

A request is the same. Response time is everything the client sees, end to end. Service time is the part the server spends actively working on it. Queueing delay is time spent waiting in line — for a free CPU, or for the network card to send. Latency is any time a request is not being worked on (it is latent), and network latency is the time spent travelling.

A server can only work on a few things at once, so a couple of slow requests at the front hold up quick ones behind them: head-of-line blocking. The quick request’s service time is tiny, but its response time isn’t. That’s why response times must be measured on the client.

Try it: press Send request and read the bar underneath: network, queue, service, network. Add a hiccup (a garbage-collection pause, a lost packet) and send again. Then switch to Behind a slow one and compare what the server’s log says with what the client waited.

What the book says

The book uses these terms in a specific way (Figure 2-4). The response time is what the client sees; it includes all delays incurred anywhere in the system. The service time is the duration for which the service is actively processing the request. Queueing delays can occur at several points: after a request is received it might wait until a CPU is available, or a response packet might be buffered before it is sent if other tasks on the machine are sending a lot of data via the outbound network interface. Latency is a catchall term for time during which a request is not being actively processed — during which it is latent. Network latency or network delay is the time request and response spend traveling through the network.

Response time can vary significantly from one request to the next, even for the same request. Random delays come from a context switch to a background process, the loss of a network packet and TCP retransmission, a garbage collection pause, a page fault forcing a read from disk, or mechanical vibrations in the server rack.

Queueing delays often account for a large part of the variability. A server can process only a small number of things in parallel (limited, for example, by its number of CPU cores), so a small number of slow requests can hold up the processing of subsequent ones — head-of-line blocking. Even with fast service times, the client sees a slow overall response. The queueing delay is not part of the service time, so it is important to measure response times on the client side.

Describing performance · average, median and percentiles

Don’t ask “how fast?” — ask “how fast for whom?”

Line up 100 requests from fastest to slowest. The one in the middle is the median: half were faster, half slower. That’s the typical wait. The average (the mean) is different: add up all the times and divide by 100. A handful of very slow requests drag it upwards, so it doesn’t tell you what a typical user felt.

To see how bad the slow ones are, look further down the line. p95 is the time that 95 of the 100 beat; p99, 99 of them; p999, 999 in 1,000. These high percentiles are the tail latencies.

The tail matters because the slowest requests often belong to your best customers — the ones with the most data on their accounts. Amazon set targets for internal services at the 99.9th percentile for that reason, but found the 99.99th not worth the cost.

Try it: press New sample a few times, then Sort them. Drag the percentile slider to read off any pN. Then press Add hiccups — three very slow requests — and watch the mean jump while the median barely moves.

What the book says

Because response time varies from one request to the next, think of it as a distribution of values that you can measure (Figure 2-5): most requests are reasonably fast, with occasional outliers that take much longer. Variation in network delay is also known as jitter.

It is common to report the average response time — technically the arithmetic mean, the sum of all response times divided by the number of requests. The mean is useful for estimating throughput limits, but it is not a very good metric for your “typical” response time, because it doesn’t tell you how many users actually experienced that delay.

Usually it’s better to use percentiles. Sort the response times from fastest to slowest: the median is the halfway point, also called the 50th percentile or p50. If the median is 200 ms, half your requests return in less than 200 ms and half take longer. To see how bad outliers are, look at higher percentiles — p95, p99 and p999 are common. If p95 is 1.5 seconds, 95 out of 100 requests take less than 1.5 seconds and 5 out of 100 take 1.5 seconds or more.

High percentiles, also known as tail latencies, directly affect users’ experience. Amazon describes response time requirements for internal services in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though this affects only 1 in 1,000 requests, because the customers with the slowest requests are often those with the most data on their accounts — the most valuable customers. Optimizing the 99.99th percentile was deemed too expensive for Amazon’s purposes. Very high percentiles are easily affected by random events outside your control, and the benefits are diminishing.

Describing performance · the user impact of response times

Do faster pages really sell more?

Everyone agrees that fast is better than slow. Putting a number on how much better turns out to be surprisingly hard, and some famous numbers don’t survive a closer look.

Take a study that compares page speed with how often visitors buy something. Oddly, the very fastest pages sell worst! But the fastest pages are often the empty ones: a “page not found” error loads instantly and sells nothing. Unless you separate what is on the page from how fast it loaded, the result means little. A careful study compares like with like: the same quality of results, only the speed different.

Try it: press Measure 200 visits in a toy shop and look at the dots: fast pages seem to sell worst. Then switch to Error pages apart and watch the pattern turn the right way round. Tap a study card to see what it found and how careful it was.

What the book says

It seems obvious that a fast service is better for users than a slow one, but it is surprisingly difficult to get hold of reliable data quantifying the effect latency has on user behavior, and some often-cited statistics are unreliable.

In 2006 Google reported that a slowdown in search results from 400 ms to 900 ms was associated with a 20% drop in traffic and revenue. Another Google study from 2009 reported that a 400 ms increase in latency resulted in only 0.6% fewer searches per day, and in the same year Bing found that a two-second increase in load time reduced ad revenue by 4.3%. Newer data from these companies appears not to be publicly available.

A more recent Akamai study claims that a 100 ms increase in response time reduced the conversion rate of ecommerce sites by up to 7%; but the same study reveals that very fast page load times are also correlated with lower conversion rates. The pages that load fastest are often those with no useful content (e.g., 404 error pages), and since the study makes no effort to separate the effects of page content from the effects of load time, its results are probably not meaningful.

A Yahoo study compared click-through rates on fast-loading versus slow-loading search results, controlling for quality of search results. It reports 20%–30% more clicks on fast searches when the difference between fast and slow responses is 1.25 seconds or more.

Describing performance · use of response time metrics

The group photo waits for the slowest friend

Ten friends meet for a group photo. Each of them is late only one time in a hundred. But the photo can’t be taken until all ten have arrived, so the whole group is held up far more often than any one friend is late.

Backend services work this way. Loading one page can mean calling many backend services in parallel — and the page has to wait for the slowest. Even if only 1% of backend calls are slow, a page that makes 100 calls will be slow most of the time. That’s tail latency amplification, and it’s why high percentiles matter most for services that are called many times for each user request.

Try it: set Backend calls per page and press Load 100 pages. Each square is one page: coral means at least one of its calls was slow. Push the slider to 100 and compare the coral count with the prediction 1 − 0.99ⁿ.

What the book says

High percentiles are especially important in backend services that are called multiple times as part of serving a single end-user request. Even if you make the calls in parallel, the request still needs to wait for the slowest of the parallel calls to complete. It takes just one slow call to make the entire end-user request slow (Figure 2-6).

Even if only a small percentage of backend calls are slow, the chance of getting a slow call increases if an end-user request requires multiple backend calls, so a higher proportion of end-user requests end up being slow — an effect known as tail latency amplification.

Describing performance · service level objectives and agreements

The promise, and what happens when you break it

A bus company promises: half the buses within 2 minutes of the timetable, 99 in 100 within 10 minutes, and 999 in 1,000 actually turning up. Those targets are its objectives. A contract with the city saying “miss them and we pay back part of the fee” is its agreement.

Services do the same with percentiles. A service level objective (SLO) might say: median response under 200 ms, p99 under 1 second, and at least 99.9% of valid requests answered without an error. A service level agreement (SLA) is the contract that says what happens if the SLO is missed — a refund, for example.

Try it: press Run a day to replay 1,000 requests and check them against the SLO. Then add trouble — Slow disk, Bad deploy — and run the day again. Which target breaks first, and does the customer get money back?

What the book says

Percentiles are often used in service level objectives (SLOs) and service level agreements (SLAs) as ways of defining the expected performance and availability of a service. For example, an SLO may set a target for a service to have a median response time of less than 200 ms and a 99th percentile under 1 second, and a target that at least 99.9% of valid requests result in non-error responses.

An SLA is a contract that specifies what happens if the SLO is not met (e.g., customers may be entitled to a refund). That’s the basic idea, at least; in practice, defining good availability metrics for SLOs and SLAs is not straightforward.

Describing performance · computing percentiles

Add the counts, never average the percentiles

Your dashboard should show p50 and p99 for the last ten minutes, updated every minute. The simple way: keep every response time in that rolling window, sort the list once a minute, and read off the percentiles. When that costs too much, libraries keep a compact histogram instead — a count of requests per time bucket — and estimate the percentiles from it.

There’s one classic mistake. Two servers each report their p99, and someone averages the two numbers to get “the service’s p99.” That’s meaningless: a p99 doesn’t remember how many requests were behind it. The right way is to add the histograms and read the percentile from the total.

Try it: in Rolling window, press Next minute and watch the oldest minute slide out of the 10-minute window. In Combine servers, press Combine and compare the averaged p99 with the true p99 from the added histograms.

What the book says

To add response time percentiles to monitoring dashboards, you need to calculate them efficiently on an ongoing basis. For example, keep a rolling window of response times for requests in the last 10 minutes; every minute, calculate the median and various percentiles over the values in that window and plot them on a graph.

The simplest implementation keeps a list of response times for all requests within the window and sorts it every minute. If that is too inefficient, there are algorithms that calculate a good approximation of percentiles at minimal CPU and memory cost. Open source percentile estimation libraries include HdrHistogram, t-digest, OpenHistogram and DDSketch.

Beware that averaging percentiles (e.g., to reduce the time resolution or to combine data from several machines) is mathematically meaningless. The right way of aggregating response time data is to add the histograms.

Reliability and fault tolerance · faults and failures

A flat tyre is not a crashed car

What does “reliable” mean for software? It does what the user expected. It copes when users make mistakes or use it in odd ways. It’s fast enough for the job at the expected load. And it keeps out people who shouldn’t be there. Put together, reliability means roughly this: it keeps working correctly, even when things go wrong.

To talk about things going wrong, split them in two. A fault is one part not working: one disk dies, one machine crashes, a service you rely on has an outage. A failure is the whole system no longer giving users the service it promised — missing its SLO.

They are the same event seen from different heights. If your system is one disk, the disk breaking is the system failing. If your system keeps copies on three disks, one dead disk is only a fault, and users never notice.

Try it: tap a disk to break it, then press Open a photo. With One disk, one fault is a failure. Switch to Three copies and break disks one at a time: at which point does a fault become a failure?

What the book says

Typical expectations of reliable software: the application performs the function the user expected; it can tolerate the user making mistakes or using the software in unexpected ways; its performance is good enough for the required use case, under the expected load and data volume; and the system prevents any unauthorized access and abuse. If all those together mean “working correctly,” then reliability means, roughly, “continuing to work correctly, even when things go wrong.”

A fault occurs when a particular part of a system stops working correctly — for example, a single hard drive malfunctions, a single machine crashes, or an external service the system depends on has an outage. A failure occurs when the system as a whole stops providing the required service to the user: when it does not meet the SLO.

The distinction is confusing because they are the same thing at different levels. If a system consists of only one hard drive, the drive failing means the system has failed. If the system consists of multiple hard drives, the failure of a single drive is only a fault from the point of view of the bigger system, which might tolerate it by having a copy of the data on another drive.

Reliability and fault tolerance · fault tolerance

Take over without dropping or doubling a post

A system is fault-tolerant if it keeps giving users the service despite certain faults. A part whose fault takes the whole system down is a single point of failure.

Back to the timelines: a machine copying a post into thousands of mailboxes crashes halfway. Another machine has to take over without missing anyone and without delivering the post twice to anyone. Getting both right is called exactly-once semantics (Chapter 12 has the details).

Tolerance always has a limit: “two disks at once,” “one node out of three.” Nothing survives every node crashing together. And the surprising move is to cause faults on purpose — fault injection, or chaos engineering — so the recovery code gets exercised on an ordinary afternoon rather than for the first time during a real emergency.

Try it: press Deliver, then Crash worker A while it’s halfway. Try all three takeover rules and count the missing and doubled posts. In the chaos lab, press Inject a fault a few times: the first one uncovers a bug.

What the book says

A system is fault-tolerant if it continues providing the required service to users in spite of certain faults occurring. If a system cannot tolerate a certain part becoming faulty, that part is a single point of failure (SPOF), because a fault in it escalates to cause the failure of the whole system.

In the case study, a machine involved in updating the materialized timelines might crash during fan-out. To make this fault-tolerant, another machine must take over the task without missing any posts that should have been delivered, and without duplicating any. This is known as exactly-once semantics, examined in Chapter 12.

Fault tolerance is always limited to a certain number of certain types of faults — for example, at most two hard drives failing at the same time, or at most one out of three nodes crashing. If all nodes crash, nothing can be done; tolerating the entire planet Earth being swallowed by a black hole would require web hosting in space.

Counterintuitively, it can make sense to increase the rate of faults by triggering them deliberately — for example, randomly killing individual processes without warning. This is fault injection. Many critical bugs are due to poor error handling; inducing faults ensures the fault-tolerance machinery is continually exercised and tested. Chaos engineering aims to improve confidence in fault-tolerance mechanisms through such experiments.

We generally prefer tolerating faults over preventing them, but prevention is better where no cure exists — security, for example: if an attacker has compromised a system and gained access to sensitive data, that event cannot be undone.

Reliability and fault tolerance · hardware and software faults

Ten thousand disks: one dies every day

One disk is very reliable: it may run for years without trouble. A storage cluster has ten thousand of them. If 2–5% of hard drives fail each year, that’s about one dead disk every day. At that size, broken hardware isn’t an emergency; it’s the weather.

Disks aren’t alone. SSDs die less often but have uncorrectable read errors about once a year per drive. About 1 machine in 1,000 has a CPU core that sometimes calculates the wrong answer. Cosmic rays flip bits in memory. And now and then a whole datacenter goes dark.

What the book says

Approximately 2%–5% of magnetic hard drives fail per year; in a storage cluster with 10,000 disks, we should therefore expect on average one disk failure per day. Recent data suggests disks are getting more reliable, but failure rates remain significant.

Approximately 0.5%–1% of solid state drives (SSDs) fail per year. Small numbers of bit errors are corrected automatically, but uncorrectable errors occur approximately once per year per drive, even in fairly new drives — a higher error rate than magnetic hard drives. Other components (power supplies, RAID controllers, memory modules) also fail, though less frequently than hard drives.

Approximately 1 in 1,000 machines has a CPU core that occasionally computes the wrong result, likely because of manufacturing defects; sometimes that leads to a crash, sometimes to a program simply returning the wrong result. Data in RAM can be corrupted by random events such as cosmic rays or by permanent physical defects: even with error-correcting codes (ECC), more than 1% of machines encounter an uncorrectable error in a given year, which typically crashes the machine. Certain pathological memory access patterns can flip bits with high probability.

An entire datacenter might become unavailable (a power outage or network misconfiguration) or even be permanently destroyed (fire, flood or earthquake); a solar storm could damage power grids and undersea network cables. These events are rare enough to ignore on a small system whose faulty hardware is easily replaced, but in a large-scale system hardware faults happen often enough that they become part of normal system operation.

Reliability and fault tolerance · tolerating hardware faults through redundancy

Spare parts, and spare places

The first fix is spares inside the machine: mirror the disks (RAID), give the server two power supplies, put batteries and a diesel generator in the building. That can keep one machine running for years.

Spares only help if things fail independently. Three copies on three servers in the same rack all die together when that rack loses power. Cloud providers label which machines share a place — availability zones — so you can put copies where no single disaster reaches them all.

Surviving the loss of a whole machine helps on ordinary days too. To install a security patch, restart one machine at a time while the others keep serving: a rolling upgrade. A single server has to go offline for it.

Try it: in the first machine, choose where the three copies live and press a disaster: Disk dies, Rack loses power, Zone goes dark. In the second, press Patch everything with One server, then with Rolling upgrade.

What the book says

The first response to unreliable hardware is usually to add redundancy to individual components: disks set up in a RAID configuration (spreading data across multiple disks in the same machine so a failed disk does not cause data loss), servers with dual power supplies and hot-swappable CPUs, and datacenters with batteries and diesel generators for backup power. Such redundancy can often keep a machine running uninterrupted for years.

Redundancy is most effective when component faults are independent — when one fault does not change the likelihood of another. Experience has shown significant correlations between component failures; unavailability of an entire server rack or datacenter still happens more often than we would like.

Hardware redundancy increases the uptime of a single machine, but a distributed system can tolerate a complete outage of one datacenter. So cloud systems tend to focus less on the reliability of individual machines and more on making services highly available by tolerating faulty nodes at the software level. Cloud providers use availability zones to identify which resources are physically co-located; resources in the same place are more likely to fail at the same time than geographically separated ones.

A single-server system requires planned downtime to reboot (to apply operating system security patches, for example), whereas a multi-node fault-tolerant system can be patched by restarting one node at a time without affecting users. This is a rolling upgrade, discussed further in Chapter 5.

Reliability and fault tolerance · software faults

The same bug, on every machine at once

Hardware tends to break one piece at a time: when one disk dies, its neighbours are probably fine for a while. Software is different. Every node runs the same code, so they all carry the same bugs — and one unusual moment can set the bug off everywhere at once.

Real examples: on 30 June 2012 a leap second made many Java applications hang at the same moment, because of a bug in the Linux kernel. Certain SSD models had firmware that made every drive die after exactly 32,768 hours of running. A runaway process eats all the memory. A service you depend on slows down. One overloaded part slows the next, which brings down another: a cascading failure.

Such bugs can sleep for years. They rest on an assumption about the world that is usually true — until the day it stops being true.

Try it: start with Random hardware and press Trigger: faults land one at a time. Then try Leap second, 32,768 hours (drag the clock) and Cascade, and count how many servers go down together.

What the book says

Hardware failures can be weakly correlated, but they are still mostly independent. Software faults are often very highly correlated, because it is common for many nodes to run the same software and thus have the same bugs. Such faults are harder to anticipate and tend to cause many more system failures than uncorrelated hardware faults.

Examples: a software bug that causes every node to fail at the same time in particular circumstances — on June 30, 2012, a leap second caused many Java applications to hang simultaneously because of a bug in the Linux kernel, and because of a firmware bug all SSDs of certain models suddenly fail after precisely 32,768 hours of operation (less than four years), rendering their data unrecoverable. A runaway process that uses up a shared, limited resource such as CPU time, memory, disk space, network bandwidth or threads. A service the system depends on slowing down, becoming unresponsive or returning corrupted responses. Interactions between systems with emergent behavior that does not occur when each is tested in isolation. Cascading failures, where a problem in one component overloads and slows another, which in turn brings down another.

The bugs behind these faults often lie dormant for a long time until triggered by an unusual set of circumstances, revealing that the software makes an assumption about its environment that is usually true but eventually stops being true.

There is no quick solution, but lots of small things help: carefully thinking about assumptions and interactions; thorough testing; process isolation; allowing processes to crash and restart; avoiding feedback loops such as retry storms; and measuring, monitoring and analyzing system behavior in production.

Don’t ask who. Ask how.

People design, build and run every system. People are creative and adaptable — that’s their strength — which also makes them unpredictable, and sometimes mistakes happen despite everyone’s best efforts. What the book says

Humans design and build software systems, and the operators who keep them running are human too. Unlike machines, humans don’t just follow rules; being creative and adaptive is a strength, but it also leads to unpredictability and sometimes to mistakes that cause failures, despite best intentions. One study of large internet services found that configuration changes by operators were the leading cause of outages, whereas hardware faults (servers or network) played a role in only 10%–25% of cases.

Blaming people for mistakes is counterproductive. What we call “human error” is not really the cause of an incident, but rather a symptom of a problem with the sociotechnical system in which people are trying their best to do their jobs. Complex systems often have emergent behavior, in which unexpected interactions between components lead to failures.

Technical measures minimize the impact of mistakes: thorough testing (handwritten tests and property testing on lots of random inputs), rollback mechanisms for quickly reverting configuration changes, gradual rollouts of new code, detailed and clear monitoring, observability tools for diagnosing production issues, and well-designed interfaces that encourage the right thing and discourage the wrong thing. All of these need time and money, and organizations often prioritize revenue-generating features; when a preventable mistake then occurs, blaming the person makes no sense — the problem is the organization’s priorities.

In blameless postmortems, the people involved in an incident share full details of what happened without fear of punishment, so others can learn to prevent similar problems. Be suspicious of simplistic answers: “Bob should have been more careful when deploying that change” is not productive, but neither is “We must rewrite the backend in Haskell.” Management should learn how the sociotechnical system works from the people who work with it every day, and improve it based on that feedback.

A few hours down, or gone for good?

Reliability isn’t only for power stations and air traffic control. A bug in a business app wastes people’s days and can get figures wrong; an online shop that’s down loses sales and trust.

Scalability · coping with more load

“Is it scalable?” is the wrong question

A system that works well today can still struggle next year, simply because more people use it: 10,000 users become 100,000; a million become ten million. Scalability is a system’s ability to cope with that extra load.

You’ll hear “you’re not Google — stop worrying about scale and use a relational database.” For a new product with few users, that’s often right: the most important thing is staying simple and easy to change while you learn what customers want. Building for scale you may never need is wasted effort at best, and at worst locks you into a design that is hard to change.

And “X is scalable” means nothing on its own. The useful questions are: if we grow in this particular way, what are our options? How do we add resources? And at today’s growth rate, when do we hit the limit of the current design?

What the book says

Even if a system is working reliably today, that doesn’t mean it will work reliably in the future. One common reason for degradation is increased load: perhaps the system has grown from 10,000 concurrent users to 100,000, or from 1 million to 10 million, or is processing much larger volumes of data. Scalability is the term for a system’s ability to cope with increased load.

People sometimes say “You’re not Google or Amazon. Stop worrying about scale and just use a relational database”; whether that applies depends on the application. For a new product with a small number of users, perhaps at a startup, the overriding engineering goal is usually to keep the system as simple and flexible as possible, so you can modify features as you learn about customers’ needs. Worrying about hypothetical future scale is counterproductive: in the best case it is wasted effort and premature optimization; in the worst case it locks you into an inflexible design.

Scalability is not a one-dimensional label — it is meaningless to say “X is scalable” or “Y doesn’t scale.” Instead consider: if the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with the growth? How can we add computing resources to handle the additional load? Based on current growth projections, when will we hit the limits of our current architecture? Once an application is popular, you learn where the bottlenecks lie and along which dimensions you need to scale.

Scalability · understanding load

First, weigh what you’re carrying

Before you can ask “what happens if our load doubles?”, you need to know today’s load. Often that’s a throughput: requests per second, gigabytes of new data a day, checkouts per hour. Sometimes it’s a peak, like the most users online at the same moment.

The shape of the load matters too: how many reads per write, how often the cache already has the answer, how many followers a typical user has. Sometimes the average is what counts; sometimes a few extreme cases are the whole problem.

Then ask the growth question two ways. Keep the machines and grow the load: how much worse does performance get? Or keep performance fixed: how many machines must you add? If twice the machines carry twice the load just as fast, that’s linear scalability. More often, cost grows faster than that.

What the book says

First you need a clear understanding of the current load on the system; only then can you discuss growth questions (“What happens if our load doubles?”). Often this is a measure of throughput: the number of requests per second to a service, gigabytes of new data arriving per day, or shopping cart checkouts per hour. Sometimes you care about the peak of a variable quantity, such as the number of simultaneously online users.

Other statistical characteristics of the load affect the access patterns and hence the scalability requirements: the ratio of reads to writes in a database, the hit rate on a cache, or the number of data items per user (followers, in the case study). Perhaps the average case is what matters, or perhaps your bottleneck is dominated by a small number of extreme cases.

You can investigate growth in two ways: when you increase the load and keep the system resources (CPUs, memory, network bandwidth) unchanged, how is performance affected? And when you increase the load, how much do you need to increase the resources to keep performance unchanged? Usually the goal is to keep performance within the SLA while minimizing the cost of running the system.

If doubling the resources handles twice the load with the same performance, that is linear scalability, considered a good thing. Occasionally twice the load needs less than double the resources, because of economies of scale or a better distribution of peak load. Much more likely, the cost grows faster than linearly — for example, with a lot of data, processing a single write request may involve more work than with a small amount of data, even if the request is the same size.

Scalability · shared-memory, shared-disk and shared-nothing

A bigger bike, or more bikes?

The easiest way to handle more load is a more powerful machine: more CPU cores, more RAM, more disk. That’s vertical scaling, or scaling up. All the threads share the same memory — a shared-memory architecture. The catch: a machine twice as big costs a lot more than twice as much, and bottlenecks mean it can’t quite do twice the work.

A middle road is shared-disk: several machines, each with its own CPUs and RAM, all keeping data on one shared array of disks over a fast network. Fighting over locks on that shared storage limits how far it can grow.

Or go shared-nothing — horizontal scaling, or scaling out: many ordinary machines, each with its own CPU, RAM and disk, coordinating over a normal network. It can grow linearly, use the best-value hardware, shrink when load drops, and spread across datacenters. The price: you have to split the data (sharding) and live with everything that makes distributed systems hard.

Try it: pick an architecture and press Double the load a few times. Watch capacity, cost and what goes wrong. Press Pull a plug to see which ones survive losing a machine. Then try Cloud storage service.

What the book says

The simplest way of increasing hardware resources is to move to a more powerful machine. Individual CPU cores are no longer getting significantly faster, but you can buy a machine (or rent a cloud instance) with more cores, RAM and disk: vertical scaling or scaling up. Threads in the same process access the same RAM, so this is also called a shared-memory architecture. Its cost grows faster than linearly: a high-end machine with twice the hardware resources typically costs significantly more than twice as much, and because of bottlenecks it is unlikely to handle twice the load.

A shared-disk architecture uses several machines with independent CPUs and RAM that store data on an array of disks shared among them over a fast network: network-attached storage (NAS) or a storage area network (SAN). It has traditionally been used for on-premises data warehousing workloads, but contention and the overhead of locking limit its scalability.

The shared-nothing architecture (also called horizontal scaling or scaling out) is a distributed system of nodes, each with its own CPUs, RAM and disks, coordinating at the software level over a conventional network. It has the potential to scale linearly, can use whatever hardware offers the best price/performance ratio (especially in the cloud), can more easily adjust its resources as load increases or decreases, and can achieve greater fault tolerance across multiple datacenters and regions. The downsides: it requires explicit sharding (Chapter 7) and incurs all the complexity of distributed systems (Chapter 9).

Some cloud native database systems use separate services for storage and transaction execution, with multiple compute nodes sharing access to the same storage service. This resembles shared-disk but avoids the scalability problems of older systems: instead of a filesystem (NAS) or block device (SAN) abstraction, the storage service offers a specialized API designed for the specific needs of the database.

Scalability · principles for scalability

There is no magic scaling sauce

No single design scales everything. A system for 100,000 requests a second, each 1 kB, looks nothing like one for 3 requests a minute, each 2 GB — even though both move 100 MB every second. One is about handling a flood of tiny jobs; the other is about moving enormous blobs without running out of memory.

Designs also wear out as you grow. One that suits today’s load rarely copes with ten times as much, so a fast-growing service gets rethought at each order of magnitude. Planning more than one order of magnitude ahead usually isn’t worth it.

Two habits help. Break the system into parts that can work mostly independently — the idea behind microservices, sharding, stream processing and shared-nothing. And don’t make things more complicated than they need to be: a single-machine database that does the job beats a complicated distributed setup, and 5 services are simpler than 50.

Try it: in the first machine, press Run a second and compare the two pipes. In the second, press Grow 10× again and again, and see where each design runs out — then press Rethink.

What the book says

The architecture of systems operating at large scale is usually highly specific to the application. There is no generic, one-size-fits-all scalable architecture (informally known as magic scaling sauce). A system designed to handle 100,000 requests per second, each 1 kB in size, looks very different from one designed for 3 requests per minute, each 2 GB in size — even though both have the same data throughput (100 MB/second).

An architecture appropriate for one level of load is unlikely to cope with 10 times that load. On a fast-growing service you will probably need to rethink the architecture on every order of magnitude of load increase. As the needs of the application are likely to evolve, it is usually not worth planning future scaling needs more than one order of magnitude in advance.

A good general principle is to break a system into smaller components that can operate largely independently from one another: the principle behind microservices, sharding (Chapter 7), stream processing (Chapter 12) and shared-nothing architectures. The challenge lies in knowing where to draw the line between things that should be together and things that should be apart.

Another good principle is not to make things more complicated than necessary. If a single-machine database will do the job, it’s probably preferable to a complicated distributed setup. Autoscaling systems (which automatically add or remove resources in response to demand) are cool, but if your load is fairly predictable, a manually scaled system may have fewer operational surprises. A system with 5 services is simpler than one with 50. Good architectures usually involve a pragmatic mixture of approaches.

Software doesn’t rust, but it ages

Software doesn’t wear out like a bridge does. Yet it needs constant care: requirements change, the libraries and platform underneath change, and bugs turn up. What the book says

Software does not wear out or suffer material fatigue, but the requirements for an application frequently evolve, the environment it runs in changes (its dependencies and the underlying platform), and it may have bugs that need fixing. It is widely recognized that the majority of the cost of software is not in its initial development but in its ongoing maintenance — fixing bugs, keeping systems operational, investigating failures, adapting to new platforms, modifying it for new use cases, repaying technical debt, and adding new features.

Maintenance can be complex, especially for legacy systems: a system that has run successfully for a long time may use outdated technologies that not many engineers understand today (such as mainframes and COBOL code), and institutional knowledge of how and why it was designed a certain way may have been lost as people left. Because computer systems are intertwined with the human organizations they support, maintenance is as much a people problem as a technical one.

Every system we create today will one day become a legacy system if it is valuable enough to survive. The book pays attention to three principles: operability — make it easy for the organization to keep the system running smoothly; simplicity — make it easy for new engineers to understand, using well-understood, consistent patterns and structures and avoiding unnecessary complexity; and evolvability — make it easy for engineers to change the system in the future, adapting it for unanticipated use cases as requirements change.

Maintainability · operability: making life easy for operations

With thousands of machines, nobody can do everything by hand, so automation is essential. But it cuts both ways. So more automation isn’t automatically better; the sweet spot depends on your system and your team. In large-scale systems of many thousands of machines, manual maintenance would be unreasonably expensive, and automation is essential. Maintainability · simplicity: managing complexity

The tangled ball of string

Small projects can have lovely, simple code. Big ones tend to tangle until nobody understands them — a big ball of mud. Then every change is slow, budgets run over, and each fix risks breaking something hidden, because nobody can see all the assumptions.

What counts as “simple” is partly opinion. One idea splits complexity into essential (part of the problem itself) and accidental (caused only by the limits of our tools) — but the line moves as tools improve.

The best tool we have is abstraction: a clean, simple surface that hides a lot of detail and can be reused by many programs. A high-level language hides machine code, registers and system calls. SQL hides on-disk data structures, other clients’ requests running at the same time, and the mess left after a crash.

What the book says

As projects get larger they often become very complex and difficult to understand. This complexity slows down everyone who works on the system, increasing the cost of maintenance; a project mired in complexity is sometimes described as a big ball of mud. Budgets and schedules are often overrun, and there is a greater risk of introducing bugs when making a change, because hidden assumptions, unintended consequences and unexpected interactions are more easily overlooked. Reducing complexity greatly improves maintainability, so simplicity should be a key goal.

Whether something is simple is often subjective; there is no objective standard of simplicity. One system may hide a complex implementation behind a simple interface, another may have a simple implementation that exposes more internal detail — which one is simpler? One attempt splits complexity into essential (inherent in the problem domain of the application) and accidental (arising only because of limitations of our tooling), but the boundaries shift as tooling evolves.

One of the best tools for managing complexity is abstraction: a good abstraction hides a great deal of implementation detail behind a clean, simple-to-understand façade, and can be used for a wide range of applications — more efficient than reimplementing a similar thing many times, and quality improvements in the abstracted component benefit every application that uses it. High-level programming languages hide machine code, CPU registers and system calls; SQL hides complex on-disk and in-memory data structures, concurrent requests from other clients, and inconsistencies after crashes.

Abstractions for application code can be created with methodologies such as design patterns and domain-driven design (DDD). The book is instead about general-purpose abstractions to build applications on, such as database transactions, indexes and event logs.

Agile working patterns provide a framework for adapting to change, and the Agile community has developed technical tools and processes such as test-driven development (TDD) and refactoring. The book searches for ways of increasing agility at the level of a system of several applications or services; to refer to agility at the data-system level it uses a different word: evolvability.

The ease with which you can modify a data system is closely linked to its simplicity and its abstractions: loosely coupled, simple systems are usually easier to modify than tightly coupled, complex ones.

One major factor that makes change difficult in large systems is irreversibility. If you are migrating from one database to another and cannot switch back to the old system in case of problems, the stakes are much higher than if you can easily go back. Irreversible actions need to be taken very carefully; minimizing irreversibility improves flexibility.

The four promises

Nobody writes them on the ticket, but every user expects them.

Performance

Response time is what users feel; throughput is what you pay for. Describe response time as a distribution with percentiles, measure it on the client, and add histograms rather than averaging percentiles.

Reliability

Keep working correctly when things go wrong, so faults don’t become failures. Hardware faults are mostly independent; software faults are correlated; human mistakes are a symptom of the system around people.

Scalability

Having good options when load grows in a particular way. Know today’s load, ask “when do we hit the limit?”, break the system into independent parts — and don’t build for scale you don’t have.

Maintainability

Most of software’s cost comes after it ships. Operability, simplicity through good abstractions, and evolvability — with a way back from every big change.

The seesaws from this chapter

Each side wins in a different situation.

Query on read

Cheap writes, expensive reads, always current

vs

Materialized timeline

Instant reads, lots of work on every write

Fan out to everyone

Simple, and fine for ordinary accounts

vs

Merge celebrities on read

Saves millions of writes, costs a little work per read

Average

Handy for working out throughput limits

vs

Percentiles

Tell you what real users actually waited

Retry right away

Fast recovery from a single dropped request

vs

Back off and shed load

Keeps an overloaded system from locking itself up

Preventing faults

Needed where no cure exists, like a data breach

vs

Tolerating faults

Usually better: expect breakage and keep serving

Scale up

One big machine: simple, but cost grows faster than capacity

vs

Scale out

Many machines: linear growth and fault tolerance, but sharding and distributed trouble

Build for future scale

Ready for growth that may never come

vs

Stay simple

Easy to change while you learn; rethink at each 10×

More automation

Essential at thousands of machines

vs

Manual control

Fewer surprises, and someone can still take the wheel

The book’s summary: these nonfunctional requirements — performance, reliability, scalability and maintainability — have no easy answers, but building applications from well-understood building blocks that provide useful abstractions helps. The rest of the book is about those building blocks.

Words to know

Grown-up words, in plain language.

functional requirement
What the app must do: its screens, buttons, and what each one does.
nonfunctional requirement
How well it must do it: fast, reliable, secure, legal, easy to look after.
home timeline
The newest posts from everyone a user follows.
polling
Asking again and again on a timer, “anything new yet?”
fan-out
How many downstream requests one request turns into — one post, 200 mailbox writes.
materialized view
A query’s answer worked out in advance and kept up to date, so reading is instant.
derived data
Data built from other data, which must be updated when the original changes.
response time
Everything the client waits, from asking to getting the answer.
throughput
How many requests (or bytes) the system handles per second.
queueing
Waiting in line because the server is busy with earlier requests.
scalable
Its maximum throughput goes up a lot when you add resources.
retry storm
Clients time out and ask again all at once, making the overload worse.
metastable failure
An overload that keeps itself going even after the original load has gone.
exponential backoff
Waiting longer (and a bit randomly) after each failed try before retrying.
circuit breaker
Stop calling a service for a while after it keeps failing.
load shedding
The server saying “no” quickly instead of “yes” far too slowly.
backpressure
The server telling clients to slow down.
service time
The time the server spends actually working on your request.
latency
Time a request spends not being worked on: travelling or waiting.
head-of-line blocking
Quick requests stuck behind slow ones at the front of the line.
median (p50)
The middle response time: half the requests are faster.
percentile (p95, p99, p999)
“95 in 100 requests were faster than this.”
tail latency
The response times of the slowest few requests.
jitter
Variation in network delay from one request to the next.
tail latency amplification
When a request waits on many calls, one slow call makes the whole thing slow.
SLO
A target for a service, such as “p99 under 1 second.”
SLA
The contract saying what happens if the SLO is missed.
histogram
Counts of requests per time bucket; add them up to combine machines.
fault
One part of the system not working correctly.
failure
The system as a whole no longer giving users the promised service.
fault-tolerant
Keeps providing the service despite certain faults.
single point of failure
The one part whose fault brings the whole system down.
exactly-once semantics
After a takeover, nothing is missed and nothing is done twice.
fault injection / chaos engineering
Causing faults on purpose to prove the safety nets work.
RAID
Spreading data over several disks in one machine so one dead disk loses nothing.
availability zone
A group of machines in one place that may fail together.
rolling upgrade
Updating one node at a time while the rest keep serving.
cascading failure
One overloaded part slows the next, which brings down another.
sociotechnical system
People, tools and organization together — where “human error” really comes from.
blameless postmortem
Reviewing an incident openly, without punishment, so everyone learns.
linear scalability
Twice the resources handle twice the load at the same speed.
scaling up / shared-memory
One bigger machine whose threads share the same RAM.
shared-disk
Several machines storing data on one shared array of disks.
scaling out / shared-nothing
Many machines, each with its own CPU, RAM and disk, coordinating over a network.
magic scaling sauce
The scalable architecture for everything — which doesn’t exist.
legacy system
An old system still in use, often hard to understand and change.
operability
How easy it is for the organization to keep the system running smoothly.
big ball of mud
Software so tangled that nobody understands it any more.
essential / accidental complexity
Complexity from the problem itself, versus complexity from our tools.
abstraction
A simple surface that hides a lot of detail underneath, like SQL.
evolvability
How easy it is to change a data system when requirements change.
irreversibility
A change with no way back — the thing that makes change risky.
