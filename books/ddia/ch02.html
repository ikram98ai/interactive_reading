Defining Nonfunctional Requirements

An app doesn't just need buttons that work. It needs to be fast, dependable, able to grow, and easy to fix — because an app that is unbearably slow or unreliable might as well not exist.

“The Internet was done so well that most people think of it as a natural resource like the Pacific Ocean, rather than something that was man-made. When was the last time a technology with a scale like that was so error-free?” — Alan Kay, Dr. Dobb's Journal (2012)

Case study · social network timelines

Shout it out, or drop it in everyone's mailbox?

Ana posts a message. Her 200 followers should see it within about five seconds. There are two ways to make that happen.

Way one: every follower's phone asks the server every five seconds, “anything new?” The server then looks up the recent posts of all 200 people they follow and merges them. That's a huge amount of work, over and over, mostly to answer “nope, nothing new.”

Way two: the moment Ana posts, the server drops a copy into the mailbox of each follower — a ready-made timeline. Reading is then instant. The cost moved from reading to writing. That splitting of one action into many is called fan-out.

Try it: post in each mode and watch the two counters. Then switch Ana to celebrity and see why 100 million mailboxes is a problem.

What the book says

Toy assumptions from the book: 500 million posts per day (5,800 per second on average, spiking to 150,000 per second); the average user follows 200 people and has 200 followers; some celebrities have over 100 million followers.

Polling: if 10 million users are online and each re-runs the timeline query every 5 seconds, that's 2 million queries per second; times 200 followed accounts, about 400 million lookups per second.

The fix is materialization: precompute each user's home timeline and keep it in a cache — a materialized view. At a fan-out factor of 200, that's just over 1 million timeline writes per second: far less work. During load spikes, deliveries can be enqueued and arrive a bit later, while timelines still load fast.

Extreme cases: for someone following a huge number of very active accounts, it's OK to drop some timeline writes and show a sample. For celebrities it isn't — their posts are stored separately and merged at read time.

Big idea 1 · describing performance

One checkout lane on a busy Saturday

Two numbers describe how a system performs. Response time is how long you wait, from asking to getting your answer. Throughput is how many requests the whole system finishes per second.

They pull on each other. While shoppers arrive slowly, the one cashier is free when you walk up and you're out in a flash. As arrivals creep up towards what the cashier can handle, a line forms — and the wait doesn't grow gently, it shoots up.

Slide the arrivals from 3 to 9 per second. The cashier can do 10 per second, so even at 9 they're “keeping up” — but look at the wait.

What the book says

Response time: elapsed time from when a user makes a request until they receive the answer, measured in seconds. Throughput: requests per second (or data volume per second) the system is processing.

Response time stays low while throughput is low, then increases sharply as throughput approaches the hardware's capacity. The cause is queueing: an arriving request must wait for the CPU to finish earlier requests.

Response time is what users care about; throughput determines how many servers you need, and hence the cost. A system is scalable if its maximum throughput can be significantly increased by adding computing resources.

Big idea 2 · when an overloaded system won't recover

Everybody ask again! (the retry storm)

A system near its limit can fall into a nasty trap. The line gets long, so answers come back too late, so phones give up waiting and ask again — which makes the line longer still.

The scary part: even after the rush is over, the system can stay broken, feeding on its own retries, until somebody restarts it. Engineers call that a metastable failure.

Send a traffic spike with no protection and watch it never recover. Then try backoff, a circuit breaker, and load shedding.

What the book says

If response times grow so much that clients time out and resend, the rate of requests increases further — a retry storm. Even when the load falls again, the system may stay overloaded until it is rebooted: a metastable failure, a cause of serious production outages.

Client-side cures: increase and randomize the gap between retries (exponential backoff) and stop sending to a service that recently errored or timed out (a circuit breaker or token bucket algorithm).

Server-side cures: detect approaching overload and proactively reject requests (load shedding), or tell clients to slow down (backpressure). The choice of queueing and load-balancing algorithms matters too.

Big idea 3 · latency vs response time

Where did all my time go?

Think of posting a letter. Part of the time is the letter travelling, part is it sitting in a tray waiting for someone free, and only a small part is somebody actually doing the work.

Add them all up and you get the response time — the only part the person waiting can see. That's why it must be measured at the client: the server's own stopwatch never sees the waiting.

Step through it, then switch on “a slow request is already on the CPU”. Nothing about your request changed, yet your wait doubled. That's head-of-line blocking.

What the book says
  • Response time is what the client sees; it includes all delays incurred anywhere in the system.
  • Service time is the duration for which the service is actively processing the request.
  • Queueing delay can occur at several points — waiting for a CPU, or a response packet waiting to be sent.
  • Latency is a catchall for time when a request is not being actively processed — it is latent. Network latency is time spent travelling.

Response time varies from request to request: context switches, lost packets and TCP retransmission, garbage-collection pauses, page faults, even mechanical vibration in the rack. Queueing delays often account for much of that variability, because a server processes only a few things at a time — head-of-line blocking.

Big idea 4 · average, median and percentiles

Don't ask “how fast is it?” — ask “how fast for whom?”

Line up 100 requests, shortest wait first. The one in the middle is the median, also called p50: half of people waited less, half waited more. That's the typical experience.

The average (mean) is different: one monstrous 1.4-second request drags it upwards, so it describes almost nobody. The interesting people are at the far right — the tail. p95 means “95 out of 100 were faster than this”.

Sort the bars, then drag the percentile slider from 50 to 99 and watch the line climb the tail.

What the book says

The mean is useful for estimating throughput limits, but it isn't a good “typical” value because it doesn't tell you how many users experienced that delay. Percentiles are usually better: p50 (median), p95, p99, p999.

High percentiles are tail latencies. Amazon specifies internal response times at the 99.9th percentile, even though it affects 1 request in 1,000, because the slowest requests often belong to the customers with the most data — the most valuable ones. Optimizing the 99.99th percentile was judged too expensive for too little benefit.

Percentiles appear in SLOs (targets, e.g. median under 200 ms and p99 under 1 second, with 99.9% of valid requests non-error) and SLAs (contracts that say what happens if the target is missed, such as a refund).

To compute them cheaply on a rolling window, use libraries such as HdrHistogram, t-digest, OpenHistogram or DDSketch. Averaging percentiles is mathematically meaningless — add the histograms instead.

Big idea 5 · use of response time metrics

The group photo waits for the slowest friend

One tap in an app often fans out into many little calls behind the scenes: profile, timeline, ads, images. They all run at the same time — but you can't see the screen until the last one arrives.

If a page needs many backend calls, a higher share of those pages will wait on at least one slow call. Rare backend slowness becomes common user slowness. That's tail latency amplification.

Drag the number of calls up and run 50 requests. Compare the measured result with the formula on screen.

What the book says

High percentiles matter most in backend services called several times while serving a single end-user request. Even when the calls are made in parallel, the request waits for the slowest of them; it takes just one slow call to make the whole end-user request slow.

The more backend calls an end-user request needs, the higher the chance of hitting a slow one, so a larger proportion of end-user requests end up slow — tail latency amplification.

Big idea 6 · reliability and fault tolerance

A flat tyre is not a crashed car

A fault is one part going wrong: a disk dies, a machine crashes, a service you depend on goes dark. A failure is when the whole system stops giving people what they came for.

A system is fault-tolerant when faults stay faults. If there are three app servers, losing one is a bad day for that machine and a completely normal day for everyone else. But if some part has no backup, a fault there takes everything down — that part is a single point of failure.

Break things on purpose (that's fault injection, the heart of chaos engineering). Turn on the standby database replica and break the database again.

What the book says

Reliability means “continuing to work correctly, even when things go wrong”. A fault is one component ceasing to work correctly; a failure is the system as a whole failing to provide the required service — that is, missing its SLO. They are the same thing at different levels.

A part whose fault escalates into a whole-system failure is a single point of failure (SPOF). Fault tolerance is always limited to a certain number and type of faults; if all nodes crash, nothing can be done.

Counterintuitively it makes sense to increase the rate of faults deliberately — fault injection, and the discipline of chaos engineering — because many critical bugs are due to poor error handling, and this keeps the fault-tolerance machinery exercised.

In the timeline case study, a fan-out machine crashing mid-delivery must be taken over by another without missing or duplicating posts — exactly-once semantics.

Big idea 7 · hardware, software and people

One bad lightbulb, or every bulb at once?

Hardware breaks at random. In a rack of machines, one dies today and a different one next month — and the spares quietly take over. Annoying, not scary.

Software faults are scarier because every machine runs the same code, so they all hit the same bug at the same instant. And the biggest cause of outages isn't machines at all — it's a well-meaning person pushing a configuration change.

Trigger each kind. For the config change, compare “all at once” with a gradual rollout you can roll back.

What the book says

Hardware, by the numbers: 2–5% of magnetic hard drives fail per year (in a 10,000-disk cluster, about one failure a day); 0.5–1% of SSDs fail per year; roughly 1 machine in 1,000 has a CPU core that occasionally computes the wrong result; more than 1% of machines hit an uncorrectable memory error in a year even with ECC. Whole datacenters can be lost to power cuts, fire, flood or earthquake.

Responses: redundancy (RAID, dual power supplies, generators), availability zones, and tolerating the loss of entire machines at the software level. Multi-node systems also allow rolling upgrades instead of planned downtime.

Software faults are strongly correlated because many nodes run the same code: the 2012 leap second that hung Java applications through a Linux kernel bug; a firmware bug making certain SSDs fail after exactly 32,768 hours; runaway processes; cascading failures.

One study of large internet services found configuration changes by operators were the leading cause of outages, with hardware faults in only 10–25% of cases. Blaming “human error” is counterproductive — it's a symptom of the sociotechnical system. Helpful measures: thorough testing, fast rollback, gradual rollouts, clear monitoring and observability, and blameless postmortems.

Big idea 8 · scalability

A bigger bike, or more bikes?

Scalability isn't a badge a system wears — it's a question: “if the load grows this way, what are our options?” Answering it starts with knowing your load today: requests per second, reads versus writes, followers per user.

You can buy a bigger machine (scaling up, a shared-memory architecture). It's simple, but a machine with twice the power costs much more than twice as much, and eventually there is no bigger machine to buy.

Or you can add more machines, each with its own CPU, memory and disk, talking over the network (shared-nothing, scaling out). The cost can grow in a straight line — but now you must split the data up and live with all the trouble of distributed systems.

Drag the load to 16× and try each architecture. Watch which one runs out of road.

What the book says

Vertical scaling / scaling up uses one bigger machine; threads share RAM, so it's a shared-memory architecture. Cost grows faster than linearly and bottlenecks mean it rarely handles twice the load.

Shared-disk architectures give machines their own CPU and RAM but a shared array of disks (NAS or SAN); contention and locking overhead limit scalability.

Shared-nothing / horizontal scaling / scaling out: many nodes, each with its own CPUs, RAM and disks, coordinating in software over the network. It can scale linearly, pick the best price/performance hardware, and survive the loss of a datacenter — at the cost of explicit sharding and distributed-systems complexity.

If doubling resources handles twice the load at the same performance, that's linear scalability. There is no magic scaling sauce: an architecture for one level of load is unlikely to cope with 10× that load, and it's rarely worth planning more than one order of magnitude ahead. Good principles: break the system into parts that operate independently, and don't make things more complicated than necessary.

Big idea 9 · maintainability

The tangled string, and the one that isn't

Most of what software costs happens after it is built: fixing bugs, keeping it running, adapting it, adding features. So the kindest thing you can do for future people (including future you) is keep it understandable.

Watch what happens when every piece talks directly to every other piece: 4 pieces need 6 strings, 10 pieces need 45. That's a big ball of mud. Put a well-chosen abstraction in the middle and 10 pieces need 10 strings.

Three words to aim for: operability (easy to keep running), simplicity (easy to understand), evolvability (easy to change later).

Add services until it looks like spaghetti, then flip to “shared abstraction” and press “Make a change”. Fewer strings means fewer things you can accidentally snap.

What the book says

Most of the cost of software is in ongoing maintenance, not initial development. Every system we build today will one day be a legacy system, so design it with maintenance in mind. Because systems are intertwined with human organizations, maintenance is as much a people problem as a technical one.

Operability: good monitoring and observability, no dependence on individual machines, good documentation and a clear operational model (“if I do X, Y will happen”), good defaults that can be overridden, self-healing where appropriate, predictable behavior. More automation is not always better — automated systems that go wrong are harder to troubleshoot, and the leftover cases are the hardest ones.

Simplicity: complexity is a big ball of mud. Splitting complexity into essential and accidental is a flawed but useful idea, since the boundary shifts as tooling evolves. The best tool we have is abstraction — high-level languages hide machine code, SQL hides on-disk structures and concurrency.

Evolvability: loosely coupled, simple systems are easier to change. A major obstacle is irreversibility — if you can't switch back from a database migration, the stakes are far higher. Minimizing irreversibility improves flexibility.

Kleppmann & Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd ed., pp. 50–54 · Maintainability

The four promises

Nobody writes these on the ticket, but every user expects them.

Performance

How long one person waits (response time) and how much the system gets through (throughput). Describe it with percentiles, not averages.

Reliability

Keeping on working correctly even when things go wrong — so that faults don't turn into failures.

Scalability

Having good options when the load grows: what can we add, and when do we hit the wall?

Maintainability

Operability, simplicity, evolvability — being kind to the people who look after this next year.

Words to know

Grown-up words, in plain language.

functional requirement
What the app must do: the screens, the buttons, what each one does.
nonfunctional requirement
How well it must do it: fast, reliable, secure, easy to look after.
response time
How long you wait between asking and getting your answer.
throughput
How many requests the system finishes each second.
queueing delay
Time your request spends waiting in line before anyone works on it.
service time
The part where the computer is actually doing your work.
latency
Time when a request is just sitting or travelling, not being worked on.
fan-out
One action turning into many: one post copied into 200 mailboxes.
materialized view
An answer worked out in advance and kept ready, so reading is instant.
percentile (p50, p95, p99)
“95 out of 100 requests were faster than this.” p50 is the median.
tail latency
The slowest few requests — the ones that ruin somebody's day.
SLO / SLA
A target for speed and uptime; and the contract that says what happens if you miss it.
fault
One part of the system going wrong.
failure
The whole system stopping doing its job for users.
fault tolerance
Carrying on properly even though something inside is broken.
single point of failure
The one piece with no backup: if it breaks, everything breaks.
chaos engineering
Breaking your own system on purpose to check the safety nets really work.
retry storm
Everyone giving up and asking again at once, making the jam much worse.
load shedding
The server saying “no” quickly instead of saying “yes” far too slowly.
blameless postmortem
Talking through what went wrong without punishing anyone, so everyone learns.
shared-nothing
Many computers, each with its own CPU, memory and disk, working as a team.
evolvability
How easy it is to change the system later when the world changes.
