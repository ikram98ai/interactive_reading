The Trouble with Distributed Systems

One computer is either working or broken. A whole town of computers is always partly broken: messages vanish, clocks tell fibs, and machines take naps without telling anyone.

“They’re funny things, Accidents. You never have them till you’re having them.” — A.A. Milne, The House at Pooh Corner (1928), quoted at the start of the chapter

Big idea 1 · faults and partial failures

One chef, or a whole kitchen crew?

One computer is built to be boring. Do the same thing twice and you get the same answer twice. And when it really breaks, it breaks all the way: a blue screen, a crash, nothing in between.

A kitchen crew is different. The bread chef can be out sick while everyone else keeps cooking. Some orders come out fine, some never come out at all, and you can't predict which. That's a partial failure.

Try it: break something in “one computer” mode, then in “many computers” mode. Run the same eight jobs twice in many-computers mode — you won't get the same answer twice.

What the book says

A single computer with good software is usually deterministic: the same operation always gives the same result. Computers are deliberately designed to crash completely rather than return a wrong answer, so they present an “idealized system model”.

In a distributed system, parts can be broken in unpredictable ways while other parts work fine. This is a partial failure, and partial failures are nondeterministic — you may not even know whether something succeeded.

The book quotes Coda Hale's list of real faults: long-lived network partitions, PDU failures, switch failures, accidental power cycles of whole racks, whole-datacenter power failures, and a driver crashing a pickup truck into a datacenter's HVAC system.

The upside: a system that tolerates partial failures can do a rolling upgrade, rebooting one node at a time while the service stays up. We build a reliable system from unreliable components.

Big idea 2 · unreliable networks

You posted a letter. Now what?

You drop a letter in the postbox and wait for a reply. Nothing comes. Did the letter get lost? Is it still in a sack somewhere? Did your friend move away? Did they reply and their letter got lost?

From where you stand, all six stories look exactly the same: silence. The network gives no guarantee about when a packet arrives, or whether it arrives. All you ever learn is “no answer yet”.

Play the mystery: send a request without choosing what goes wrong. Watch your own timer run out — then press Reveal and see which of the six things actually happened.

What the book says

The internet and most datacenter networks are asynchronous packet networks. Six things can go wrong with a request: it may be lost; it may be queued and delivered later; the remote node may have failed; it may have paused (a long GC pause); the response may be lost; or the response may be delayed.

These are indistinguishable. The usual handling is a timeout — but when a timeout fires you still don't know whether the remote node got your request, and a queued request may still be delivered after you gave up.

TCP retransmits dropped packets, reorders them and checksums them, but it can't tell whether the packet or the acknowledgment was lost, and an ack only means the remote kernel got the bytes. To be sure a request succeeded you need a positive response from the application itself.

Fault detection is unreliable too: an RST/FIN, a crash-notification script (HBase does this), switch management interfaces, or an ICMP Destination Unreachable can give you fast feedback — but you can't count on any of it.

Big idea 3 · congestion and queueing

Four lanes merging into one

Most of the time a network is quick. What makes it suddenly slow is the same thing that makes roads slow: everyone wants the same lane at once.

When three machines all send to the same machine, the switch has to feed them into one link, one packet at a time. The rest wait in a queue. If the queue fills up, the extra packets are simply dropped — even though nothing is broken.

Try it: start with one sender, then add more. Watch the queue fill and the delay bars grow. Hit “Burst” to saturate the link and see packets fall on the floor.

What the book says

Variability of packet delay is “most often due to queueing”: at the switch when several nodes send to one destination (network congestion); at the destination machine when all CPU cores are busy; inside a VM monitor while another VM uses the core; and at the sender, because TCP limits its own send rate.

Queueing delay has an especially wide range when a system is near its maximum capacity. In public clouds a noisy neighbor can saturate shared links and NICs.

Asynchronous networks have unbounded delays. A telephone network instead sets up a circuit with reserved bandwidth (ISDN: 16 bits every 250 µs) and therefore a bounded delay. Ethernet and IP are packet-switched, optimized for bursty traffic, which maximizes utilization at the cost of queueing.

So variable delay “is not a law of nature but simply the result of a cost/benefit trade-off”. Some cases use UDP instead of TCP: when delayed data is worthless (VoIP), the retry happens at the human layer — “Could you repeat that please?”

Big idea 4 · timeouts and unbounded delays

How long do you wait for a late friend?

Wait five minutes and you'll storm off while your friend is still turning the corner. Wait an hour and you'll waste an hour when they're genuinely not coming. There's no perfect number.

A computer has the same problem. A short timeout finds dead nodes fast but keeps declaring healthy-but-slow nodes dead. A long timeout is patient but leaves users staring at a spinner.

Slide the timeout across a day of requests. Green bars answered in time; coral bars were alive but too slow, so you wrongly declared them dead. Turn on “busy day” to see the long tail grow.

What the book says

In a fictitious network with a maximum delay d and a maximum handling time r, a timeout of 2d + r would be reasonable. Real asynchronous networks give neither guarantee, so there's no “correct” timeout value — they must be determined experimentally.

Declaring a node dead prematurely is dangerous: an action (like sending an email) may be performed twice, and moving a slow node's load onto others can trigger a cascading failure, in the extreme case with every node declaring every other node dead.

Better than a constant: keep measuring response times and their jitter and adjust automatically. The Phi Accrual failure detector (used in Akka and Cassandra) does this; TCP retransmission timeouts work similarly.

Measured reality: about 12 network faults per month in one medium datacenter study; round-trip times of several minutes at high percentiles across cloud regions; packet delays over a minute inside one datacenter during a switch upgrade. Cows, beavers, sharks and human misconfiguration have all cut fiber links.

Big idea 5 · unreliable clocks

The wall clock and the stopwatch

Your kitchen has two ways to tell time. The wall clock says what time it is — handy for “when does the cake come out?” But someone can reach up and move its hands.

A stopwatch can't tell you the date at all. It only counts forward, steadily, and nobody ever winds it backwards. For measuring how long something took, the stopwatch is the only honest one.

Time a four-second job while someone messes with the wall clock: an NTP reset, a leap second, or a virtual machine waking up after a nap. The stopwatch keeps its cool; the wall clock can report a negative duration.

What the book says

A time-of-day clock (CLOCK_REALTIME, System.currentTimeMillis) returns wall-clock time since the epoch. It is synchronized with NTP, and it can jump backwards when forcibly reset, or on leap seconds and daylight saving — so it is unsuitable for measuring elapsed time.

A monotonic clock (CLOCK_MONOTONIC, System.nanoTime) always moves forward and is good for durations and timeouts. Its absolute value is meaningless, so comparing it between two computers makes no sense. NTP may slew its rate by up to 0.05%, but can't make it jump.

Clocks drift: Google assumes up to 200 ppm, about 6 ms of drift if resynchronized every 30 seconds, or 17 seconds if resynchronized once a day. Over the internet, a minimum error of about 35 ms is achievable, with spikes of around a second on congested networks.

Leap seconds make a minute 59 or 61 seconds long and have crashed many large systems; the fix is often to smear the adjustment across a day. Leap seconds will no longer be used from 2035. Inside VMs, the clock can suddenly jump forward when the VM is resumed.

Big idea 6 · timestamps for ordering events

“Whoever has the newest sticker wins”

Three friends keep copies of the same number. Whenever someone changes it, they stick a time sticker on it, and the copy with the newest sticker wins. That rule is called last write wins.

It only works if everybody's watch agrees. Here node 1's clock is three milliseconds fast — less skew than you'd get in real life — and that tiny lie is enough to make a write silently disappear.

Step through it with time-of-day stickers, then switch to logical clocks (plain counters that only say “this came after that”) and watch the same story end correctly.

A clock reading is a fuzzy blob, not a point

Your computer will happily tell you the time down to the nanosecond. That doesn't mean it knows the time that precisely. It's more honest to say “somewhere between 10.3 and 10.5 seconds past the minute”.

Google's TrueTime hands back both ends of that blob. If two blobs don't overlap, you know for certain which happened first. If they do overlap, you simply don't know — so Spanner waits until they can't overlap before it commits.

Widen the uncertainty until the two blobs touch, then press “commit wait” and watch the second transaction politely wait its turn.

What the book says

Because node 1's write is tagged 42.004 and node 3's causally-later increment is tagged 42.003, a LWW node drops the increment. LWW also can't distinguish quick-succession writes from truly concurrent ones; version vectors are needed to preserve causality, and equal timestamps need a random tiebreaker.

Cassandra and ScyllaDB use the client clock plus LWW. A node with a lagging clock then can't overwrite values from a faster node until the skew has elapsed, and “arbitrary amounts of data” can be silently dropped.

NTP can't be made accurate enough to fix this, because its accuracy is limited by network round-trip time. Logical clocks — incrementing counters — are the safer alternative for ordering; time-of-day and monotonic clocks are physical clocks.

The TrueTime API in Spanner and Amazon ClockBound return [earliest, latest]. Spanner waits out the confidence interval before committing, and Google puts a GPS receiver or atomic clock in each datacenter to keep it near 7 ms. YugabyteDB can use ClockBound on AWS.

Big idea 7 · process pauses

The nap you don't know you took

Imagine you're the class monitor, holding a badge that expires every ten seconds unless you say “still here”. Then, mid-sentence, you fall asleep. Not tired-sleep — frozen-sleep, where no time passes for you at all.

The class waits, gives up, and hands the badge to someone else. You wake up two minutes later and calmly finish your sentence, still holding a badge that isn't yours. Now there are two monitors: split brain.

Freeze the leader with a short pause and a long one. Watch its own clock stop while the world's clock keeps going, and see the moment the lease runs out.

What the book says

A leader may hold a lease — a lock with a timeout — and renew it while it works. The naive loop is wrong twice: it compares another machine's expiry to its own clock, and it assumes almost no time passes between checking the lease and processing the request.

Causes of long pauses: stop-the-world garbage collection (once minutes long), VM suspend/resume and live migration, laptop lids closing, context switches and steal time from other VMs, slow synchronous disk I/O (even a lazy Java classloader read), page faults and thrashing when swapping, and SIGSTOP from a stray Ctrl-Z.

A node must assume it can be paused at any point, even mid-function, while the rest of the world keeps moving — and may not notice until it next checks the clock.

Escapes exist but are costly: hard real-time systems with an RTOS, documented worst-case execution times and restricted allocation (airbags, rockets). For servers this is “not economical or appropriate”. GC impact can be limited instead: treat a GC pause as a brief planned outage and route traffic away, or restart processes before long-lived objects pile up.

Big idea 8 · fencing off zombies

The ticket number at the bakery counter

You can't stop a sleepy node from waking up and acting as if it's still in charge. That's a zombie. Shouting at it doesn't help, and its old request might still be crawling through the network anyway.

So don't try to stop it — make its message useless. Every time the lock service hands out the lease it also hands out a ticket number that only ever goes up: a fencing token. The storage service remembers the highest number it has seen and refuses anything smaller.

Step through the zombie's return with fencing switched off, then switch it on and run it again. Same story, completely different ending.

What the book says

Two ways one file gets corrupted: a client pauses past its lease and writes anyway (a bug HBase used to have), or a client crashes and its write is delayed in the network for a minute and lands after someone else took the lease.

Shutting the zombie down — “shoot the other node in the head” (STONITH) — doesn't protect against the delayed request, can make nodes shut each other down, and may come too late.

A fencing token is a number that increases each time the lock is granted; the storage service rejects any write with an outdated token. A client that just acquired the lease should write immediately, which fences off any zombie.

Other names: sequencers in Chubby, epoch numbers in Kafka, ballot number in Paxos and term number in Raft. ZooKeeper's zxid or cversion, etcd's revision number, or Hazelcast's FencedLock all work. Object stores offer conditional writes instead (S3 conditional writes, Azure conditional headers, GCS request preconditions). Put the token in the top digits of a timestamp and it even works for leaderless replicas.

Big idea 9 · knowledge, truth and lies

The vote, and the general who lies

A node can't trust its own opinion of itself. If four out of five classmates say you left the game, you left the game — even if you're standing right there shouting “I'm not dead!”. Decisions belong to a quorum: more than half. There can only ever be one group with more than half, which is exactly why it's safe.

All of that assumes the nodes are honest — slow or confused, maybe, but never lying. If a node can send two different answers to two different friends, you have a Byzantine fault, and the puzzle gets much harder.

Four generals, and a traitor or two

Four generals must agree: attack or retreat? Loyal generals say the same thing to everyone. A traitor whispers “attack” to one and “retreat” to another, hoping the loyal ones end up doing different things.

With one traitor out of four, the loyal generals still land on the same plan. Add a second traitor and the votes tie — agreement breaks. That's the rule of thumb: you need more than two-thirds of the nodes to be good.

What the book says

A semi-disconnected node whose outgoing messages are dropped gets declared dead by everyone else — “dragged to the graveyard, kicking and screaming ‘I'm not dead!’”. A paused node is treated the same way. So algorithms rely on a quorum, most commonly an absolute majority: 3 nodes tolerate 1 fault, 5 tolerate 2, and there can never be two conflicting majorities.

A node that “lies” — sending arbitrary faulty or corrupted responses, such as casting contradictory votes — has a Byzantine fault; agreeing anyway is the Byzantine Generals Problem, a generalization of the two generals problem. Most Byzantine-tolerant algorithms need a supermajority of more than two-thirds of nodes to be correct.

It matters for aerospace (radiation can corrupt memory) and for systems with mutually untrusting parties, such as blockchain consensus. In ordinary datacenters we assume nodes are “unreliable but honest”: BFT protocols are expensive and a bug deployed to all nodes defeats them anyway.

Cheap partial defenses are still worth it: application-level checksums (corrupt packets do slip past TCP checksums), input sanitization and range checks, and NTP clients querying several servers so a misconfigured one is spotted as an outlier.

What to carry out of this chapter

This one is all problems. The next chapter is the solutions.

Silence means nothing

No reply could be a lost request, a dead node, a napping node, or a lost reply. A timeout never tells you which — and the work may still happen later.

Delay has no ceiling

Packets queue in switches, in the OS, in the VM monitor and in TCP itself. Minutes-long delays have been measured, so there is no “correct” timeout.

Clocks are two different tools

Use a monotonic clock to measure how long something took. Never use a wall clock to decide which of two events came first.

A pause can happen anywhere

GC, swapping, live migration or a stray Ctrl-Z can freeze a process mid-function. It won't even know it was gone.

Don't trust one node — count votes

A quorum of more than half decides, including the decision that a node is dead. Only one majority can exist at a time.

Fence, don't argue

Ever-increasing tokens make a zombie's write harmless, which beats trying to shoot it down in time.

Words to know

Grown-up words, in plain language.

partial failure
Some parts broken, others fine — and you can't predict which.
nondeterministic
Do the same thing twice, get different answers.
asynchronous packet network
A network that makes no promise about when, or whether, a message arrives.
timeout
Giving up on waiting. It never tells you why there was no answer.
network partition
Also “netsplit”: part of the network is cut off from the rest.
congestion
Too many packets wanting the same link, so they queue up.
queueing
Waiting in line — in the switch, in the OS, in the VM, in TCP.
unbounded delay
No upper limit on how late a message can be.
circuit switching
Reserving a fixed slice of the line, like an old phone call, so delay is bounded.
packet switching
Everyone jostles for the wire; great use of the wire, variable delay.
Phi Accrual failure detector
A detector that learns normal response times and adjusts its own timeout.
time-of-day clock
The wall clock: says the date and time, but can jump.
monotonic clock
The stopwatch: only counts forward, only good for durations.
NTP
The service computers use to ask other computers what time it is.
clock drift
A clock quietly running fast or slow, often with temperature.
clock skew
How far apart two machines' clocks are right now.
leap second
A minute with 59 or 61 seconds. It has crashed real systems.
confidence interval
“The time is somewhere between here and here.” TrueTime and ClockBound report it.
last write wins (LWW)
Keep the value with the newest timestamp. Dangerous when clocks disagree.
logical clock
A counter that only says what came after what — no seconds involved.
process pause
A program frozen mid-step by GC, swapping, a VM move or a signal.
lease
A lock that expires unless you keep renewing it.
quorum
A vote among nodes; usually more than half must agree.
fencing token
An ever-increasing ticket number that makes a stale write harmless.
zombie / split brain
An old leader that hasn't heard it was replaced — and two leaders at once.
Byzantine fault
A node that lies, or sends different answers to different nodes.
limping node
Also gray failure: alive and answering health checks, but far too slow to be useful.
partially synchronous model
“Usually well behaved, occasionally terrible” — the realistic assumption.
safety vs liveness
Nothing bad ever happens, versus something good eventually happens.
fault injection
Deliberately breaking things (Chaos Monkey, Jepsen) to see what your system does.
