Transactions · what exactly is a transaction

The lamp that was paid for twice

Lucía buys a lamp. The shop’s order service makes three separate changes: take one lamp out of stock, charge her card $30, create the order. Halfway through, the service crashes. The lamp is gone from stock and her card is charged, but there is no order. The app retries, and now she has paid twice for one lamp.

A transaction groups several reads and writes into one logical unit. Either all of it succeeds, a commit, or it fails, an abort (or rollback), and then none of it happened. That makes errors simple: the application never has to work out which half of the work got done. It just tries again.

Try it: in No transaction, pick the fault app crash after step 2 and press Place order. Then press Place order again to retry, and watch the counters. Switch to Transaction and do the same: the crash rolls everything back, and the retry is safe.

What the book says

Many things can go wrong: the database software or hardware may fail in the middle of a write, the application may crash halfway through a series of operations, the network may cut the application off from the database, several clients may overwrite each other’s changes, a client may read partially updated data, and race conditions cause surprising bugs. For decades, transactions have been the mechanism of choice for simplifying these issues: a way for an application to group several reads and writes together into a logical unit, executed as one operation. Either the whole transaction succeeds (commit) or it fails (abort, rollback), and if it fails, the application can safely retry.

Transactions are not a law of nature. They were created to simplify the programming model for applications: the database takes care of certain error scenarios and concurrency issues (safety guarantees), so the application can ignore them. Not every application needs them, and weakening or abandoning them can buy performance or availability. On the other hand, the book suggests the technical cause behind the Post Office Horizon scandal was probably a lack of ACID transactions in the accounting system.

Almost all relational databases follow the style introduced in 1975 by IBM System R, the first SQL database; transaction support in MySQL, PostgreSQL, Oracle and SQL Server is uncannily similar to it. In the late 2000s, many NoSQL databases abandoned transactions or redefined the word to mean much weaker guarantees, and a belief spread that transactions can’t scale. That turned out to be wrong: so-called NewSQL databases such as CockroachDB, TiDB, Spanner, FoundationDB and YugabyteDB combine sharding with consensus protocols to provide strong ACID guarantees at scale.

The meaning of ACID · four letters and BASE

Four promises, and a sticker that says less than you think

Picture four guards at a bank. One catches jobs that fall over halfway and undoes them. One checks that the books still balance. One keeps customers from bumping into each other at the counter. One makes sure nothing is forgotten once the receipt is printed. Those are the four letters of ACID: atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability.

The idea is sound, but the details differ from one database to the next, isolation most of all. A sticker that says “ACID compliant” doesn’t tell you which guarantees you actually get. Systems that don’t aim for ACID are sometimes called BASE, which is even vaguer: in practice it just means “not ACID.”

Try it: throw each of the four problems at the ACID database and see which guard catches it. Then switch to BASE store and throw them again: every problem lands on your application’s desk.

What the book says

The safety guarantees of transactions are often described by the acronym ACID: atomicity, consistency, isolation and durability. It was coined in 1983 by Theo Härder and Andreas Reuter, to establish precise terminology for fault-tolerance mechanisms in databases.

In practice, one database’s implementation of ACID does not equal another’s; there is a lot of ambiguity around the meaning of isolation in particular. The high-level idea is sound, but the devil is in the details. When a system claims to be ACID compliant, it’s unclear what guarantees you can actually expect, and ACID has unfortunately become mostly a marketing term.

Systems that do not meet the ACID criteria are sometimes called BASE: basically available, soft state, and eventual consistency. That is even more vague than ACID; the only sensible definition of BASE seems to be “not ACID” (it can mean almost anything you want).

The meaning of ACID · atomicity

An undo button the database presses for you

Wei Ling books room 12 at a hotel. Three writes: mark the room booked, add $120 to her bill, add 120 loyalty points. After two of them the disk fills up. In an ACID database, that fault means the whole transaction is aborted, and the database walks back through what it already did and undoes it. Wei Ling can simply try again later.

Careful: in multithreaded programming, “atomic” means something else, that no other thread can ever see a half-finished operation. That is about concurrency, which ACID calls isolation. ACID atomicity is about faults. A better name would have been abortability.

Try it: in ACID: abortable, set the fault after write 2 and press Run transaction: watch the undo log play backwards. In Threads: indivisible, press Write (2, 2) and then quickly press Peek while it runs; switch to atomic and peek again.

What the book says

In general, atomic refers to something that cannot be broken into smaller parts, and the word means subtly different things in different branches of computing. In multithreaded programming, if one thread executes an atomic operation, no other thread can see the half-finished result: the system is only in the state before or after the operation.

In the context of ACID, atomicity is not about concurrency (that is covered by isolation). It describes what happens if a client wants to make several writes, but a fault occurs after some of them have been processed: a process crashes, a network connection is interrupted, a disk becomes full, or an integrity constraint is violated. If the writes are grouped into an atomic transaction that cannot be committed because of a fault, the transaction is aborted and the database must discard or undo any writes it has made so far.

Without atomicity, after an error partway through, it’s difficult to know which changes have taken effect; retrying risks making some changes twice. With it, an aborted transaction is known to have changed nothing, so it can safely be retried. The ability to abort on error and have all of the transaction’s writes discarded is the defining feature; perhaps abortability would have been a better term.

The meaning of ACID · consistency

The rule only you know about

The word “consistency” is used for at least five different things in this book, and only one of them is the C in ACID. That one is about invariants: statements about your data that must always be true. Kofi’s accounting app has one: across all accounts, debits and credits must balance.

A transaction may break an invariant while it runs (after the debit, before the credit), as long as it holds again at commit. The database can enforce the rules you declare as constraints, such as a CHECK on one row. A rule it was never told about, it can’t stop you from breaking. So the C depends on how the application uses the database. It isn’t a property of the database alone.

Try it: in Five meanings, tap each word. In Invariants, run the good sale and watch the totals go out of balance and back. Then run the buggy sale (the database lets it through) and the negative amount (the declared CHECK stops it).

What the book says

The word consistency is terribly overloaded: replica consistency and eventual consistency in asynchronously replicated systems (Chapter 6); a consistent snapshot, consistent with the happens-before relation; consistent hashing, an approach to sharding; in the CAP theorem, consistency means linearizability; and in ACID, an application-specific notion of the database being in a “good state.” At least five meanings.

ACID consistency means you have certain statements about your data (invariants) that must always be true, for example that credits and debits across all accounts are balanced. If a transaction starts with a valid database and its writes preserve validity, the invariants are always satisfied. An invariant may be temporarily violated during transaction execution, but it should be satisfied again at commit.

To have the database enforce invariants, declare them as constraints in the schema: foreign-key, uniqueness and check constraints (which restrict the values in an individual row); more complex requirements can sometimes use triggers or materialized views. Complex invariants can be difficult or impossible to model this way; then it is the application’s responsibility to define its transactions correctly. If you write bad data that violates undeclared invariants, the database can’t stop you, so the C in ACID often depends on how the application uses the database.

The meaning of ACID · isolation

Two clicks, one like

A post has 42 likes. Amara and Diego both tap like at the same moment. Each app reads the counter (42), adds one in its own memory, and writes back 43. Two likes happened, but the counter says 43, not 44. Neither app did anything wrong on its own. The trouble is the timing: a race condition.

Isolation means concurrently running transactions can’t step on each other’s toes. The textbook version is serializability: each transaction can pretend it is the only one running, and the result is the same as if they had run one after another, even though in reality they overlapped.

Try it: you are the scheduler. Press Amara: next step and Diego: next step in any order. Read, read, write, write gives 43; read, write, read, write gives 44. Then switch to Serializable and try to break it: the database makes the second reader wait.

What the book says

Most databases are accessed by several clients at the same time. That’s no problem if they touch different parts of the database, but if they access the same records, you can run into concurrency problems (race conditions). In Figure 8-1, two clients concurrently increment a counter: each reads the current value, adds 1 and writes the new value back. The counter should have gone from 42 to 44, but it only went to 43.

Isolation in the sense of ACID means that concurrently executing transactions are isolated from each other; they cannot step on each other’s toes. The classic textbooks formalize isolation as serializability: each transaction can pretend it is the only one running on the entire database, and when they have committed, the result is the same as if they had run serially (one after another), even though they may have run concurrently.

Serializability has a performance cost, so in practice many databases use weaker forms of isolation that let concurrent transactions interfere in limited ways. Some popular databases, such as Oracle, don’t even implement it: Oracle has a level called “serializable,” but it actually implements snapshot isolation, a weaker guarantee. So some race conditions can still occur.

The meaning of ACID · durability

“Saved!” is a promise, so don’t say it too early

When you write a file, the operating system usually keeps the bytes in memory and sends them to the disk sometime later. If the power fails in between, they’re gone. A database that says “committed” at that moment has broken its word. So databases call fsync, which forces the data onto the disk, and only then report the commit.

They also write a write-ahead log so a crash halfway through a write can be recovered, and many store a checksum with each log entry, so a torn, half-written entry is spotted and thrown away after a crash. In a replicated database, durability can instead mean the data has been copied to a certain number of nodes before the commit is reported.

Try it: choose ack without fsync, press Commit next transaction, and as soon as the client hears “committed,” press Pull the power. Then Restart & recover. Do it again with fsync before ack, and pull the power during the fsync to see a torn entry caught by its checksum. In Replicated, compare waiting for 0, 1 or 2 followers.

What the book says

Durability is the promise that once a transaction has committed successfully, any data it has written will not be forgotten, even if there is a hardware fault or the database crashes.

In a single-node database, durability typically means the data has been written to nonvolatile storage such as a hard drive or SSD. Regular file writes are usually buffered in memory before being sent to the disk sometime later, so they may be lost in a sudden power failure; many databases therefore use the fsync system call to ensure the data really has been written to disk. Databases usually also have a write-ahead log, which allows them to recover if a crash occurs partway through a write. Many (such as MySQL, MongoDB and PostgreSQL) store their data with a checksum, to detect corrupted or incomplete log entries and restore a consistent snapshot after a crash.

In a replicated database, durability may mean the data has been successfully copied to a certain number of nodes. Either way, the database must wait until these writes or replications are complete before reporting a transaction as committed. Perfect durability does not exist: if all your disks and all your backups are destroyed at the same time, there’s nothing your database can do to save you.

Durability · replication and durability

No single safe, only layers of luck

Durability once meant writing to an archive tape, then to a disk, and lately it often means replication. Which is best? None of them on its own. A disk in a dead machine still holds your data, but nobody can reach it. Replicas in memory all vanish together in a power cut. Drives lie about fsync, wear out, and rot silently, and a corrupted file can be copied faithfully to every replica.

So there are no absolute guarantees, only risk-reduction techniques: write to disk, replicate to remote machines, and keep backups. They can and should be used together.

Try it: switch techniques on and off and watch each fault’s outcome change. Press Strike a fault a few times. Can you find a mix that leaves every fault green or at least recoverable? Notice which faults only backups save.

What the book says

Historically durability meant writing to an archive tape, then to a disk or SSD, and more recently it has been adapted to mean replication. Nothing is perfect: if you write to disk and the machine dies, the data isn’t lost but is inaccessible until you fix the machine or move the disk; replicated systems can remain available. A correlated fault (a power outage, or a bug that crashes every node on a particular input) can knock out all replicas at once, losing data that is only in memory, so writing to disk is still relevant. In an asynchronously replicated system, recent writes may be lost when the leader becomes unavailable.

When the power is suddenly cut, SSDs in particular have been shown to sometimes violate their guarantees; even fsync isn’t guaranteed to work correctly, and it is hard to use: even PostgreSQL used it incorrectly for over 20 years. Disk firmware can have bugs, for example causing drives to fail after exactly 32,768 hours of operation. Interactions between the storage engine and the filesystem can corrupt files after a crash, and filesystem errors on one replica can sometimes spread to other replicas.

Data on disk can gradually become corrupted without being detected; if that went on for some time, replicas and recent backups may be corrupted too, and you need a historical backup. One study found that 30% to 80% of SSDs develop at least one bad block during their first four years. A worn-out SSD disconnected from power can start losing data within weeks to months, depending on the temperature.

In practice, no one technique can provide absolute guarantees. Writing to disk, replicating to remote machines and backups are risk-reduction techniques, and they can and should be used together. Take any theoretical guarantees with a healthy grain of salt.

The inbox that says “0 unread” over an unread email

Counting unread emails with SELECT COUNT(*) every time is slow, so the mail app keeps the number in a separate unread counter. Now every new email needs two writes: insert the email, then add one to the counter. If Yusuf opens his inbox between the two, he sees a new unread email under a badge that says 0. If the counter update fails, the two are out of sync for good.

These are the two letters that matter when one transaction makes several writes. Isolation: another transaction sees all of them or none of them. Atomicity: if anything fails halfway, the earlier writes are undone. A relational database knows which writes belong together by the client’s TCP connection: everything between BEGIN TRANSACTION and COMMIT on that connection. A key-value store’s multi-put may update several keys in one call and still fail halfway.

Try it: in No transaction, press Deliver an email and watch what Yusuf sees halfway. Then press Deliver, counter fails. Repeat both in Transaction, and finally try the KV multi-put.

What the book says

Atomicity: if an error occurs halfway through a sequence of writes, the transaction should be aborted and the writes made up to that point discarded, an all-or-nothing guarantee. Isolation: concurrently running transactions shouldn’t interfere; if one transaction makes several writes, another should see either all or none of them, not a subset. These definitions assume you want to modify several objects (rows, documents, records) at once: multi-object transactions, needed when several pieces of data must be kept in sync.

Figure 8-3: if the counter update fails, atomicity rolls back the email insertion. (Think of an account balance and a payment instead of a counter and an email.)

Relational databases usually group a transaction’s operations by the client’s TCP connection: everything between BEGIN TRANSACTION and COMMIT; if the connection is interrupted, the transaction must be aborted. Many nonrelational databases have no such grouping, and even a multi-object API such as a key-value multi-put doesn’t necessarily have transaction semantics: it may succeed for some keys and fail for others, leaving a partial update.

Single-object and multi-object operations · single-object writes

Half a JSON document is not a document

Atomicity and isolation matter even when you change just one object. Say Hana saves a 20 kB JSON document. What if the network drops after 10 kB: does the database keep an unparseable fragment? What if the power fails while the old value is being overwritten: do you get old and new spliced together? What if someone reads the document mid-write?

Storage engines almost universally protect a single object (such as a key-value pair) on one node: a log for crash recovery gives atomicity, and a lock on each object gives isolation. Some go further with single-object operations such as an atomic increment or a conditional write (compare-and-set). Useful, but not transactions: they cover one object only.

Try it: in each of the first three modes, press Run with no protection, then switch to log + lock and run again. In Atomic ops, press Two increments and Two conditional writes.

What the book says

Atomicity and isolation also apply when a single object is changed. Writing a 20 kB JSON document: if the network connection is interrupted after the first 10 kB, does the database store that unparseable fragment? If the power fails while the database is overwriting the previous value on disk, do you end up with old and new values spliced together? If another client reads the document while the write is in progress, will it see a partially updated value?

Storage engines almost universally aim to provide atomicity and isolation on the level of a single object (such as a key-value pair) on one node. Atomicity can be implemented using a log for crash recovery, and isolation using a lock on each object (allowing only one thread to access an object at any one time).

Some databases provide more complex atomic operations, such as an increment, which removes the need for a read-modify-write cycle, and a conditional write, which only happens if the value has not been concurrently changed (like compare-and-set in shared-memory concurrency). Strictly, “atomic increment” uses atomic in the multithreading sense; in ACID terms it should be called an isolated or serializable increment. These operations are useful to prevent lost updates, but they are not transactions in the usual sense: Aerospike’s strong consistency mode and the lightweight transactions of Cassandra and ScyllaDB offer linearizable reads and conditional writes on a single object, but no guarantees across multiple objects.

Single-object and multi-object operations · the need for multi-object transactions

One change, three places to make it

Could you build everything with single-object writes and no transactions? Sometimes. But in many apps, one logical change touches several objects that must agree. An order row points at a customer row through a foreign key, and a graph vertex points at others through edges. A document database without joins nudges you to copy a name into every post (denormalization), so a rename means updating many documents. And almost every database has secondary indexes, which are separate objects that must change along with the row.

Without a transaction, a reader can arrive in the gap between those writes, and a crash in the gap leaves the gap there forever. You can still build such apps, but error handling gets much harder, and missing isolation brings concurrency problems.

Try it: in each of the three modes, press Write, reader peeks halfway with no transaction, then Write, crash halfway. Switch to transaction and repeat: the reader never sees a half-made change, and a crash leaves nothing behind.

What the book says

Do we need multi-object transactions at all? In some use cases single-object inserts, updates and deletes are sufficient, but in many others, writes to several objects need to be coordinated:

  • In a relational model, a row often has a foreign-key reference to a row in another table; in a graph-like model, a vertex has edges to other vertices. Multi-object transactions keep these references valid when inserting several records that refer to one another.
  • In a document model, fields updated together are often in the same document, so no multi-object transaction is needed. But document databases lacking joins encourage denormalization, and when denormalized information is updated you need to update several documents in one go; transactions prevent it from going out of sync.
  • With secondary indexes (almost everything except pure key-value stores), the indexes must also be updated whenever a value changes. They are different objects from a transaction’s point of view: without isolation, a record can appear in one index but not another, because the second index hasn’t been updated yet.

Such applications can still be implemented without transactions, but error handling becomes much more complicated without atomicity, and the lack of isolation can cause concurrency problems.

Single-object and multi-object operations · handling errors and aborts

The order form that forgot everything

Lina fills in a long order form and presses Buy. Deep in the database, her transaction collides with another one and is aborted. Nothing was half-written: the abort undid it all. That is exactly what makes it safe to try again. Yet her app shows “Something went wrong” and throws away everything she typed.

The fix seems obvious: retry. But a retry is only as smart as the code around it. If the commit actually worked and only the reply got lost, a retry places the order twice. If the database is overloaded, instant retries make the overload worse. If the error is permanent, retrying is pointless. And anything the transaction did outside the database, like sending an email, is not undone by the abort.

Try it: tap a fault on the left, then press Place the order. Run each fault under No retry, Retry blindly and Retry wisely, and watch the counters: lost input, duplicate orders, extra emails, wasted retries.

What the book says

A key feature of a transaction is that it can be aborted and safely retried if an error occurs. ACID databases would rather abandon a transaction entirely than allow it to remain half-finished when atomicity, isolation or durability is in danger. Datastores with leaderless replication instead work on a best-effort basis: the database does as much as it can, and if it runs into an error it won’t undo what it has already done, so recovering is the application’s responsibility.

Popular object-relational mapping frameworks such as Rails ActiveRecord and Django don’t retry aborted transactions: the error usually bubbles up the stack as an exception, any user input is thrown away, and the user gets an error message. That is a shame, because the whole point of rolling back is to enable safe retries.

Retrying isn’t perfect, though:

  • If the transaction succeeded but the acknowledgment was lost in the network, a retry performs it twice, unless you have application-level deduplication.
  • If the error is due to overload or high contention, retrying makes it worse. Limit the number of retries, use exponential backoff, and handle overload errors differently.
  • It is only worth retrying after transient errors (deadlock, isolation violation, temporary network interruption, failover), not after a permanent one such as a constraint violation.
  • Side effects outside the database, like sending an email, may happen even if the transaction aborts. Two-phase commit can help several systems commit or abort together.
  • If the client process crashes while retrying, any data it was trying to write is lost.

Weak isolation levels · why race conditions are so hard

The bug that never shows up in testing

Kwame’s wallet app pays out cash in two steps: read the balance, and if there is enough, subtract the amount and pay. His tests run it thousands of times and it never misbehaves, because two withdrawals from one account almost never land within the same 30 milliseconds.

Then someone fires twelve withdrawal requests at the same instant. Every one of them reads “$100, plenty,” before any of them has written anything. Twelve payouts from a $100 account. A race condition only fires when the timing is unlucky, which is why testing rarely finds it, and why an attacker can make the timing unlucky on purpose.

Try it: in Test suite, press Run 20 times a few times and count the red runs. Switch to Attacker burst, move the slider and press Fire the burst.

What the book says

If two transactions don’t access the same data, or both are read-only, they can safely run in parallel. Race conditions only come into play when one transaction reads data that another is concurrently modifying, or when two transactions modify the same data. Concurrency bugs are hard to find by testing, because they are triggered only when you get unlucky with the timing, and they are hard to reason about in a large application.

Databases have long tried to hide concurrency issues by providing transaction isolation; serializable isolation guarantees the same effect as running transactions one at a time. But it has a performance cost, so systems commonly use weaker levels that protect against some issues but not all.

These bugs have caused substantial loss of money, including bankrupting a Bitcoin exchange, led to investigation by financial auditors, and corrupted customer data. The popular comment “Use an ACID database if you’re handling financial data!” misses the point: many relational databases considered ACID use weak isolation too. (Incidentally, much of the banking system relies on text files exchanged via secure FTP, where an audit trail and human-level fraud prevention matter more than ACID.)

Even if races are rare in normal operation, an attacker might deliberately send a burst of highly concurrent requests to your API to exploit them. To be reliable and secure, such bugs must be systematically prevented.

Read committed · no dirty reads

Don’t copy homework that isn’t finished

Mei is halfway through her maths homework and has written “x = 3” in pencil. Jonas glances over, copies the 3, and builds his next answer on it. Then Mei rubs it out: she made a mistake. Jonas’s answer is now built on a number that never really existed, and so is anything Ana copied from Jonas.

Reading another transaction’s write before it commits is a dirty read. Under read committed, a transaction’s writes only become visible to others when it commits, all at once. Until then, everyone else keeps seeing the old committed value. That also means a rollback can’t drag anyone else down with it: no cascading aborts.

Try it: under Read committed, press User 1 commits, then User 1 aborts. Switch to Dirty reads allowed and press User 1 aborts: watch the abort spread down the lanes.

What the book says

The most basic level of transaction isolation is read committed. It makes two guarantees: when reading, you see only data that has been committed (no dirty reads); when writing, you overwrite only data that has been committed (no dirty writes).

If a transaction has written data but not yet committed or aborted, and another transaction can see it, that’s a dirty read. Under read committed, a transaction’s writes become visible to others only when it commits, and then all at once. In Figure 8-4, user 1 has set x = 3, but user 2’s get x still returns the old value, 2, while user 1 has not yet committed.

Preventing dirty reads is useful because a transaction that updates several rows could otherwise be seen half-done (the unread email without the updated counter, Figure 8-2), which confuses users and can make other transactions decide wrongly. And if a transaction aborts, a dirty reader would have seen data that was never actually committed; any transaction that read it would also need to be aborted, a problem called cascading aborts.

Read committed · no dirty writes

Two buyers, one used car

Aaliyah and Bryce both click Buy on the same used car at the same moment. Buying takes two writes: put the buyer’s name on the listing, and address the invoice to the buyer. If two transactions concurrently update the same row, we normally assume the later write overwrites the earlier one. The librarian who keeps the old page

Ravi is rewriting a page of the library’s catalogue, and it takes him a long time. Stopping other writers is easy: whoever is writing a row holds its row-level lock until the transaction commits or aborts, and a second writer waits its turn.

What the book says

Read committed is a very popular isolation level: the default in Oracle Database, PostgreSQL, SQL Server and many other databases. Most commonly, databases prevent dirty writes with row-level locks: to modify a row, a transaction must first acquire its lock and hold it until the transaction commits or aborts. Only one transaction can hold the lock for a row; another that wants to write it must wait. This locking is done automatically in read-committed mode or stronger.

To prevent dirty reads, a reader could briefly acquire the same lock and release it right after reading. But read locks don’t work well in practice: one long-running write transaction can force many read-only transactions to wait, harming their response time, and a slowdown in one part of an application can have a knock-on effect in a completely different part. Locks are nevertheless used to prevent dirty reads in some databases, such as IBM Db2 and Microsoft SQL Server with the read_committed_snapshot=off setting.

The more common approach (Figure 8-4): for every row written, the database remembers both the old committed value and the new value set by the transaction holding the write lock. Other transactions reading the row are given the old value until the new one is committed.

Some databases support a weaker level, read uncommitted: it prevents dirty writes but not dirty reads, immediately returning the latest written value. That can perform better, since the database needn’t store two versions of the row.

Read committed allows aborts, prevents reading the incomplete results of transactions and prevents concurrent writes from getting intermingled, but there are still plenty of ways to have concurrency bugs. It is considered acceptable under read committed, since the balances she saw were indeed committed when she read them. Such temporary inconsistency is not tolerable for backups, which may take hours while writes continue, so parts of the backup hold older data and parts newer, and after a restore the inconsistencies become permanent; or for analytical queries and integrity checks that scan large parts of the database and return nonsensical results if they observe it at different points in time.

Snapshot isolation is the most common solution: each transaction reads from a consistent snapshot, seeing all data committed at the start of the transaction. It is a boon for long-running, read-only queries. Variants are supported by PostgreSQL, MySQL with InnoDB, Oracle, SQL Server and others; Oracle, TiDB and Aurora DSQL even choose it as their highest isolation level, and cloud data warehouses such as BigQuery frequently use it too.

Never erase, only add a new version

Picture a ledger where nobody ever uses an eraser. To change a balance, you strike through the old line, write who struck it, and add a new line below it signed with your name. Someone who started reading an hour ago can still read the struck-through line, because they know it was crossed out by someone who came after them.

That is multiversion concurrency control (MVCC). Every transaction gets an ever-increasing transaction ID. Every row version records inserted_by and deleted_by. An update is a delete plus an insert. Writers still lock rows against other writers, but readers take no locks at all: readers never block writers, and writers never block readers. Later, garbage collection sweeps away versions nobody can see any more.

What the book says

Implementations of snapshot isolation typically use write locks to prevent dirty writes, so a writer can block another writer of the same row, but reads require no locks. A key principle is that readers never block writers, and writers never block readers, so long-running read queries on a consistent snapshot can run alongside normal writes without lock contention.

The database generalizes the two-version mechanism of Figure 8-4: it may keep several committed versions of a row, because in-progress transactions may need to see the database at different points in time. Hence multiversion concurrency control (MVCC).

In PostgreSQL (Figure 8-7), each transaction gets a unique, always-increasing transaction ID (txid), and data it writes is tagged with it. Each row has an inserted_by field and a deleted_by field, initially empty. Deleting a row only marks it with the deleter’s txid; later, when no transaction can access it any more, garbage collection removes it. An update is translated into a delete and an insert: transaction 13 deducting $100 from account 2 leaves a $500 row marked deleted by 13 and a $400 row inserted by 13.

All versions of a row live in the same heap, whether committed or not, linked newest to oldest or the other way round. PostgreSQL txids are 32-bit integers that overflow after approximately 4 billion transactions; the vacuum process cleans up so that overflow doesn’t affect the data.

Snapshot isolation and repeatable read · visibility rules

Four rules decide what you can see

A busy museum keeps every version of every exhibit label in a drawer. Each visitor gets a ticket stamped with the moment they came in, and a list of the curators who were still mid-edit at that moment. To read a label, a visitor ignores anything written by someone on that list, anyone who arrived later, or anyone whose edit was thrown out. Everything else counts.

That is how an MVCC database presents a consistent snapshot. When a transaction starts, the database notes which transactions are in progress. Then a row version is visible only if the transaction that inserted it counts for the reader, and the transaction that deleted it (if any) doesn’t. Aborted writes simply never count, so they can stay on disk until garbage collection gets to them.

Try it: pick a reader, txn 8, txn 12 or txn 14, and press Check every version. Follow the code as each version is tested, and compare the timeline: the blue line is the moment the reader started.

What the book says

When a transaction reads, transaction IDs decide which row versions it can see. Roughly:

  • At the start of each transaction, the database lists all other transactions in progress (not yet committed or aborted). Their writes are ignored, even if they subsequently commit.
  • Writes by transactions with a later transaction ID (which started after the current one) are ignored, whether or not they have committed.
  • Writes by aborted transactions are ignored, regardless of when the abort happened. So rows written by an aborted transaction needn’t be removed immediately; garbage collection can remove them later.
  • All other writes are visible.

These rules apply to both insertion and deletion. In Figure 8-7, transaction 12 reading account 2 sees a balance of $500, because the deletion of the $500 balance was made by transaction 13 (rule 2), and the insertion of $400 is not visible either.

Put another way, a row is visible if, when the reader’s transaction started, the inserting transaction had already committed; and the row is not marked for deletion, or the deleting transaction had not yet committed when the reader started. By never updating values in place but inserting a new version every time, the database provides a consistent snapshot with only a small overhead.

Snapshot isolation and repeatable read · indexes and snapshot isolation

An index that points at a family of versions

If a row can have three versions at once, what does an index point to? Most commonly, at one of them, the newest or the oldest, and each version points to the next one along. A query follows the index to the chain, then walks it until it finds the version that is visible to its own snapshot. When garbage collection drops a version nobody can see, the index entries for it go too.

CouchDB, Datomic and LMDB do it differently: they never modify a page. A write copies the leaf it changes, then the parent that points at the leaf, all the way up to a new root. Pages that didn’t change are shared. Every root is a complete, frozen snapshot, so there is nothing to filter: a reader simply keeps using the root it started with.

What the book says

How do indexes work in a multiversion database? The most common approach is that each index entry points at one of the versions of a row that matches the entry (either the oldest or the newest version). Each row version may contain a reference to the next-oldest or next-newest version. A query that uses the index must then iterate over the rows to find one that is visible and where the value matches. When GC removes old row versions that are no longer visible to any transaction, the corresponding index entries can also be removed.

Many implementation details affect MVCC performance. PostgreSQL, for example, has optimizations for avoiding index updates if different versions of the same row can fit on the same page. Some other databases store only differences between versions instead of full copies of modified rows, to save space.

CouchDB, Datomic and LMDB use an immutable (copy-on-write) variant of B-trees that does not overwrite pages when they are updated but creates a new copy of each modified page. Parent pages, up to the root, are copied and updated to point to the new versions of their children; pages not affected by a write are shared with the new tree. Every write transaction (or batch) creates a new root, and a particular root is a consistent snapshot of the database at the point in time it was created. There is no need to filter rows by transaction ID, because subsequent writes cannot modify an existing tree. This approach also requires a background process for compaction and GC.

Read committed and snapshot isolation are mostly about what a read-only transaction can see in the presence of concurrent writes. Dirty writes are only one kind of write-write conflict; the best known other one is the lost update problem, illustrated by the two concurrent counter increments of Figure 8-1.

It can occur if an application reads a value from the database, modifies it, and writes back the modified value (a read-modify-write cycle). If two transactions do this concurrently, one of the modifications can be lost, because the second write does not include the first modification. We sometimes say that the later write clobbers the earlier write.

The pattern occurs when incrementing a counter or updating an account balance; when making a local change to a complex value, such as adding an element to a list within a JSON document; and when two users edit a wiki page at the same time, each saving by sending the entire page contents to the server. Because it is so common, a variety of solutions have been developed.

Don’t do the sum yourself, ask the bank to do it

What the book says

Many databases provide atomic update operations, which remove the need to implement read-modify-write cycles in application code. They are usually the best solution if your code can be expressed in terms of those operations. For example, UPDATE counters SET value = value + 1 WHERE key = 'foo'; is concurrency-safe in most relational databases.

Similarly, document databases such as MongoDB provide atomic operations for making local modifications to a part of a JSON document, and Redis provides atomic operations for modifying data structures such as priority queues. Not all writes can easily be expressed this way: updates to a wiki page involve arbitrary text editing (which can be handled with the CRDT and operational transformation algorithms of Chapter 6).

Atomic operations are usually implemented by taking an exclusive lock on the object when it is read, so that no other transaction can read it until the update has been applied. Another option is to force all atomic operations to be executed on a single thread.

Unfortunately, ORM frameworks make it easy to accidentally write code that performs unsafe read-modify-write cycles instead of using the database’s atomic operations. This can be a source of subtle bugs that are difficult to find by testing.

If the database’s built-in atomic operations don’t provide the necessary functionality, the application can explicitly lock objects that are going to be updated. The referee who blows the whistle at the end

Locks and atomic operations stop lost updates by making read-modify-write cycles take turns. What the book says

Atomic operations and locks prevent lost updates by forcing read-modify-write cycles to happen sequentially. An alternative is to allow them to execute in parallel and, if the transaction manager detects a lost update, abort the transaction and force it to retry its read-modify-write cycle.

An advantage of this approach is that databases can perform this check efficiently in conjunction with snapshot isolation. PostgreSQL’s repeatable read, Oracle’s serializable, and SQL Server’s snapshot isolation levels automatically detect when a lost update has occurred and abort the offending transaction. However, MySQL/InnoDB’s repeatable read isolation level does not detect lost updates. Some authors argue that a database must prevent lost updates in order to qualify as providing snapshot isolation, so under this definition MySQL does not provide snapshot isolation.

A big advantage of lost update detection is that it doesn’t require application code to use any special database features. You may forget to use a lock or an atomic operation, but lost update detection happens automatically and is thus less error-prone. However, you also have to retry aborted transactions at the application level.

What the book says

In replicated databases, preventing lost updates takes on another dimension: since they have copies of the data on multiple nodes, and the data can potentially be modified concurrently on different nodes, additional steps need to be taken.

Locks and conditional write operations assume that there is a single up-to-date copy of the data. A common approach in such databases is to allow concurrent writes to create several conflicting versions of a value (also known as siblings), and to use application code or special data structures to resolve and merge these versions after the fact. Merging can prevent lost updates if the updates are commutative (you can apply them in a different order on different replicas and still get the same result), for example incrementing a counter or adding an element to a set. That is the idea behind CRDTs.

However, some operations, such as conditional writes, cannot be made commutative. In addition, the LWW conflict resolution method, which is the default in many replicated databases, is prone to lost updates.

Write skew and phantoms · characterizing write skew

The bug no row-level guard can see

Write skew isn’t a dirty write and it isn’t a lost update: Aaliyah and Bryce changed different rows. Yet it is clearly a race condition, because run one after the other, the second request would have been refused. Think of it as the general case. Two transactions read the same objects, then each updates some of them. If they happen to update the same object, you get a dirty write or a lost update; if they update different objects, you get write skew.

That changes which defenses work. Most of the tools for lost updates guard a single object, and here no single object is in danger.

Try it: in Try a defense, tap each defense on the left and watch whether a doctor stays on call; the list keeps score. In Same row or not, choose which row Bryce updates and press Run: the same race turns from a lost update into write skew.

What the book says

This anomaly is called write skew. It is neither a dirty write nor a lost update, because the two transactions are updating two different objects (Aaliyah’s and Bryce’s on-call records). It is less obvious that a conflict occurred, but it’s definitely a race condition: if the two transactions had run one after another, the second doctor would have been prevented from going off call.

You can think of write skew as a generalization of the lost update problem. Write skew can occur if two transactions read the same objects and then update some of those objects (different transactions may update different objects). In the special case where different transactions update the same object, you get a dirty write or lost update anomaly (depending on the timing).

The options are more restricted. Atomic single-object operations don’t help, as multiple objects are involved. The automatic detection of lost updates doesn’t help either: write skew is not automatically detected in PostgreSQL’s repeatable read, MySQL/InnoDB’s repeatable read, Oracle’s serializable, or SQL Server’s snapshot isolation level; preventing it automatically requires true serializable isolation. A constraint that at least one doctor must be on call would involve multiple objects, and most databases have no built-in support for such constraints, though you may be able to implement them with triggers or materialized views.

If you can’t use a serializable isolation level, the second-best option is probably to explicitly lock the rows that the transaction depends on, with SELECT … FOR UPDATE.

Once you see it, it’s everywhere

The doctors weren’t a one-off. Any time a transaction checks a rule by reading, then writes something that the rule depends on, two copies of it running at once can each pass the check and together break the rule. What the book says

Meeting room booking. Multiplayer game. Claiming a username. Two users may try to create accounts with the same username at the same time. Preventing double-spending. A service might insert a tentative spending item into a user’s account, list all the items in the account, and check that the sum is positive. You can’t put a lock on a row that isn’t there

Every write skew example follows the same three steps. 1. A SELECT checks a requirement by searching for rows that match a condition. 2. Based on the result, the application decides whether to go ahead. 3. It writes, and that write changes the very thing step 1 looked at: run step 1 again afterwards and the answer is different. (The steps can come in another order: write first, check after.)

For the doctors, the rows being checked exist, so SELECT … FOR UPDATE can lock them and the second transaction has to wait. But the meeting room check looks for the absence of rows: no booking overlaps noon. Zero rows come back, and there is nothing to attach a lock to. When the other transaction inserts a matching row, it is a phantom: a write that changes the result of someone else’s search.

What the book says

All the examples follow a similar pattern: a SELECT query checks whether a requirement is satisfied by searching for rows that match a search condition; depending on the result, the application code decides how to continue; and if it goes ahead, it makes a write (INSERT, UPDATE, or DELETE) and commits. The effect of this write changes the precondition of the decision: repeating the SELECT after committing would give a different result. The steps may occur in a different order, for example the write first, then the query, then the decision to abort or commit.

In the doctors example, the row being modified in step 3 was one of the rows returned in step 1, so you could make the transaction safe by locking the rows in step 1 (SELECT FOR UPDATE). The other four examples are different: they check for the absence of rows matching a search condition, and the write adds a row matching the same condition. If the query in step 1 doesn’t return any rows, SELECT FOR UPDATE can’t attach locks to anything.

This effect, where a write in one transaction changes the result of a search query in another transaction, is called a phantom. Snapshot isolation avoids phantoms in read-only queries, but in read/write transactions like these, phantoms can lead to particularly tricky cases of write skew. The SQL generated by ORMs is also prone to write skew.

What the book says

If the problem of phantoms is that there is no object to which we can attach the locks, perhaps we can artificially introduce a lock object into the database. In the meeting room case, you could create a table of time slots and rooms, where each row corresponds to a particular room for a particular time period (say, 15 minutes), created ahead of time for all combinations (e.g., for the next six months).

A transaction that wants to create a booking can lock (SELECT FOR UPDATE) the rows in the table that correspond to the desired room and time period. After acquiring the locks, it can check for overlapping bookings and insert a new booking as before. The additional table isn’t used to store information about the booking; it’s purely a collection of locks that prevents bookings of the same room and time range from being made concurrently.

This approach is called materializing conflicts, because it takes a phantom and turns it into a lock conflict on a concrete set of rows that exist in the database. Unfortunately, it can be hard and error-prone to figure out how to materialize conflicts, and it’s ugly to let a concurrency control mechanism leak into the application data model. For those reasons it should be considered a last resort if no alternative is possible. A serializable isolation level is preferable in most cases.

Serializability · the strongest level

Any order you like, as long as it’s an order

Amara and Diego both edit the same number, x = 10. Amara’s transaction reads it and adds 1. Diego’s reads it and doubles it. If Amara goes first, x ends at 22. If Diego goes first, it ends at 21. Either answer is fine: nobody promised who would be first.

But the database interleaves their steps to keep busy. Some interleavings end at 22 or 21. Others end at 20 or 11, numbers that no one-at-a-time order could ever produce. A serializable database only allows results that some serial order could have produced. It doesn’t care which order, as long as there is one. What the book says

After read committed, snapshot isolation, write skew and phantoms, the book calls it a sad situation. Isolation levels are hard to understand and inconsistently implemented (the meaning of “repeatable read” varies significantly). It is difficult to tell by looking at application code whether it is safe to run at a particular isolation level. And there are no good tools to detect race conditions: static analysis may help, but research techniques have not found their way into practical use, and concurrency problems are usually nondeterministic.

This has been the case since the 1970s, when weak isolation levels were first introduced. All along, the answer from researchers has been simple: use serializable isolation!

Serializable isolation is the strongest isolation level. It guarantees that even though transactions may execute in parallel, the end result is the same as if they had executed one at a time, serially, without any concurrency. If the transactions behave correctly when run individually, they continue to do so when run concurrently: the database prevents all possible race conditions.

Most databases that provide serializability today use one of three techniques: literally executing transactions in a serial order; two-phase locking, which for several decades was the only viable option; and optimistic concurrency control such as serializable snapshot isolation.

What the book says

The simplest way of avoiding concurrency problems is to remove the concurrency entirely: execute only one transaction at a time, in serial order, on a single thread. This sidesteps detecting and preventing conflicts; the resulting isolation is by definition serializable.

Only in the 2000s did database designers decide that a single-threaded loop for executing transactions was feasible. Two developments caused the rethink: RAM became cheap enough to keep the entire active dataset in memory, so transactions execute much faster than if they wait for data from disk; and OLTP transactions are usually short and make only a small number of reads and writes, whereas long-running analytical queries are typically read-only and can run on a consistent snapshot, outside the serial execution loop.

Serial execution is implemented in VoltDB/H-Store, Redis and Datomic, for example. A system designed for single-threaded execution can sometimes perform better than one that supports concurrency, because it avoids the coordination overhead of locking. However, its throughput is limited to that of a single CPU core, and transactions need to be structured differently from their traditional form.

Imagine phoning a pharmacist and dictating a prescription one ingredient per call: “Is there any ibuprofen?” Hang up. Think. Call back: “Put 20 aside.” Hang up. Most applications talk to their database the first way: an interactive transaction, one statement, one network round trip, one decision in the application, the next statement. A database that runs one transaction at a time can’t afford that: its only thread would sit idle while the network carries each answer back. So serial databases require the whole transaction to be sent ahead of time as a stored procedure. With the data in memory, it runs start to finish without waiting for anything.

What the book says

In the early days, the intention was that a transaction could encompass an entire flow of user activity, such as booking an airline ticket: searching for routes, fares and seats, deciding on an itinerary, booking seats on each flight, entering passenger details, making payment. But humans are very slow to make up their minds and respond; if a transaction waits for user input, the database must support a huge number of concurrent, mostly idle transactions. So almost all OLTP applications keep transactions short: on the web, a transaction is committed within the same HTTP request.

Even with the human out of the critical path, transactions are still executed in an interactive client/server style, one statement at a time. A lot of time is spent in network communication between the application and the database. If the database processed only one transaction at a time, throughput would be dreadful, because it would spend most of its time waiting for the application to issue the next query.

For this reason, systems with single-threaded serial transaction processing don’t allow interactive multistatement transactions. The application must limit itself to single-statement transactions or submit the entire transaction code to the database ahead of time, as a stored procedure (Figure 8-9). Provided that all data required is in memory, the stored procedure can execute very quickly, without waiting for any network or disk I/O.

Stored procedures have a bad name. Each vendor had its own old-fashioned language, and code inside a database is awkward to debug, version, test and monitor. Why people dislike them

  • Vendor languages (PL/SQL, T-SQL, PL/pgSQL) look archaic and lack libraries
  • Hard to debug, version, deploy, test and monitor
  • A bad procedure hurts a database shared by many servers
  • Running tenants’ untrusted code inside the database is a security risk

Why they’re worth it

  • General-purpose languages now
  • A home for validation when, say, a GraphQL proxy exposes the database directly
  • With in-memory data, one thread gets good throughput

Serial databases scale the same way. Shard the data so each CPU core owns one shard and runs its own single-threaded loop. Transactions that touch one shard scale linearly with the number of cores. A cross-shard transaction must be coordinated across every shard it touches, and that is vastly slower: VoltDB reports about 1,000 cross-shard writes per second, a ceiling that more machines don’t raise.

What the book says

Executing all transactions serially limits throughput to the speed of a single CPU core on a single machine. Read-only transactions may execute elsewhere using snapshot isolation, but for high write throughput the single-threaded processor becomes a serious bottleneck.

To scale to multiple cores and nodes you can shard your data, which VoltDB supports. If each transaction only reads and writes within a single shard, each shard can have its own transaction processing thread, independently of the others; give each CPU core its own shard, and throughput scales linearly with the number of cores.

A transaction that accesses multiple shards must be coordinated across all the shards it touches, with the stored procedure performed in lockstep across them to ensure serializability. Cross-shard transactions are vastly slower: VoltDB reports about 1,000 cross-shard writes per second, orders of magnitude below its single-shard throughput, and it cannot be increased by adding more machines.

Whether transactions can be single-shard depends on the structure of the data: simple key-value data can often be sharded very easily, but data with multiple secondary indexes is likely to require a lot of cross-shard coordination.

Serializability · two-phase locking

The library where reading a book stops anyone rewriting it

In a snapshot-isolation library, you read your own photocopy. Somebody can rewrite the original while you read, and you never notice. In a two-phase locking library, the rule is stricter. While you are reading a book, nobody may write in it. While somebody is writing in it, nobody may read it. Readers can share, but a writer needs the book to themselves, and you keep every book you touched until you leave.

So under 2PL, writers block readers and readers block writers, the exact opposite of snapshot isolation’s mantra. That waiting is the price. The reward is that 2PL is serializable: it prevents every race in this chapter, including lost updates and write skew.

2PL is not 2PC. Two-phase locking gives serializable isolation on one database. Two-phase commit, later in this chapter, gives atomic commit across several nodes. Despite the names, they are entirely separate ideas.

What the book says

For around 30 years, only one algorithm was widely used for serializability in databases: two-phase locking (2PL), sometimes called strong strict two-phase locking (SS2PL). It is not two-phase commit: 2PL provides serializable isolation, whereas 2PC provides atomic commit in a distributed database.

Locks already prevent dirty writes: a second writer waits until the first transaction commits or aborts. 2PL makes the requirements much stronger. Several transactions may read the same object concurrently as long as nobody is writing to it, but as soon as anyone wants to write it, exclusive access is required. If A has read an object and B wants to write it, B must wait until A commits or aborts. If A has written an object and B wants to read it, B must wait too; reading an old version, as in Figure 8-4, is not acceptable under 2PL.

So in 2PL, writers don’t just block other writers; they also block readers, and vice versa. Snapshot isolation’s mantra, readers never block writers and writers never block readers, captures this key difference. Because 2PL provides serializability, it protects against all the race conditions discussed earlier, including lost updates and write skew.

Two-phase locking · implementation of 2PL

A key rack with two kinds of keys

Every row has a lock with two modes. Shared is a visitor’s badge: any number of readers can wear one at once. Exclusive is the only key to the room: a writer gets it only when nobody else holds anything. A reader who decides to write must upgrade their badge to the key, which works exactly like asking for the key from scratch.

The “two phases” are about when you give things back. While a transaction runs, it only collects locks: the growing phase. At commit or abort it hands them all back at once: the shrinking phase. It never releases one and then takes another. With so many locks held so long, two transactions can easily wait for each other forever, a deadlock. The database spots the cycle and aborts one, and the application retries it.

Try it: click the R (read) and W (write) pills to request locks for T1, T2 and T3, and commit to release them. Watch the lock table and the waits-for graph on the right. Or press Deadlock demo or Upgrade demo to see the classic cases played out.

What the book says

2PL is used by the serializable isolation level in MySQL/InnoDB and SQL Server, and by the repeatable-read isolation level in Db2. Each object has a lock in shared or exclusive mode (a multi-reader single-writer lock):

  • To read an object, a transaction must first acquire the lock in shared mode. Several transactions may hold it in shared mode simultaneously, but if another transaction already has an exclusive lock, they must wait.
  • To write an object, a transaction must first acquire the lock in exclusive mode. No other transaction may hold it at the same time in either mode.
  • A transaction that first reads and then writes may upgrade its shared lock to an exclusive lock; the upgrade works the same way as getting an exclusive lock directly.
  • Once acquired, a lock must be held until the end of the transaction. The first phase (the growing phase) acquires locks; the second (the shrinking phase, at the end) releases them all. Once a lock is released, no new locks may be acquired.

Since so many locks are in use, transaction A can easily be stuck waiting for B to release a lock, and vice versa: a deadlock. The database automatically detects deadlocks and aborts one transaction so the others can make progress; the aborted transaction needs to be retried by the application.

Two-phase locking · performance of 2PL

One slow reader, and the whole checkout line freezes

Every few milliseconds a customer’s order writes a row in the orders table, and each write takes a moment. Then at 11:00 Haruto starts the backup: one read-only transaction that reads the entire table. Under two-phase locking that read needs a shared lock on the whole table. It waits for the writes in progress, and then, for as long as the scan runs, every new order waits behind it.

Nothing crashed, yet for those seconds the database is effectively unavailable for writes. That is why 2PL hasn’t been the default for most systems since the 1970s: less throughput, and above all unstable latency, very slow at high percentiles whenever there is contention. Locks held longer also mean more deadlocks, and every deadlock throws away work that has to be redone.

What the book says

The big downside of 2PL, and the reason it hasn’t been the default for most systems since the 1970s, is performance: transaction throughput and response times are significantly worse than under weak isolation. Partly that is the overhead of acquiring and releasing locks, but more importantly it is reduced concurrency: if two transactions try to do anything that may in any way result in a race condition, one has to wait for the other.

A transaction that reads an entire table (a backup, analytical query or integrity check) must take a shared lock on the entire table. It first waits for all in-progress writers on that table; then, while the table is read, all writers are blocked until it commits. In effect, the database becomes unavailable for writes for an extended time.

So databases running 2PL can have quite unstable latencies and be very slow at high percentiles if there is contention. One slow transaction, or one that accesses a lot of data and acquires many locks, can cause the rest of the system to grind to a halt. Transaction timeouts and slow query monitoring are used to detect and limit misbehaving queries.

Deadlocks can happen with lock-based read committed, but occur much more frequently under 2PL serializable isolation, depending on access patterns. A transaction aborted because of deadlock and retried must do its work all over again, so frequent deadlocks mean significant wasted effort.

Two-phase locking · predicate locks

Locking an empty square on the calendar

Ngozi’s transaction asks, “is room 123 free between noon and 1 p.m.?” and gets no rows back. Plain row locks can’t help her: there is no row to lock. If Tomás inserts a 12:30 booking for room 123 a moment later, her “free” answer is a phantom waiting to happen.

A predicate lock locks the question instead of a row: every object that matches room_id = 123 AND end_time > 12:00 AND start_time < 13:00, including ones that don’t exist yet. Ngozi’s read takes it in shared mode. Every insert, update or delete then checks whether its old or new value matches a predicate someone else holds; if it does, it waits. Another reader asking the same question can share the lock. Bookings for other rooms, or other times, pass straight through.

Try it: click the writes under the calendar one by one. Some wait, some go straight in. Then press T1 books and commits and watch the waiting writes wake up, check again and find the slot taken. Drag Predicate locks held to see why checking every predicate gets slow.

What the book says

A database with serializable isolation must prevent phantoms. In the meeting room example, if one transaction has searched for existing bookings for a room within a time window (Example 8-2), another transaction may not concurrently insert or update another booking for the same room and time range. Bookings for other rooms, or the same room at a different time, are fine.

Conceptually, this needs a predicate lock. It works like the shared/exclusive lock, but rather than belonging to one object, it belongs to all objects that match a search condition. If transaction A wants to read objects matching a condition, it must acquire a shared-mode predicate lock on the conditions of the query; if B has an exclusive lock on any matching object, A waits. If A wants to insert, update or delete any object, it must first check whether either the old or the new value matches any existing predicate lock; if B holds a matching one, A waits until B commits or aborts.

The key idea is that a predicate lock applies even to objects that do not yet exist in the database but might be added in the future (phantoms). If 2PL includes predicate locks, the database prevents all forms of write skew and other race conditions, and its isolation becomes serializable.

But predicate locks do not perform well: if there are many locks by active transactions, checking for matching locks becomes time-consuming.

Roping off the whole aisle instead of one shelf

Checking every write against every predicate is too slow, so real databases cheat, safely. They lock something bigger than the question asked. Instead of “room 123 between noon and 1 p.m.”, lock “room 123, at any time”, or “any room, between noon and 1 p.m.” Anything that matches the precise question also matches the bigger one, so no conflict can slip through.

The bigger region is chosen to be one the database touched anyway: the index entry it used to run the search. If the search used the room_id index, it puts a shared lock on entry 123. If it used a time index, it locks that range of times. A later write for the same room or time has to update the same part of that index, bumps into the lock and waits. This is an index-range lock, also called next-key locking. Its cost is some waits that weren’t strictly necessary. With no usable index, the fallback is a shared lock on the whole table.

Try it: choose which index the search used, then press Test all six writes. Each row compares the precise predicate with the index lock: a true conflict caught (mint), a wait that wasn’t needed (yellow), or free to go. Try No suitable index too. The Missed counter never moves.

What the book says

Because predicate locks do not perform well, most databases with 2PL implement index-range locking (also known as next-key locking), a simplified approximation of predicate locking.

It’s safe to simplify a predicate by making it match a greater set of objects. A predicate lock for bookings of room 123 between noon and 1 p.m. can be approximated by locking bookings for room 123 at any time, or by locking all rooms between noon and 1 p.m. Any write that matches the original predicate will definitely also match the approximations.

With an index on room_id, the database can attach a shared lock to the index entry for room 123. With a time-based index, it can attach a shared lock to a range of values in that index. A transaction that wants to insert, update or delete a booking for the same room and/or an overlapping time period must update the same part of the index, encounters the shared lock, and waits until it is released.

This provides effective protection against phantoms and write skew. Index-range locks are not as precise as predicate locks (they may lock a bigger range than strictly necessary), but with much lower overheads they are a good compromise. If there is no suitable index, the database can fall back to a shared lock on the entire table: bad for performance, since it stops all other writers to the table, but safe.

Serializable snapshot isolation · pessimistic versus optimistic

Wait at the door, or walk in and get checked on the way out

So far the choice looks bleak. Serializability either performs badly (two-phase locking) or doesn’t scale (serial execution), and the weak levels that perform well are full of races. Serializable snapshot isolation (SSI), first described in 2008, breaks the deadlock: full serializability for only a small penalty over plain snapshot isolation. It is the serializable level in PostgreSQL, and CockroachDB and FoundationDB use it too.

The difference is temperament. Kofi runs a workshop pessimistically: if anybody might be using the lathe, you wait until they are done. That’s 2PL, and serial execution is the extreme case, one lock on the whole shop. Mei runs hers optimistically: everybody just starts, and at the door she checks whether two people used the same lathe. If so, one of them throws the work away and starts again. When few people want the same lathe, Mei’s shop gets more done, because nobody stands around waiting. When contention is high and the shop is already busy, the do-overs eat the time that other jobs needed.

What the book says

Serializable snapshot isolation (SSI) provides full serializability with only a small performance penalty compared to snapshot isolation. It was first described in 2008, and SSI and similar algorithms are used in single-node databases (the serializable isolation level in PostgreSQL, SQL Server’s In-Memory OLTP/Hekaton, and HyPer), distributed databases (CockroachDB and FoundationDB), and embedded storage engines such as BadgerDB.

2PL is a pessimistic concurrency control mechanism: if anything might possibly go wrong, as indicated by a lock held by another transaction, it’s better to wait until the situation is safe again. Serial execution is pessimistic to the extreme, essentially an exclusive lock on the entire database (or one shard) for the duration of each transaction, compensated by making each transaction very fast.

SSI is optimistic: instead of blocking, transactions continue anyway in the hope that everything will turn out all right. When a transaction wants to commit, the database checks whether isolation was violated; if so, the transaction is aborted and has to be retried. Only transactions that executed serializably may commit.

Optimistic concurrency control performs badly under high contention, which leads to a high proportion of aborts, and if the system is already close to its maximum throughput, the extra load from retried transactions can make performance worse. With enough spare capacity and not too much contention, it tends to perform better than pessimistic techniques. Commutative atomic operations reduce contention: concurrent increments can all be applied without conflicting, as long as the counter isn’t read in the same transaction. On top of snapshot isolation, SSI adds an algorithm for detecting serialization conflicts among reads and writes and deciding which transactions to abort.

Write skew in snapshot isolation follows a recurring pattern: a transaction reads data from the database, examines the result of the query, and decides to take an action (write to the database) based on the result it saw. Under snapshot isolation, the result of the original query may no longer be up-to-date by the time the transaction commits.

The transaction is taking an action based on a premise, a fact that was true at the beginning of the transaction, such as there being two doctors on call. When it wants to commit, the original data may have changed and the premise may no longer be true.

The database doesn’t know how the application logic uses the result of a query, so to be safe it must assume that any change in the query result means the writes in that transaction may be invalid: there may be a causal dependency between the queries and the writes. To provide serializable isolation, it must detect transactions that may have acted on an outdated premise and abort them.

Two cases: detecting reads of a stale MVCC object version (an uncommitted write occurred before the read), and detecting writes that affect prior reads (the write occurs after the read).

Serializable snapshot isolation · detecting stale MVCC reads

The note that says “I skipped something”

Under snapshot isolation, a reader walks a row’s versions and skips any version written by a transaction that hadn’t committed when the reader started. That is how transaction 43 sees Aaliyah still on call, even though transaction 42 has already written her off call.

SSI adds one small habit: whenever a reader skips a version like that, it writes down whose write it ignored. At commit time, it looks at the list. If one of those writers has committed since, the reader’s snapshot was out of date, and so was its premise: abort. Why not abort at the moment of the read? Because the database doesn’t know yet whether the reader will ever write, whether the writer will commit, or whether the reader will finish first. Waiting avoids aborts that turn out to be unnecessary, which keeps long read-only queries cheap.

What the book says

Snapshot isolation is usually implemented by MVCC. When a transaction reads from a consistent snapshot in an MVCC database, it ignores writes made by any other transactions that hadn’t yet committed when the snapshot was taken.

In Figure 8-10, transaction 43 sees Aaliyah as having on_call = true, because transaction 42 (which modified Aaliyah’s on-call status) is uncommitted. By the time 43 wants to commit, 42 has committed: the write that was ignored has now taken effect, and 43’s premise is no longer true.

To prevent this anomaly, the database tracks when a transaction ignores another transaction’s writes because of MVCC visibility rules. When the transaction wants to commit, the database checks whether any of the ignored writes have now been committed; if so, the transaction must be aborted.

Why wait until committing? If 43 was a read-only transaction, it wouldn’t need to be aborted, because there is no risk of write skew, and at the time of the read the database doesn’t yet know whether 43 will later write. Moreover, 42 may yet abort or may still be uncommitted when 43 commits, so the read may turn out not to have been stale after all. By avoiding unnecessary aborts, SSI preserves snapshot isolation’s support for long-running reads from a consistent snapshot.

The other way a premise goes stale: somebody writes data after you read it. Two-phase locking would make the writer wait. When a transaction writes to the database, it must look in the indexes for any other transactions that have recently read the affected data. Serializable snapshot isolation · performance of SSI

Fine notes or rough notes, short trips or long ones

How well SSI works depends on engineering details. The big one is granularity: how precisely the database records what each transaction read. Note every row, and it aborts only the transactions that truly have to go, but the bookkeeping grows. Note whole pages, or the whole table, and the bookkeeping is cheap, but a write anywhere nearby looks like a conflict, and more transactions are aborted than necessary.

The other knob is how long a transaction runs. A transaction that reads and writes over a long time is likely to see what it read change under it, and be aborted. A long read-only transaction is fine: it never writes, so its stale snapshot can’t cause write skew. And compared with 2PL and serial execution, SSI barely cares about one slow transaction, because nobody ever waits for anybody.

Try it: in Granularity, press Run 10 transactions at row, then page, then table, and watch the false alarms grow while the bookkeeping shrinks. In Long transactions, drag the length up and run it as read-write, then read-only. In One slow transaction, add a slow report and compare the three approaches.

What the book says

One trade-off is the granularity at which transactions’ reads and writes are tracked. Detailed tracking lets the database be precise about which transactions need to abort, but the bookkeeping overhead can become significant. Less detailed tracking is faster but may lead to more transactions being aborted than strictly necessary. In some cases it’s OK for a transaction to read information overwritten by another, and it’s possible to prove the execution is still serializable; PostgreSQL uses this theory to reduce unnecessary aborts.

Compared to 2PL, the big advantage of SSI is that one transaction doesn’t need to block waiting for locks held by another. Writers don’t block readers, and vice versa, which makes query latency much more predictable and less variable; read-only queries can run on a consistent snapshot without any locks, which is very appealing for read-heavy workloads.

Compared to serial execution, SSI is not limited to the throughput of a single CPU core: FoundationDB distributes the detection of serialization conflicts across multiple machines, allowing it to scale to very high throughput, and transactions can read and write data in multiple shards while remaining serializable.

Compared to nonserializable snapshot isolation, checking for serializability violations has some overhead; how significant is debated. The rate of aborts significantly affects SSI’s performance: a transaction that reads and writes data over a long period is likely to run into conflicts and abort, so read/write transactions should be fairly short (long-running read-only transactions are OK). SSI is less sensitive to slow transactions than 2PL or serial execution.

One disk decides, until there are three

On a single node, atomicity comes down to order. The database first writes the transaction’s data to its log, then appends one small commit record. If the power fails before that record reaches the disk, the transaction is rolled back on restart; if it fails after, the transaction stands. The deciding moment is the instant one disk drive finishes writing one record.

Now Lucía’s transaction touches three shards. The obvious idea, sending “commit” to each node and letting each commit by itself, breaks easily. One node finds a constraint violation, a commit request gets lost in the network, or a node crashes before its commit record is written. The result is a transaction that is committed on some nodes and aborted on others. And a commit can’t be taken back: the moment it happens, other transactions can read the data. Making every node commit or every node abort is the atomic commitment problem.

What the book says

In a single-node transaction, one machine is in charge of executing the transaction logic. With single-leader replication, transaction execution happens only on the leader; followers apply the log of committed writes. When multiple nodes are involved, for example a transaction touching several shards, or a global secondary index whose entry lives on a different node from the primary data, it is a distributed transaction. Concurrency control is broadly similar to the single-node case; achieving atomicity is a whole new challenge.

On a single node, when the client asks to commit, the database makes the transaction’s writes durable (typically in a write-ahead log) and then appends a commit record to the log on disk. If the database crashes in the middle, the transaction is recovered from the log on restart: committed if the commit record made it to disk, rolled back if not. The deciding moment is when the disk finishes writing the commit record, so a single device makes the commit atomic.

It is not sufficient to send a commit request to all the nodes and commit independently on each (Figure 8-12): some nodes may detect a constraint violation or conflict, some commit requests may be lost in the network, and some nodes may crash before the commit record is fully written.

Once a transaction has been committed on one node, it cannot be retracted if it later turns out to have aborted elsewhere, because committed data becomes visible to other transactions under read-committed or stronger isolation; their work would have to be reverted too. Ensuring all nodes either commit or abort is known as the atomic commitment problem.

Two-phase commit is an algorithm for achieving atomic transaction commit across multiple nodes. A distributed transaction begins with the application reading and writing data on multiple database nodes, called participants. When the application is ready to commit, the coordinator begins phase 1 by sending a prepare request to each node, asking whether it is able to commit. If all participants reply yes, the coordinator sends a commit request in phase 2; if any participant replies no, it sends an abort request to all nodes in phase 2.

The book compares this to a traditional Western marriage ceremony: the officiant asks each partner individually, receives both acknowledgments, pronounces the couple married and broadcasts the fact; if either partner doesn’t say yes, the ceremony is aborted. Two-phase commit · a system of promises

Two promises that can’t be taken back

If messages can get lost in one phase, they can get lost in two. So why does two-phase commit work when one-phase doesn’t? Because of two promises. The first is the participant’s. When a database votes yes to prepare, it has already written everything to disk and checked every constraint, so a crash or a full disk is no longer an excuse. Voting yes means surrendering the right to abort, without committing yet.

The second is the coordinator’s. Once every vote is in, it writes its decision to its own log on disk. That is the commit point. From then on the decision is final: if a commit request is lost, or a participant has crashed, the coordinator retries forever. A participant that comes back after a crash must commit, because it promised. Before saying “I do”, you can still say no. After it, fainting doesn’t undo the wedding.

Try it: press Play once to see the six steps. Play again and tap db 2 while it is still active to crash it: the coordinator is allowed to abort. Then crash db 2 after it has voted yes, or press Lose next message during phase 2: the coordinator keeps retrying until db 2 commits.

What the book says

(1) The application requests a globally unique transaction ID from the coordinator. (2) It begins a single-node transaction on each participant and attaches the global ID; all reads and writes happen in these. If anything goes wrong at this stage, the coordinator or any participant can abort. (3) When the application is ready to commit, the coordinator sends a prepare request tagged with the global ID to all participants; if any request fails or times out, it sends abort to all.

(4) A participant receiving prepare makes sure it can definitely commit under all circumstances: it writes all transaction data to disk and checks for conflicts or constraint violations. By replying yes, it promises to commit without error if requested: it surrenders the right to abort, but without actually committing.

(5) Having received all responses, the coordinator makes a definitive decision (commit only if all voted yes) and must write it to its transaction log on disk, so it knows its decision if it crashes. This is the commit point. (6) The commit or abort request is then sent to all participants; if it fails or times out, the coordinator must retry forever. A participant that crashed in the meantime commits when it recovers, since it voted yes and cannot refuse.

So the protocol has two crucial points of no return: a participant’s yes vote, and the coordinator’s decision. Single-node atomic commit lumps both into one event, writing the commit record. In the marriage analogy, if you faint after saying “I do”, you are still married; when you recover you can ask the officiant about your global transaction ID, or wait for the officiant’s next retry.

Lucas’s shop writes an order to PostgreSQL and sends an “order created” message to a broker, atomically. There is no separate transaction server anywhere. The coordinator is a library loaded into his application process, and it keeps its commit/abort log on the application server’s local disk.

That is how XA usually works. XA isn’t a network protocol: it’s a C API for talking to a transaction coordinator, with bindings such as Java’s JTA. Each database or broker driver that supports XA joins the global transaction and exposes callbacks the coordinator uses to say prepare, commit or abort. Everything flows through the driver. So if the application server dies after the participants have prepared, they are stuck in doubt, and they can’t even call the coordinator to ask.

What the book says

X/Open XA (eXtended Architecture) is a standard for implementing 2PC across heterogeneous technologies, introduced in 1991 and widely implemented: by many relational databases (including PostgreSQL, MySQL, Db2, SQL Server and Oracle) and message brokers (including ActiveMQ, HornetQ, MSMQ and IBM MQ).

XA is not a network protocol; it is merely a C API for interfacing with a transaction coordinator. In Java EE, XA transactions are implemented with the Java Transaction API (JTA), supported by many JDBC database drivers and JMS message-broker drivers. A driver that supports XA calls the XA API to find out whether an operation is part of a distributed transaction, sends the necessary information to the server, and exposes callbacks through which the coordinator asks the participant to prepare, commit or abort.

The coordinator implements the XA API; in practice it is often a library loaded into the same process as the application, which keeps track of the participants, collects their responses, and uses a log on the local disk to record each transaction’s commit/abort decision.

If the application process crashes or its machine dies, the coordinator goes with it, and participants with prepared but uncommitted transactions are stuck in doubt. The server must be restarted and the coordinator library must read its log to recover each outcome before using the XA callbacks to commit or abort. The database server cannot contact the coordinator directly, since all communication must go via its client library.

One stuck transaction, and a queue that never moves

Why worry about one transaction stuck in doubt? Can’t everyone else just carry on? No, because of locks. The in-doubt transaction wrote two account rows, so it holds exclusive locks on them to prevent dirty writes. Under two-phase locking it also holds shared locks on what it read.

It can release none of them until it commits or aborts, and it can’t do either until the coordinator comes back. If the coordinator takes 20 minutes to restart, the locks are held for 20 minutes. If its log is lost, they are held forever, or until an administrator steps in. Every other transaction that touches those rows piles up behind them, and large parts of the application stop working.

What the book says

The problem with a transaction stuck in doubt is locking. Database transactions usually take row-level exclusive locks on any rows they modify, to prevent dirty writes; with serializable isolation, a database using 2PL also takes shared locks on any rows the transaction reads.

The database cannot release those locks until the transaction commits or aborts, so when using 2PC a transaction must hold them throughout the time it is in doubt. If the coordinator has crashed and takes 20 minutes to start up again, the locks are held for 20 minutes; if the coordinator’s log is entirely lost, they are held forever, or at least until an administrator resolves the situation manually.

While the locks are held, no other transaction can modify those rows, and depending on the isolation level other transactions may even be blocked from reading them. This can cause large parts of your application to become unavailable until the in-doubt transaction is resolved.

What the book says

In theory, a crashed and restarted coordinator should cleanly recover its state from the log and resolve any in-doubt transactions. In practice, orphaned in-doubt transactions do occur: transactions whose outcome the coordinator cannot decide, e.g. because the transaction log has been lost or corrupted by a software bug. They cannot be resolved automatically, so they sit forever in the database, holding locks and blocking other transactions.

Even rebooting the database servers will not fix this, since a correct implementation of 2PC must preserve the locks of an in-doubt transaction across restarts; otherwise it would risk violating atomicity.

The only way out is for an administrator to manually decide whether to commit or roll back: examine the participants of each in-doubt transaction, determine whether any participant has committed or aborted already, then apply the same outcome to the others. This most likely happens under high stress and time pressure during a serious production outage.

Many XA implementations have an emergency escape hatch called heuristic decisions: a participant unilaterally decides to abort or commit an in-doubt transaction without a definitive decision from the coordinator. Heuristic here is “a euphemism for probably breaking atomicity”, since it violates the system of promises in 2PC; it is intended only for catastrophic situations, not for regular use.

Three cracks that replication can’t paper over

First crack: the coordinator is a single point of failure for the whole system, and because it lives in the application server, that server’s local disk is suddenly as precious as the databases. Second crack: even if you replicate the coordinator, XA gives it no direct line to the participants. Every message goes through the application code and its database drivers, so the application becomes the single point of failure instead.

Third crack: XA has to work with every kind of data system, so it is a lowest common denominator. There is no standard way for systems to tell each other who is waiting for which lock, so it can’t detect a deadlock that spans two systems. For the same reason it doesn’t work with SSI, which would need a protocol for spotting conflicts across systems.

Try it: in Single point of failure, press Kill the app server, then Lose its disk. In Replicated coordinator, kill a coordinator node (fine), then kill the application process. In Cross-system deadlock, press Next step until both transactions wait, and see what each database’s deadlock detector can see.

What the book says

A single-node coordinator is a single point of failure for the entire system, and making it part of the application server is also problematic, because the coordinator’s logs on its local disk become a crucial part of the durable system state, as important as the databases themselves.

In principle, an XA coordinator could be highly available and replicated. That still doesn’t solve a fundamental problem: XA provides no way for the coordinator and the participants to communicate directly. They can communicate only via the application code that invoked the transaction and the database drivers through which it calls the participants, so even with a replicated coordinator, the application code would be a single point of failure. Fixing that would require redesigning how application code runs to make it replicated or restartable, perhaps similar to durable execution, but no tools seem to take this approach in practice.

Because XA must be compatible with a wide range of data systems, it is a lowest common denominator. It cannot detect deadlocks across different systems, since that would need a standardized protocol for exchanging information on the locks each transaction is waiting for, and it does not work with SSI, since that would need a protocol for identifying conflicts across systems.

These problems are somewhat inherent in transactions across heterogeneous technologies, but keeping heterogeneous data systems consistent with one another is still a real and important problem, which the book returns to in the next section and in Chapter 12.

Same 2PC, minus the weak spots

“NewSQL” databases such as CockroachDB, TiDB, Spanner, FoundationDB and YugabyteDB use 2PC for transactions that write to several shards, and they don’t suffer from XA’s problems. The book lists four fixes. Replicate the coordinator, with automatic failover. Let the coordinator talk to the shards directly, with no application code in between. Replicate the shards, so one faulty node doesn’t force an abort. And couple the commit protocol with distributed concurrency control, so deadlocks across shards are detected and reads are consistent across shards. The replication is usually done with consensus (Chapter 10), which fails over without any human.

What the book says

Internal distributed transactions, where all participating nodes are part of the same database running the same software, are a defining feature of NewSQL databases such as CockroachDB, TiDB, Spanner, FoundationDB and YugabyteDB. Some message brokers, such as Kafka, also support internal distributed transactions.

Many of these systems use 2PC to ensure atomicity of transactions that write to multiple shards, yet they don’t suffer from the problems of XA: they don’t need to interface with other technologies, so they avoid the lowest-common-denominator trap and can use more reliable and faster protocols.

The biggest problems with XA can be fixed by replicating the coordinator with automatic failover; allowing the coordinator and data shards to communicate directly without intermediary application code; replicating the participating shards, which reduces the risk of having to abort because of a fault in one shard; and coupling the atomic commitment protocol with a distributed concurrency control protocol that supports deadlock detection and consistent reads across shards.

Consensus algorithms are commonly used to replicate the coordinator and the shards; they fail over automatically without human intervention while continuing to guarantee strong consistency (Chapter 10). The isolation levels depend on the system, but snapshot isolation and serializable snapshot isolation are both possible across shards.

Distributed transactions · exactly-once message processing revisited

A guest list at the door, instead of a treaty between two systems

Back to Mei’s order queue. You don’t actually need a distributed transaction across the broker and the database to process each message exactly once. You need one small table, processed_ids, and an ordinary transaction inside the database.

Every message has a unique ID. In one database transaction: check whether the ID is already in the table (if so, it was processed: ack it and drop it), otherwise insert the ID and make the message’s writes, then commit. Only after the commit, acknowledge the message to the broker, and later delete the ID in a separate transaction. A crash anywhere is harmless, and a uniqueness constraint on the ID stops two concurrent retries. Recording the ID makes processing idempotent.

Try it: pick a crash point and press Deliver m-42; the broker redelivers when it must. Watch the Order rows counter stay at 1. Then press Two retries at once.

What the book says

An important use of distributed transactions is to make an operation take effect exactly once, even if processing crashes and is retried. But you don’t actually need distributed transactions for exactly-once semantics; this approach needs only transactions within the database:

  • Every message has a unique ID, and the database has a table of processed message IDs. Begin a database transaction and check the ID; if it is already present, the message was processed, so acknowledge it to the broker and drop it.
  • Otherwise add the ID to the table, process the message (possibly with more writes in the same transaction), and commit.
  • Once the transaction is committed, acknowledge the message to the broker.
  • Once it is acknowledged, the broker won’t process it again, so delete the ID from the table (in a separate transaction).

A crash before the commit aborts the transaction and the broker retries. A crash after the commit but before the ack also retries, but the retry sees the ID and drops it. A crash after the ack but before the delete leaves an old ID that does no harm beyond a little storage. If a retry happens before the transaction is aborted (e.g. a network interruption), a uniqueness constraint on the table of message IDs prevents two concurrent transactions from inserting the same ID.

So exactly-once processing needs only transactions within the database. Recording the message ID makes processing idempotent; stream processors such as Kafka Streams use a similar approach (Chapter 12). Internal distributed transactions are still useful for scaling such patterns, e.g. storing message IDs on one shard and the main data on other shards with atomic commit across them.

One transaction at a time on one thread. Simple and effective if every transaction is short (stored procedures), the data fits in memory, and write throughput fits one CPU core or shards cleanly.

Readers and writers block each other, and locks are held until commit. The standard for decades, avoided by many for its poor performance and frequent deadlocks.

Optimistic: nobody blocks. Each transaction is checked when it commits, and aborted if what it read has gone stale. Long read-write transactions abort more.

The book’s conclusion: transactions are an abstraction layer that lets an application pretend certain concurrency problems and certain hardware and software faults don’t exist. A large class of errors becomes a simple abort, and the application just tries again. An application that only reads and writes single records may manage without them; for more complex access patterns, they hugely reduce the number of error cases you have to think about, whichever data model you use.

Words to know

Grown-up words, in plain language.

transaction
A group of reads and writes the database treats as one logical unit.
commit
“Done: make it real.” After this, all the transaction’s writes are official at once.
abort / rollback
“Never mind.” Every write from that transaction is thrown away, so it is safe to retry.
ACID
Atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability. Sound as an idea, vague as a label.
BASE
Basically available, soft state, eventual consistency. In practice, just “not ACID.”
atomicity
On a fault, the transaction is aborted and all of its writes are undone. Really: abortability.
consistency (ACID)
Your own invariants stay true. Mostly the application’s job, not the database’s.
invariant / constraint
A rule that must always hold, and the schema’s way of making the database enforce one.
isolation
Concurrent transactions can’t step on each other’s toes.
durability
Once committed, not forgotten: on nonvolatile storage, copied to other nodes, or both.
fsync
The system call that forces buffered writes to actually reach the disk.
write-ahead log
A log of changes written before the data itself, so a crash partway through can be recovered.
multi-object transaction
One transaction that changes several rows, documents or index entries together.
compare-and-set (CAS)
“Write this only if the value is still what I read.” A conditional write.
race condition
A bug that appears only when concurrent operations happen to interleave unluckily.
dirty read
Seeing another transaction’s write before it has committed. It might still be rolled back.
dirty write
Overwriting another transaction’s uncommitted write.
cascading abort
Having to abort every transaction that read data which was later rolled back.
read committed
No dirty reads and no dirty writes. The default in Oracle, PostgreSQL and SQL Server.
read uncommitted
Prevents dirty writes but not dirty reads.
read skew / nonrepeatable read
Seeing different parts of the database at different points in time, so the totals look wrong.
snapshot isolation
Each transaction reads from a consistent snapshot: what was committed when it started.
MVCC
Multiversion concurrency control: keep several committed versions of each row, tagged by transaction ID.
txid
A unique, always-increasing transaction ID that tags every row version a transaction writes.
garbage collection (MVCC)
Removing row versions that no transaction can see any more. In PostgreSQL, the vacuum.
repeatable read
An SQL-standard level whose meaning differs from database to database.
lost update
Two read-modify-write cycles run concurrently, and one change clobbers the other.
optimistic locking
Applying an update only if a version number hasn’t changed since you read it.
SELECT … FOR UPDATE
Read rows and lock them, so nobody else can change them until you commit.
deadlock
Transactions each waiting for a lock the other holds. The database aborts one.
write skew
Two transactions read the same data, then each writes based on a premise the other just made false.
phantom
A write in one transaction that changes the result of a search query in another.
materializing conflicts
Creating rows purely so there is something to lock. A last resort.
serializable
The result is the same as if the transactions had run one at a time. The strongest level.
stored procedure
Transaction code submitted to the database ahead of time, so it runs without network round trips.
state machine replication
Running the same deterministic procedure on every replica instead of copying its writes.
two-phase locking (2PL)
Acquire shared or exclusive locks as you go, release them all only at commit or abort.
shared / exclusive lock
Many readers may share a lock; a writer needs it all to itself.
predicate lock
A lock on every object that matches a search condition, even ones that don’t exist yet.
index-range (next-key) lock
A cheaper, broader approximation of a predicate lock, attached to part of an index.
pessimistic / optimistic
Wait whenever something might go wrong, or carry on and check at commit.
SSI
Serializable snapshot isolation: snapshot reads, plus aborting transactions whose premise went stale.
distributed transaction
A transaction that touches several nodes, such as several shards.
atomic commitment
Making every node in a transaction commit, or every node abort, never a mixture.
two-phase commit (2PC)
Ask every participant to prepare; if all vote yes, commit everywhere, otherwise abort.
coordinator / participant
The transaction manager that runs 2PC, and the database nodes it asks.
commit point
The moment the coordinator writes its decision to its log. No going back after it.
in doubt
A participant that voted yes and hasn’t heard the decision. It must wait, still holding its locks.
three-phase commit
A nonblocking alternative to 2PC that needs bounded network delays, so it can’t guarantee atomicity in practice.
XA
X/Open eXtended Architecture: a C API for 2PC across different databases and message brokers.
heuristic decision
A participant deciding an in-doubt transaction on its own. A euphemism for probably breaking atomicity.
exactly-once semantics
A message’s effects happen once, even if processing has to be retried.
idempotent
Safe to do twice: doing it again changes nothing.
NewSQL
Databases such as CockroachDB, TiDB, Spanner, FoundationDB and YugabyteDB that scale and keep ACID transactions.
