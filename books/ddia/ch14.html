Big idea 1 · predictive analytics

One label, five closed doors

Using data to guess tomorrow's weather hurts nobody. Using data to guess whether Imani will pay back a loan is another matter: that guess lands on a real person's life.

Every organisation in town is only being careful. For a bank, a missed customer costs a little and a bad loan costs a lot, so its rule is simple: if in doubt, say no. The trouble starts when everyone leans on the same kind of guess. One “risky” label — right or wrong — and Imani hears no at the job, no at the airport, no for insurance, no for a flat, and no at the bank.

A court has to prove you did something before it shuts you in. A score needs no proof, and there is almost nobody to appeal to. The book repeats the name this has been given: an algorithmic prison.

Try it: press “Apply everywhere”. Then drag the caution slider up and down and watch the doors. Finally switch the label to “a mistake” — and notice that not one door changes its mind.

What the book says

The chapter opens by stepping back: every system is built for a purpose, and every action has intended and unintended consequences. Many datasets are about people — their behavior, their interests, their identity — so “we must treat such data with humanity and respect”. Guidelines such as the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct exist but are rarely discussed, applied and enforced in practice. Ethics is not a checklist to comply with; it is “a participatory and iterative process of reflection, in dialog with the people involved, with accountability for the results”.

Predictive analytics is a major reason people are excited about big data and AI. Predicting the weather or the spread of diseases is one thing; predicting whether a convict is likely to reoffend, a loan applicant likely to default, or an insurance customer likely to make expensive claims has a direct effect on individual lives.

Payment networks want to prevent fraud, banks want to avoid bad loans, airlines want to avoid hijackings, companies want to avoid bad hires. For them a missed business opportunity is cheap and a bad loan or problematic employee is expensive, so when in doubt they are better off saying no.

As algorithmic decision making spreads, someone who has (accurately or falsely) been labeled as risky may suffer many of those “no” decisions. Being systematically excluded from jobs, air travel, insurance coverage, property rental, financial services and other key aspects of society has been called “algorithmic prison”. Criminal justice presumes innocence until proven guilty; automated systems can exclude a person without any proof of guilt, and with little chance of appeal.

Big idea 2 · bias and discrimination

The learner that copies yesterday's unfairness

Nobody writes the rules of a prediction machine. It is handed a pile of old decisions and works the rules out by itself: “find past people who look like this new person, and do what was done to them.”

In this town the old loan officers were unfair to people in the circle group. Circles who paid every bill on time still heard no. Those decisions are now the machine's lesson book — so it learns the unfairness along with everything else, faithfully.

“Easy,” says the bank, “we'll hide the group column.” But most circles live in the South postcode. The machine can no longer see the group, yet the postcode whispers nearly the same thing. A stand-in like that is called a proxy.

Try it: Lars and Noor both pay on time — they differ only in group and postcode. Press “Learn and decide”. Then hide the group column. Then hide the postcode too, and look at the “agrees with history” number: to be fair, the machine has to disagree with the past.

What the book says

Decisions made by an algorithm are not necessarily better or worse than a human's. Every person is likely to have biases, and discriminatory practices can become culturally institutionalized; there is hope that decisions based on data could be fairer to people who are often overlooked.

But with predictive analytics and AI we are not merely automating a human's rules — we leave the rules themselves to be inferred from data. The learned patterns are opaque: even if the data indicates a correlation, we may not know why. “If the input to an algorithm carries a systematic bias, the system will most likely learn and amplify that bias in its output.”

Anti-discrimination laws in many countries prohibit treating people differently depending on protected traits such as ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, or beliefs. Other features may be analyzed — but in racially segregated neighborhoods a person's postal code or even their IP address is a strong predictor of race.

Believing that an algorithm could take biased data as input and produce fair and impartial output has been satirized as “machine learning is like money laundering for bias”.

Predictive systems merely extrapolate from the past; if the past is discriminatory, they codify and amplify that discrimination. “If we want the future to be better than the past, moral imagination is required, and that's something only humans can provide. Data and models should be our tools, not our masters.”

Big idea 3 · responsibility and accountability

“Computer says no” — and then what?

Mateo is turned down for a loan. If a person had decided, he could ask her why, show her the mistake, and she would have to answer for it. When a program decides, who answers?

An old-fashioned credit score asks “How did you behave in the past?” It is built from facts about Mateo's own borrowing. If one fact is wrong it can be corrected — though the agencies don't make that easy.

A prediction machine asks something else: “Who is similar to you, and how did people like you behave?” It drops Mateo into a bucket with his neighbours. The bucket can be right on average and still wrong about him. If people in your country live to 80 on average, nobody expects you to drop dead on your 80th birthday.

Try it: in each mode press “Ask why”, then “Dispute a fact”, then “Appeal”. Count how many of the three actually get Mateo somewhere.

What the book says

If a human makes a mistake they can be held accountable, and the person affected can appeal. Algorithms make mistakes too — but who is accountable? When a self-driving car causes an accident, who is responsible? If a credit scoring algorithm systematically discriminates, is there any recourse? If your ML system comes under judicial review, can you explain to the judge how it decided? “People should not be able to evade their responsibility by blaming an algorithm.”

Credit rating agencies are a classic example: a bad score makes life difficult, but it is normally based on relevant facts about a person's actual borrowing history, and errors can be corrected (although the agencies do not make this easy). ML-based scoring typically uses a much wider range of inputs and is much more opaque.

Drawing parallels to others' behavior implies stereotyping — for example by where people live, a close proxy for race and socioeconomic class. What about people put in the wrong bucket? And if a decision is wrong because of erroneous data, recourse is almost impossible.

Much data is statistical: even if the probability distribution as a whole is correct, individual cases may well be wrong. “A blind belief in the supremacy of data for making decisions is not only delusional but also positively dangerous.” We need to work out how to avoid reinforcing biases, make algorithms accountable and transparent, and fix them when they inevitably make mistakes.

The same analytics can do good or harm: it could focus aid on those who need it most, or be used by predatory businesses to find vulnerable people and sell them risky products such as high-cost loans or worthless college degrees.

Big idea 4 · feedback loops

The spiral that feeds itself

Rosa is a good baker with a good credit score. Then the bakery closes — nothing she did. She misses a few bills while she looks for work, and her score dips.

In her town, employers look up credit scores before hiring. A lower score means no job; no job means more missed bills; more missed bills mean a lower score. The prediction “Rosa is risky” makes itself come true. That is a feedback loop.

Loops hide in gentler places too. A service that is good at guessing what you like may end up showing you only opinions you already hold — an echo chamber. You can't always see a loop coming, but you stand a chance if you look at the whole system, people included. That habit is called systems thinking.

Try it: press “Next month” a few times and watch the score sink. Then break one link — make employers look at Rosa's work instead of her score — and keep going.

What the book says

Even recommendation systems raise hard issues. When services become good at predicting the content users want to see, they may show people only opinions they already agree with, leading to echo chambers in which stereotypes, misinformation and polarization can breed. The book notes we are already seeing the impact of social media echo chambers on election campaigns.

When predictive analytics affect people's lives, self-reinforcing feedback loops are particularly pernicious. The book's example is employers using credit scores to evaluate hires: a misfortune outside your control leads to missed payments, a worse score, less chance of work, joblessness, poverty, and a worse score again — “a downward spiral due to poisonous assumptions, hidden behind a camouflage of mathematical rigor and data”.

Another loop: economists found that when gas stations in Germany introduced algorithmic prices, competition was reduced and prices for consumers went up, because the algorithms learned to collude.

Systems thinking means thinking about the entire system — not just the computerized parts but also the people interacting with it. Does the system reinforce and amplify existing differences (making the rich richer or the poor poorer), or does it try to combat injustice? Even with the best intentions, beware of unintended consequences.

Big idea 5 · privacy, tracking and surveillance

Swap one word and read it again

When an app stores what you typed in because you asked it to, it is working for you. When it quietly logs everything you do on the side, it starts working for somebody else as well. If adverts pay for the app, the advertisers are the real customers — and you are what is being watched.

The book plays a small game to make this felt. Take a proud sentence about a company's data, swap the word data for the word surveillance, and listen to whether it still sounds so good.

It matters because the watching has moved indoors. We are nearly at the point where every lived-in room holds at least one internet-connected microphone: phones, smart TVs, voice assistants, baby monitors, even children's toys. The most controlling rulers of the past could only dream of a microphone in every room — and we carried them in ourselves, because the gadgets are so useful.

Try it: tap the gadgets to connect them and watch what the company learns. Then flip the word at the top and read the sentence and the labels again.

What the book says

Some tracking serves users: clicked search results improve ranking, “people who liked X also liked Y” helps discovery, A/B tests and user flow analysis improve a UI. But when a service is funded through advertising, the advertisers are the actual customers; tracking becomes more detailed, analyses further-reaching, and data is retained for a long time to build profiles. The book says this relationship can be appropriately described by a word with more sinister connotations: surveillance.

The thought experiment replaces the word data with surveillance — and jokes that this makes the book Designing Surveillance-Intensive Applications. In trying to make software “eat the world” we have built “the greatest mass surveillance infrastructure ever seen”: smartphones, smart TVs, voice-controlled assistants, baby monitors, and children's toys with cloud speech recognition, many with a terrible security record.

Surveillance of our location and movements, social relationships and communications, purchases and payments, and health has become almost unavoidable. A surveillance organization may know more about a person than they know themselves — for example identifying illnesses or economic problems before the person is aware of them. The difference from totalitarian regimes is “just” that the data is collected by corporations to provide services rather than by government agencies seeking control.

Not all data collection qualifies as surveillance, but examining it as such helps us understand our relationship with the collector. Feeling you have “nothing to hide” means you are in line with existing power structures and not a marginalized minority; not everyone is so fortunate.

It looks less benign once it drives decisions: driving behavior tracked by cars without drivers' consent affecting insurance premiums, health insurance that depends on wearing a fitness tracker — and the movement sensor in a smartwatch can be used to work out what you are typing, such as passwords, with fairly good accuracy.

Big idea 6 · consent and freedom of choice

“I agree” — but did you have a choice?

“Nobody forced you,” says the app. “You pressed Agree.” True — but look at the button. The terms are long and foggy, the box is already ticked, and you can't bargain: the service writes the rules, you take them or leave them.

And leaving isn't free. If your whole class plans parties, football and homework on one app, saying no means missing out. The more friends are on it, the more a “no” costs — that pull is called a network effect. Worse, your friends' address books and photos describe you, even though you never agreed to anything.

Real consent looks different: plain words, nothing ticked in advance, one question per purpose — and you can say no without being punished for it.

Try it: press “Decline”, then “A week goes by”, and read the three numbers. Slide the number of friends on the app up and down. Then switch to “A real choice” and untick whatever you like.

What the book says

The usual defence is that users voluntarily agree to the terms of service and privacy policy and get a valuable service in return. The book finds several problems with it. First, why is the tracking necessary? Some directly improves features (click-through rates improve search ranking; products bought together feed suggestions); tracking to build advertising profiles is less clearly in the user's interest.

Second, most users have little knowledge of what data they feed into our databases or how it is retained and processed, and “most privacy policies do more to obscure than to illuminate”. Without understanding, users cannot give meaningful consent. Data from one user often also says things about other people who are not users and have agreed to nothing; derived datasets are precisely the kind of data users cannot meaningfully understand.

Data is extracted through a one-way process, not a relationship with true reciprocity: no dialogue, no negotiation. The relationship is asymmetric and one-sided; the terms are set by the service.

Under the EU's GDPR, consent must be “freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous”, the user must be able to “refuse or withdraw consent without detriment”, requests must use “clear and plain language”, and “silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity” do not constitute consent. Consent is not the only lawful basis — others include complying with legislation, protecting somebody's life, and legitimate interest (e.g. fraud prevention) — but it is the most frequently used by internet services.

Opting out is not a free choice either. If a service is “regarded by most people as essential for basic social participation”, its use is effectively mandatory; the book's examples are carrying a smartphone, using social networks for socializing, and using Google to find information. With network effects there is a social cost to declining. Platforms are designed to engage, many using game mechanics and tactics common in gambling. Declining is realistic only for the few with the time, knowledge and privilege to afford it; for everyone else “surveillance becomes inescapable”.

Big idea 7 · privacy and use of data

Privacy means you choose who knows what

Some people say “privacy is dead” because lots of us post our lives online. The book says that gets the word wrong. Privacy isn't keeping everything secret. It is the freedom to choose what to reveal, and to whom.

Aiko has a rare illness. She is glad to share her medical data with researchers hunting for a treatment. She would be far more careful if the same facts could reach an insurer or an employer. Same fact, different audience, different answer — and the answer is hers to give. The book calls this a decision right.

When a service collects her data, that right doesn't vanish. It moves. Her privacy settings still decide what other users see, but the service itself sees everything, and it now decides what is revealed — for instance by selling adverts aimed at “people with a rare illness”.

Try it: tap the boxes to open or close each fact for each audience. Then switch to “Through an online service” and try to close the last column.

What the book says

The claim that “privacy is dead” is false and rests on a misunderstanding. Having privacy “does not mean keeping everything secret; it means having the freedom to choose what to reveal to whom, what to make public, and what to keep secret”. The right to privacy is a decision right: each person decides where to be on the spectrum between secrecy and transparency in each situation. It is an important aspect of freedom and autonomy.

When data is extracted through surveillance infrastructure, privacy rights are not necessarily eroded but transferred to the data collector. “Trust us to do the right thing with your data” means the right to decide what to reveal moves from the individual to the company, which exercises it with the goal of maximizing profit. Companies keep most of the outcome secret, because revealing it would seem creepy; intimate information surfaces only indirectly, for example as tools for targeting ads at people suffering from a particular illness. Even if nobody can be personally reidentified from that bucket, they have lost their agency over the disclosure.

Many companies focus on managing the perception of creepiness rather than asking how intrusive they are. Any data may be wrong, undesirable or inappropriate — something factually correct may still trigger painful memories — so engineers must be humble and build mechanisms for handling those failures.

Privacy settings are a starting point for handing back control, but regardless of the setting the service itself has unfettered access and may use the data in any way its privacy policy permits, often going much further than what is overtly visible to users.

This large-scale transfer of privacy rights from individuals to corporations is historically unprecedented. Trust relationships have always existed — patient and doctor, defendant and attorney — but there the use of data is strictly governed by ethical, legal and regulatory constraints.

Big idea 8 · data as assets and power

Not the new oil — handle it like something hazardous

Some people call the trail you leave behind “data exhaust”, as if it were worthless smoke. It isn't worthless: companies trade it in secret, and a young company is often valued by how many people it can watch.

Because it is valuable, many hands reach for it. Thieves break in. Someone inside leaks it. The company goes bankrupt and the data is sold with the furniture. New bosses arrive who don't share the old values. A future government demands a copy.

So critics say data is less like gold or oil and more like hazardous material: the more you store, and the longer you keep it, the bigger the spill when something goes wrong. And there is one spill-proof kind of data — the kind you never collected.

Try it: Crumb delivers bread. It only needs your address and your order, but it collects everything and keeps it forever. Run a year, then press “Something goes wrong” and read the spill. Reset, switch off what Crumb doesn't need, shorten the keeping time, and do it again.

What the book says

Behavioral data is sometimes called “data exhaust”, suggesting worthless waste. The book argues the opposite view is more correct: if targeted advertising pays for a service, the user activity that generates the data could be regarded as a form of labor, and the application as a means to lure users into feeding ever more personal information into the surveillance infrastructure.

Personal data is a valuable asset, as shown by data brokers operating in secrecy — purchasing, aggregating, analyzing and reselling people's personal data, mostly for marketing. Startups are valued by their user numbers, or “eyeballs” — that is, by their surveillance capabilities.

Companies want the data; governments want it too, and may obtain it through secret deals, coercion, legal compulsion, or simply theft. When a company goes bankrupt, personal data is one of the assets sold. And because data is difficult to secure, breaches happen disconcertingly often.

Hence critics call data a “toxic asset”, or at least “hazardous material” — “maybe data is not the new gold, or the new oil, but rather the new uranium”. Systems may be compromised by criminals or hostile intelligence services, leaked by insiders, inherited by unscrupulous management, or seized by a regime with no qualms about compelling a handover. We must consider not just today's political environment but all possible future governments; as Bruce Schneier puts it, “It is poor civic hygiene to install technologies that could someday facilitate a police state.”

“Knowledge is power” — and “to scrutinize others while avoiding scrutiny oneself is one of the most important forms of power”. Technology companies may not seek political power, but the data they have accumulated, much of it surreptitiously and outside public oversight, gives them a lot of power over our lives.

Big idea 9 · the Industrial Revolution, laws and self-control

Dirty rivers then, leaky data now

Factories once made whole countries richer — and filled the air with smoke and the rivers with waste. Owners lived in splendour while workers, children among them, laboured long hours in harsh and dangerous places.

It took a long time, but safeguards came: rules for clean air and water, safety at work, no child labour, inspections for food. Doing business cost more. Society gained enormously, and hardly anyone wants to go back.

The book borrows Bruce Schneier's words: data is the pollution problem of the information age. Laws can help — collect only what you need, only for a purpose you named — but the book's real hope is a change of heart in the people who build things: stop treating users as numbers to push up, and remember they are humans who deserve respect, dignity and a say.

Try it: switch the safeguards on one by one in the 1800s, then jump to today and do the same. Watch what each one costs — and what it buys.

What the book says

The Industrial Revolution brought sustained growth and better living standards in the long run, along with dreadful air and water pollution, cramped and unsanitary housing, long hours and child labor. Safeguards — environmental protection regulations, workplace safety protocols, laws prohibiting child labor, health inspections for food — took a long time. The cost of doing business rose, “but society as a whole benefited hugely”.

Schneier, as quoted: “Data is the pollution problem of the information age, and protecting privacy is the environmental challenge… our grandchildren will look back at us during these early decades of the information age and judge us on how we addressed the challenge of data collection and misuse. We should try to make them proud.”

Legislation: the GDPR says personal data must be “collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes” and be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary”. This principle of data minimization runs directly counter to the philosophy of big data — maximize collection, combine datasets, explore for unforeseen insights. The book judges that the regulation has had some effect on online advertising but has been weakly enforced and has not changed culture much across the wider industry.

Opposition to regulation is partly justified: sharing medical data creates privacy risks but also opportunities for better diagnostics and treatments, and overregulation may prevent such breakthroughs. Balancing is difficult.

Self-regulation: the book calls for a culture shift — stop regarding users as metrics to be optimized; educate them about how their data is used; let each individual keep control over their own data. That control is like a national park: unprotected, it suffers the tragedy of the commons. “Ubiquitous surveillance is not inevitable. We are still able to stop it.” As a first step: don't retain data forever, purge it as soon as it is no longer needed, and minimize what you collect. “Data you don't have is data that can't be leaked, stolen, or compelled by governments to be handed over.”

The end of the road · looking back

Fourteen stops through Data Town

This is the last page of the book, so the authors do what you do at the top of a hill: turn around and look at the road. Storage engines, copies, shards, transactions, unreliable networks, votes, batch jobs, streams — every stop was a tool.

Tools don't choose what they're used for. The people holding them do. The same data that can find the families who most need help can also pick out the ones who are easiest to trick.

So the book ends with a job for its readers rather than a fact: work toward the kind of world you want to live in — one that treats people with humanity and respect. “Let's work together toward that goal.”

Try it: tap any stop to walk there and hear what the book says it taught — or press “Walk the whole road”.

What the book says

The closing summary runs through all fourteen chapters — from the trade-offs of Chapter 1 to the streaming philosophy of Chapter 13 — and then describes this one: “although data can be used to do good, it can also do significant harm: making decisions that seriously affect people's lives and are difficult to appeal against, leading to discrimination and exploitation, normalizing surveillance, and exposing intimate information.” It adds the risk of data breaches, and that a well-intentioned use of data may have unintended consequences.

Its last words: “Given the large impact that software and data have on the world, we as engineers must remember that we carry a responsibility to work toward the kind of world that we want to live in: a world that treats people with humanity and respect. Let's work together toward that goal.”

Earlier in the chapter, in the same spirit: “As people working in technology, if we don't consider the societal impact of our work, we're not doing our job.”

What to carry with you

This chapter has no seesaws — just a handful of things worth remembering every time you build something.

Data is about people

Behind the rows are behaviour, interests and identities. Treat them with humanity and respect; human dignity comes first.

A guess can close a door

Careful organisations say no when in doubt. Many of them leaning on one label can lock a person out — with no proof and little chance of appeal.

The past is an unfair teacher

A learner copies the bias in its lesson book, even through stand-ins like a postcode. A better future needs moral imagination, and only humans have it.

Somebody must answer

Decisions need reasons you can see, facts you can correct and a human you can appeal to. Nobody gets to hide behind “the algorithm did it”.

Look for the loops

Predictions can make themselves come true. Think about the whole system — people included — and watch for consequences nobody intended.

Tracking can become surveillance

Ask who the real customer is. If the sentence sounds bad with “surveillance” in it, the word “data” was hiding something.

Privacy is a choice, not a secret

It is the right to decide who knows what. Real consent is informed, specific, never pre-ticked — and saying no must not be punished.

Store less, keep it shorter

Data is hazardous material. What you never collected, or already purged, can't be leaked, stolen or demanded.

Make the grandchildren proud

Safeguards cost something and are worth it. Laws help, but the real fix is a culture that sees users as humans who deserve respect, dignity and agency.

Words to know

Grown-up words, in plain language.

ethics
Thinking hard about what is right and wrong to do — by reflecting and talking with the people affected, not by ticking a checklist.
unintended consequence
Something your system causes that you never meant it to.
predictive analytics
Using past data to guess what a person will do next — repay, claim, reoffend.
algorithmic decision making
Letting a program, rather than a person, say yes or no.
algorithmic prison
Being shut out of jobs, travel, insurance, housing and banking because programs labelled you risky.
bias
A steady tilt against some people. If it is in the training data, the learner picks it up and can amplify it.
protected trait
Something the law says you may not be treated differently for: ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, beliefs.
proxy
A harmless-looking fact, such as a postcode, that secretly stands in for a protected trait.
opaque
You can see the answer but not the reason. Learned patterns are often like this.
moral imagination
Picturing a future that is fairer than the past. Only humans can supply it.
accountability
Someone has to answer for a decision, explain it, and put it right when it is wrong.
recourse
A real way to challenge a decision and have a mistake corrected.
stereotyping
Judging one person by what others “like them” did — putting them in a bucket.
feedback loop
The result of a system flows back in as its next input, so an effect keeps strengthening itself.
echo chamber
A place where you are shown only opinions you already agree with.
systems thinking
Looking at the whole system — computers and people together — to spot consequences early.
tracking
Logging what you do as a side effect of using a service, rather than what you chose to type in.
surveillance
Watching people. The book's test: swap this word in for “data” and see how the sentence sounds.
meaningful consent
A yes from someone who understands what they agree to and could freely have said no.
network effect
A service gets more useful as more people join — which also makes it costly to stay out.
privacy
Not secrecy: the freedom to choose what to reveal, to whom, and what to keep to yourself.
decision right
The right to make that choice. Surveillance moves it from the person to the company.
data broker
A company that quietly buys, combines and resells personal data, mostly for marketing.
hazardous material
The critics' picture of stored personal data: useful, but dangerous when it leaks or changes hands.
data minimization
Collect only what you need for a purpose you named — and purge it when you no longer need it.
tragedy of the commons
A shared treasure that gets ruined because nobody protects it. The book says control over our data could go that way.
