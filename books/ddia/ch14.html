Opening · purpose and consequences

The feature that worked exactly as designed

Priya’s team ships Nearby: opt in, and friends within a kilometre see you as a dot on a map, refreshed every five minutes. Every test passes, p99 latency is fine, and the launch dashboard goes up and to the right. As far as the system is concerned, nothing is wrong.

The book opens its last chapter by stepping back from correctness. Every system is built for a purpose, and every action has intended and unintended consequences. The purpose can be as plain as making money; the consequences can reach people who never opened the app. The dots on Priya’s map aren’t abstract data: they are where people are, which says who they are with, which says a great deal about who they are. Data like that has to be handled with humanity and respect, because users are humans and human dignity comes first.

Nobody on the team meant harm. That’s exactly the trap: the unintended consequences only show up when you look at the system through the eyes of people who aren’t in the requirements doc, and over a longer time than the launch review covers.

On the job
  • Next to “intended behaviour” in a design doc, write “who else does this touch, and how, in a year?”, and fill it in.
  • List the people the data is about, not just the people who use the feature. They are often different.
  • Codes such as the ACM Code of Ethics exist; the book’s complaint is that they are rarely discussed, applied or enforced. Bring them into reviews.

Try it: tap each lens on the left and watch the review fill in. Then move the clock from Launch day to Three years: some consequences only appear later.

What the book says

The book has examined architectures, their pros and cons, and techniques for building reliable, scalable and maintainable applications, but “we have left out a fundamental part of the discussion”.

“Every system is built for a purpose; every action we take has both intended and unintended consequences. The purpose may be as simple as making money, but the consequences may be far-reaching. We, the engineers building these systems, have a responsibility to carefully consider those consequences and to ensure that our decisions do not cause harm.”

Many datasets are about people: their behavior, their interests, their identity. “We must treat such data with humanity and respect. Users are humans too, and human dignity is paramount.”

Software development increasingly involves important ethical choices. Guidelines such as the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct exist, but they are rarely discussed, applied and enforced in practice, so engineers and product managers sometimes take a cavalier attitude to privacy and to potential negative consequences of their products.

Opening · ethics as a process

Ethics is a loop, not a checkbox

A search engine and a gun are both just technology. The book’s point is that neither is good or bad in itself: what matters is how it is used and how it affects people. And the responsibility for that can’t be handed to the technology. It is ours, and an engineer who looks only at the technology and ignores its consequences hasn’t done the whole job.

The words at the heart of ethics, like “good”, “harm” and “fair”, don’t have fixed meanings the way 200 OK does. They need interpretation, and people disagree. That makes it tempting to turn ethics into a compliance step: a list of boxes to tick before launch. The book rejects that. Ethics is a participatory and iterative process of reflection, in dialog with the people involved, with accountability for the results.

Each word in that sentence does work. Participatory: the people affected are in the room. Iterative: you keep looking after launch. Accountability: a named person answers for what happens.

On the job
  • Talk to people who will be on the receiving end, before you build, not after the press coverage.
  • Treat harms like incidents: monitor for them after launch, and give them an owner and a fix-by date.
  • A checklist is fine as a floor. It can’t find a harm nobody thought to write down.

Try it: in Checklist mode press Run the release: every box gets ticked. Then switch to Participatory loop and press Run an iteration two or three times. Compare the harm list on the right.

What the book says

“A technology is not good or bad in itself — what matters is how it is used and how it affects people.” This is true of a software system like a search engine in much the same way as of a weapon like a gun. “The ethical responsibility is ours to bear; it is not sufficient for software engineers to focus exclusively on the technology and ignore its consequences.”

Unlike much of computing, the concepts at the heart of ethics “are not fixed or determinate in their precise meaning; they require interpretation, which may be subjective”. What makes something good or bad is not well defined, and serious discourse on the subject among computing professionals is lacking.

Reasoning about ethics is difficult, but too important to ignore. “Ethics is not going through a checklist to confirm you comply; it’s a participatory and iterative process of reflection, in dialog with the people involved, with accountability for the results.”

Predictive analytics · asymmetric costs

If in doubt, say no — and who pays for the doubt

Predicting tomorrow’s rain or the spread of flu hurts nobody. Predicting whether this applicant will default on a loan, reoffend, or make expensive insurance claims is different: each prediction lands on one person’s life. That’s why predictive analytics, a big part of the excitement about big data and AI, is also full of ethical dilemmas.

Look at it from the bank’s side and caution is just arithmetic. A loan that is repaid earns, say, €1,000. One that defaults loses €10,000. So the bank should approve only when p × loss < (1 − p) × gain, which works out as refuse anyone whose default risk is above 9%. A missed opportunity costs the bank little; a bad loan costs it a lot. If in doubt, say no, exactly as the book says.

Now count the green dots to the right of the line. They are people who would have repaid. The bank’s ledger records them as a small missed profit. For each of them it is a house, a business or a course that didn’t happen. The cost of the bank’s caution is real; it just isn’t on the bank’s books.

On the job
  • When you pick a decision threshold, write down the cost of a false positive and a false negative for the person, not only for the business.
  • Report the number of people wrongly refused next to accuracy and profit. If nobody measures it, nobody will optimise for it.

Try it: press Bank picks its best cut-off. Then raise the cost of a bad loan and press it again. Watch the bank’s profit, and the number of good payers refused.

What the book says

Predictive analytics is a major part of why people are excited about big data and AI, and an area fraught with ethical dilemmas. Predicting the weather or the spread of diseases is one thing; predicting whether a convict is likely to reoffend, whether a loan applicant is likely to default, or whether an insurance customer is likely to make expensive claims is another: “the latter have a direct effect on individual people’s lives”.

Payment networks want to prevent fraudulent transactions, banks want to avoid bad loans, airlines want to avoid hijackings, and companies want to avoid hiring ineffective or untrustworthy people. “From their point of view, the cost of a missed business opportunity is low, but the cost of a bad loan or a problematic employee is much higher, so it is expected for organizations to want to be cautious. If in doubt, they are better off saying no.”

Predictive analytics · algorithmic prison

One label, five closed doors

Each organisation’s caution makes sense on its own, as the last machine showed. The trouble is that they increasingly lean on the same kind of prediction, and often on the very same shared score, fraud list or data broker. A person labelled risky, rightly or wrongly, doesn’t get one “no”. They get a “no” from the employer, the airline, the insurer, the landlord and the bank.

Being systematically shut out of jobs, travel, insurance, housing and financial services limits a person’s freedom so much that it has been called algorithmic prison. Compare it with real prison. In countries that respect human rights, a court presumes you innocent until someone proves you guilty, and you can appeal. An automated system can exclude you without any proof of guilt and with little chance of appeal. Nobody even has to tell you that a score exists.

On the job
  • If your service consumes a shared risk score or blocklist, you are part of the prison. Ask where it comes from, how wrong it is, and how a person gets off it.
  • Give labels an expiry date and a dispute path, and tell people when a decision used one.

Try it: press Apply everywhere. Drag the caution slider and watch the doors. Then switch the label to Label is a mistake and apply again: not one door notices.

What the book says

“As algorithmic decision making becomes more widespread, someone who has (accurately or falsely) been labeled as risky by an algorithm may suffer a large number of those ‘no’ decisions. Systematically being excluded from jobs, air travel, insurance coverage, property rental, financial services, and other key aspects of society is such a large constraint of an individual’s freedom that it has been called ‘algorithmic prison’.”

“In countries that respect human rights, the criminal justice system presumes innocence until proven guilty; on the other hand, automated systems can systematically and arbitrarily exclude a person from participating in society without any proof of guilt, and with little chance of appeal.”

Bias and discrimination · rules inferred from data

Nobody wrote this rule

An algorithm’s decisions aren’t automatically better or worse than a person’s. A loan officer has biases too, even one who tries hard to counter them, and whole institutions can bake discrimination into “how we’ve always done it”. So there is a real hope behind data-driven decisions: that they might be fairer, and give a chance to people the old system overlooked.

But look at what changed. With hand-written rules, software automates a decision a human specified, and every if can be read, argued with and fixed. With predictive analytics and machine learning, the rules themselves are inferred from data. The model may find that people who apply after 11 p.m. default more often, and weight that heavily. The correlation is real in the data, and nobody knows why. Night-shift workers? Panic borrowing? Something else entirely? The pattern is opaque, even to the team that trained it.

On the job
  • For each feature a model uses, ask: would we accept this as a written rule? “Refuse people who apply late at night” fails that test instantly.
  • Keep feature lists reviewable, and log per-decision feature contributions so “why” has an answer you can check.

Try it: pick an applicant and press Decide in each of the three modes. In which mode could the applicant check whether the reason is even relevant?

What the book says

“Decisions made by an algorithm are not necessarily any better or any worse than those made by a human. Every person is likely to have biases, even if they actively try to counteract them, and discriminatory practices can become culturally institutionalized.” There is hope that basing decisions on data rather than subjective and instinctive assessments by people could be more fair and give a better chance to people who are often overlooked or disadvantaged in the traditional system.

“When we develop predictive analytics and AI systems, we are not merely automating a human’s decision by using software to specify the rules for when to say yes or no; we are leaving the rules themselves to be inferred from data. However, the patterns learned by these systems are opaque: even if the data indicates a correlation, we may not know why.”

Bias and discrimination · learn, codify, amplify

The model is more biased than its teacher

For thirty years, loan officers at a bank were a little harder on group B. Not every officer, not every time: people are noisy, so plenty of B applicants still got through. Those thirty years of decisions are now the training set. The model’s job, as far as the loss function is concerned, is to agree with the officers as often as possible.

It learns the tilt, because the tilt is in the data. And then it does something no officer did: it applies the tilt perfectly consistently. Every B applicant near the line now falls on the wrong side of it, every time. The book’s warning is exact: if the input carries a systematic bias, the system will most likely learn and amplify it.

Predictive systems only extrapolate from the past. If the past was discriminatory, they codify it. A fairer future is not in the data, so it can’t be learned from the data; someone has to decide it. The book calls that moral imagination, “something only humans can provide”. Data and models should be our tools, not our masters.

On the job
  • Labels that are past human decisions (“was hired”, “was approved”, “was arrested”) are not ground truth. They are the bias you are worried about.
  • Measure outcome rates per group before and after the model. A model that widens the gap is amplifying, even at high accuracy.
  • Fairness targets are product decisions. Write them down as requirements, like latency.

Try it: press Train on the history, then Add moral imagination. Drag the slider to make the past officers fairer or less fair and watch the amplification number.

Bias and discrimination · protected traits and proxies

Drop the column, keep the bias

Many countries’ anti-discrimination laws forbid treating people differently because of protected traits: ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, beliefs. So the obvious engineering fix is to delete the column. df.drop(columns=["ethnicity"]), ship it, done.

It doesn’t work, because other columns know the answer. In racially segregated neighbourhoods a person’s postal code, or even their IP address, is a strong predictor of race. A model that can’t see the protected trait finds it again through the stand-in. That stand-in is called a proxy, and the model doesn’t need anyone’s permission to use it.

Put like that, it’s absurd to believe biased data could go in and fair, impartial output come out. Yet data-driven decision making often seems to assume exactly that. One critic called it “money laundering for bias”: the prejudice goes in dirty and comes out looking like clean, objective maths.

On the job
  • “We don’t use the protected attribute” is not a fairness argument. Test how well your remaining features predict it.
  • To check for disparate outcomes you often need the protected attribute in an audit dataset, kept apart from the model.
  • Location, device, name, language and purchase history are all common proxies.

Try it: Lars and Noor both pay on time; they differ only in group and postcode. Press Learn and decide. Hide the group column. Then hide the postcode as well, and watch the “agrees with history” number: to be fair, the model has to disagree with the past.

What the book says

“In many countries, anti-discrimination laws prohibit treating people differently depending on protected traits such as ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, or beliefs. Other features of a person’s data may be analyzed, but what happens if they are correlated with protected traits? For example, in racially segregated neighborhoods, a person’s postal code or even their IP address is a strong predictor of race.”

“Put like this, it seems ridiculous to believe that an algorithm could somehow take biased data as input and produce fair and impartial output from it. Yet this belief often seems to be implied by proponents of data-driven decision making — an attitude that has been satirized as ‘machine learning is like money laundering for bias’.”

Responsibility and accountability · who answers?

Can you explain it to the judge?

When a person makes a bad decision, two things normally follow: they can be held accountable, and the person affected can appeal. Algorithms make mistakes too. So who is accountable when they do? When a self-driving car causes an accident, who is responsible? If an automated credit model systematically discriminates against people of one race or religion, is there any recourse? If your ML system ends up in front of a judge, can you explain how it reached its decision?

Most of those questions can only be answered if somebody built the system to answer them. A service that logs {"result": "REFUSE"} and nothing else has made accountability impossible by design. Then “the algorithm decided” becomes the whole story, and the book is blunt about that: people should not be able to evade their responsibility by blaming an algorithm.

On the job
  • Log a decision record for every automated decision about a person: inputs as seen, model and code version, reasons, owner, appeal link.
  • Keep training-data snapshots, so a decision can be reproduced and audited for disparate impact.
  • Every model in production gets a named human owner, the way every service has an on-call rotation.

Try it: pick a case and press Go to court with nothing recorded. Then switch on the fields of the decision record one at a time until every question can be answered.

What the book says

Responsibility and accountability · credit scores vs. look-alikes

Who is similar to you — and then what?

Credit rating agencies are the classic case of collecting data to make decisions about people. A bad score makes life hard, but it has two saving graces. It is normally built from relevant facts about your own borrowing, and an error in your record can be corrected, even if the agencies don’t make that easy.

ML-based scoring usually takes a far wider range of inputs and is far more opaque, so it’s harder to see how a decision came about, or whether someone is being treated unfairly. It also asks a different question. A credit score asks “How did you behave in the past?” Predictive analytics asks “Who is similar to you, and how did people like you behave?”

That second question is stereotyping, for example by where you live, which is a close proxy for race and class. What about the people who land in the wrong bucket? And if the decision rests on erroneous data, recourse is almost impossible: there is no single fact about you to dispute.

On the job
  • Prefer features about the individual’s own relevant behaviour over features about their neighbours, cohort or segment.
  • Build a correction path: a person must be able to see the data used about them and get an error fixed, and the fix must reach every derived dataset.

Try it: in each mode press Ask why, then Dispute a fact, then Appeal. Count how many of the three actually get Mateo anywhere.

What the book says

“Credit rating agencies are a classic example of collecting data to make decisions about people. A bad credit score makes life difficult, but at least a credit score is normally based on relevant facts about a person’s actual borrowing history, and any errors in the record can be corrected (although the agencies normally do not make this easy).” Scoring algorithms based on machine learning typically use a much wider range of inputs and are much more opaque, making it harder to understand how a particular decision has come about and whether someone is being treated in an unfair or discriminatory way.

What about people who get put in the wrong bucket? Furthermore, if a decision is incorrect because of erroneous data, recourse is almost impossible.”

Responsibility and accountability · statistics vs. individuals

Right on average, wrong about you

If average life expectancy in your country is 80, nobody expects you to drop dead on your 80th birthday. The average and the distribution say a lot about the population and very little about the age any one person will reach.

Prediction systems are the same. Their output is a probability, and it can be perfectly right for the population while being wrong for many of the individuals in it. Take a model that gives 100 people a 30% chance of default and is perfectly calibrated: exactly 30 of them default. Treat “30%” as a verdict and you refuse all 100, including the 70 who would have paid. The maths was right. The decision was still wrong about 70 people.

So, the book says, a blind belief in the supremacy of data for making decisions is “not only delusional but also positively dangerous”. As data-driven decisions spread, we have to learn how to avoid reinforcing existing biases, make algorithms accountable and transparent, and fix them when they inevitably make mistakes.

On the job
  • Calibration and accuracy are population metrics. Ask what fraction of individuals a decision is wrong about, and what it costs them.
  • Don’t turn a score into a hard yes/no for high-stakes decisions without a way for the individual to show they are the exception.

Try it: in Life expectancy, press Meet a person a few times and watch the average error. In A 30% risk score, press Let a year pass, then Refuse everyone at 30%.

What the book says

“Much data is statistical in nature, which means that even if the probability distribution on the whole is correct, individual cases may well be wrong. For example, if the average life expectancy in your country is 80 years, that doesn’t mean you’re expected to drop dead on your 80th birthday. From the average and the probability distribution, you can’t say much about the age to which one particular person will live. Similarly, the output of a prediction system is probabilistic and may well be wrong in individual cases.”

“A blind belief in the supremacy of data for making decisions is not only delusional but also positively dangerous. As data-driven decision making becomes more widespread, we will need to figure out how to avoid reinforcing existing biases, how to make algorithms accountable and transparent, and how to fix them when they inevitably make mistakes.”

Feedback loops · recommendation systems

The feed that learns you, then narrows you

Even systems with less dramatic stakes than loans raise hard questions. A recommender is trained to predict what you’ll click. It gets good at it, and that’s the problem. What it shows you shapes what you click, your clicks become its next training data, and what you see shapes what you come to like.

Run that loop for a month and the feed drifts towards whatever you agreed with on day one. That’s an echo chamber, in which stereotypes, misinformation and polarisation can breed, and the book notes we’re already seeing what social-media echo chambers do to election campaigns. Meanwhile every dashboard looks great: click-through rate goes up.

On the job
  • Engagement is a proxy objective. Track diversity of exposure alongside it, per user and over time, not only in an offline evaluation set.
  • Exploration, diversity constraints and “show me the other side” controls cost a little CTR. Decide that trade-off on purpose.
  • Your training data is produced by your own previous model. That is a feedback loop whether you planned one or not.

Try it: with Maximise clicks, press Run 30 days and watch the chart and the diversity number. Reset, switch to Keep some variety, and run again. Compare click-through rates too.

What the book says

“Even with predictive applications that have less immediately far-reaching effects on people, such as recommendation systems, there are difficult issues that we must confront. When services become good at predicting the content users want to see, they may end up showing people only opinions they already agree with, leading to echo chambers in which stereotypes, misinformation, and polarization can breed. We are already seeing the impact social media echo chambers can have on election campaigns.”

Feedback loops · self-reinforcing predictions

The spiral that feeds itself

When predictions affect people’s lives, feedback loops get vicious. The book’s example: employers looking up credit scores to evaluate job candidates. Rosa is a good baker with a good score. Then the bakery closes, a misfortune entirely outside her control. She misses a few bills while she looks for work, so her score dips.

Now every application hits the score check. A lower score means no job; no job means more missed bills; more missed bills mean a lower score. The prediction “Rosa is risky” makes itself come true, and the record will later “prove” the model right. The book calls it a downward spiral of poisonous assumptions, hidden behind a camouflage of mathematical rigour and data.

In engineering terms, the score has become an input to the process that generates its own training data. No individual component is buggy. The loop is the bug.

On the job
  • Before reusing a score for a new purpose (credit score → hiring), ask whether the decision feeds back into the thing being scored.
  • A model validated on data its own decisions shaped will look more accurate than it is: the people it refused never got the chance to prove it wrong.

Try it: press Next month a few times and watch the score sink. Then break one link (make employers look at Rosa’s work instead of her score) and keep going.

What the book says

“When predictive analytics affect people’s lives, particularly pernicious problems arise because of self-reinforcing feedback loops. For example, consider the case of employers using credit scores to evaluate potential hires. You may be a good worker with a good credit score, but suddenly find yourself in financial difficulties due to a misfortune outside of your control. As you miss payments on your bills, your credit score suffers, and you will be less likely to find work. Joblessness pushes you toward poverty, which further worsens your score, making it even harder to find employment. It’s a downward spiral due to poisonous assumptions, hidden behind a camouflage of mathematical rigor and data.”

Feedback loops · algorithms that collude

Two pricing bots that never spoke to each other

Two petrol stations face each other across a road. Each runs a pricing algorithm that watches the other’s price and sets its own. With a simple undercut by 2 cents rule they fight, and prices fall to just above cost. That’s competition doing its job for drivers.

Now give each station an algorithm that learns what maximises profit by trial and error. A strategy such algorithms can discover goes like this: match your rival and nudge the price up; if the rival ever undercuts, punish with a few days of rock-bottom prices, then go back to climbing. Neither bot sends a message or signs an agreement. Each simply learned that undercutting gets punished. Prices drift up towards what a monopoly would charge.

This isn’t hypothetical. The book cites economists who found that when petrol stations in Germany introduced algorithmic pricing, competition fell and consumer prices rose, because the algorithms learned to collude. It’s a feedback loop between two systems, each one’s output being the other’s input.

On the job
  • When your system reacts to other automated systems (pricing, bidding, ranking, trading), simulate them together. Emergent behaviour lives between components.
  • “The model maximised the reward we gave it” is not a defence if the reward was “extract more from customers”.

Try it: with Undercut rule, press Run 40 days. Reset, switch to Learned policy, and run again. Halfway through, press Station B undercuts once and watch what happens to B.

What the book says

“As another example of a feedback loop, economists found that when gas stations in Germany introduced algorithmic prices, competition was reduced and prices for consumers went up because the algorithms learned to collude.”

Feedback loops · systems thinking

Draw the whole loop, people included

You can’t always predict when a feedback loop will appear. But many consequences can be predicted by thinking about the entire system: not only the computerised parts, but also the people interacting with them. That habit is called systems thinking.

A causal-loop diagram is a good tool for it. Arrows say “more of this causes more (+) or less (−) of that”. A cycle of + arrows is a reinforcing loop: it amplifies whatever differences it starts with, so the rich get richer and the poor get poorer. A cycle with one − arrow is a balancing loop: it pulls things back together. The book’s question for any data system is exactly that: does it reinforce and amplify existing differences between people, or does it try to fight injustice?

And don’t forget the arrows that run through people. Someone who is refused may stop applying altogether, or turn to a payday lender, and neither shows up in your service’s logs.

On the job
  • Sketch the loop on the whiteboard during design review: decision → behaviour → data → model → decision. Mark each arrow + or −.
  • If every loop is reinforcing, design in a balancing one (a correction, a cap, support for the people the system rejects) before launch.

Data you gave, data that was taken

Beyond using data to make decisions about people, the book asks about collecting it: what is the relationship between the organisation collecting data and the people it is about?

If a notes app stores only the notes you typed, because you want them stored, it is performing a service and you are the customer. The service no longer just does what you tell it; it develops interests of its own, which may conflict with yours.

Consent and freedom of choice · what the GDPR calls consent

Build a consent screen the law would accept

The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) turns “meaningful consent” into requirements you can check. Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous. The user must be able to refuse or withdraw it without detriment, or it doesn’t count as freely given. The request must be in an intelligible and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language. And silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity are not consent.

Consent isn’t the only lawful basis for processing personal data. Others include complying with other legislation and protecting somebody’s life, and the legitimate interest basis allows some uses, such as fraud prevention, which fraudsters would presumably not consent to. Still, consent is the basis internet services use most often, which is why the screen matters so much.

On the job
  • Store consent as data: per user, per purpose, with the exact text version shown, how it was given, and when it was withdrawn. Check it at processing time, not only at sign-up.
  • Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent, and has to propagate to every pipeline that relied on it.
  • Pick the lawful basis per purpose before you build the feature, not after the regulator asks.

Consent and freedom of choice · “just don’t use it”

“I agree” — but did you have a choice?

The last defence: if you don’t want to be tracked, don’t use the service. But that choice isn’t free either. Privacy and use of data · a decision right

Privacy means you choose who knows what

“Privacy is dead,” some people say, pointing at everything users post about their lives, mundane and deeply personal. The book calls that claim false: it rests on a misunderstanding of the word. Privacy doesn’t mean keeping everything secret. It means having the freedom to choose what to reveal to whom, what to make public, and what to keep secret.

The right to privacy is a decision right: each person decides where to sit on the spectrum between secrecy and transparency, situation by situation. Aiko has a rare illness. She’d gladly give her medical data to researchers if it might help find a treatment. She would be far more careful if the same facts could hurt her access to insurance or a job. Same fact, different audience, different answer, and the answer is hers.

When data is extracted through surveillance infrastructure, that right isn’t necessarily destroyed. It is transferred to the data collector. “Trust us to do the right thing with your data” means the company now decides what to reveal and what to keep secret, and it exercises that right to maximise profit. It keeps most of what it learns secret, because revealing it would seem creepy; intimate facts leak out only indirectly, for example as ad-targeting groups.

Because data is valuable, many people want it. Companies, of course; that’s why they collect it. But governments want it too, and may get it through secret deals, coercion, legal compulsion, or simply theft. When a company goes bankrupt, the personal data it collected is one of the assets that gets sold. And because data is hard to secure, breaches happen disconcertingly often.

So critics call data not just an asset but a toxic asset, or at least hazardous material. Maybe it isn’t the new gold or the new oil but the new uranium. Even if we think we can prevent abuse, every time we collect data we have to weigh the benefits against the risk of it falling into the wrong hands: criminals, hostile foreign intelligence services, leaking insiders, new management that doesn’t share our values, or a new regime with no qualms about compelling a handover.

For an engineer, the useful part is that the size of the spill is a design parameter. It is how much you collect × how long you keep it.

On the job
  • Your breach blast radius is your retention policy. Write it down per table, and enforce it with jobs, not good intentions.
  • In threat modelling, include “the company changes hands” and “a court or government demands it”, not only “an attacker gets in”.

Data as assets and power · civic hygiene

Design for every future government

When you decide what to collect, the book says, consider not only today’s political environment but all possible future governments. Nothing guarantees that every government elected in the future will respect human rights and civil liberties. Bruce Schneier puts it as a rule of hygiene: “It is poor civic hygiene to install technologies that could someday facilitate a police state.”

A religion column added in 2026 for a harmless reason (“personalised holiday greetings”) is, in a different decade, a ready-made list. And a list doesn’t need the column: location history that shows who visits which place of worship works almost as well.

“Knowledge is power”, and “to scrutinise others while avoiding scrutiny oneself is one of the most important forms of power”. That’s why totalitarian governments want surveillance: it gives them power to control the population. Today’s technology companies aren’t overtly seeking political power, but the data and knowledge they have accumulated, much of it surreptitiously and outside public oversight, still gives them a lot of power over our lives.

On the job
  • For every sensitive column, ask: if a hostile authority compelled this table in ten years, what list would it produce? If the answer scares you, don’t store it, or store it so you can’t read it back.
  • Derived signals (location, contacts, purchases) can rebuild a column you deliberately left out.

Remembering the Industrial Revolution · data as pollution

Dirty rivers then, leaky data now

Data is the defining feature of the information age, and the changes it brings to the economy and daily life invite comparison with the Industrial Revolution. That revolution brought sustained growth and, in the long run, much better living standards. It also brought dreadful air and water pollution, factory owners living in splendour while workers lived in cramped, unsanitary housing and worked long hours in harsh conditions, and child labour, including dangerous, poorly paid work in mines.

Safeguards took a long time: environmental protection, workplace safety, laws against child labour, health inspections for food. Doing business got more expensive once factories could no longer dump waste in rivers, sell tainted food or exploit workers. But society as a whole benefited hugely, and few of us would want to go back.

The book quotes Bruce Schneier: data is the pollution problem of the information age, and protecting privacy is the environmental challenge. Data stays around, festering. How we contain it and dispose of it is central to the health of the information economy. Our grandchildren will judge us the way we judge the factory owners who ignored pollution. We should try to make them proud.

On the job
  • Count data you keep the way an environmental engineer counts emissions: what goes in, what goes out, what stays, and for how long.
  • Safeguards are a cost of doing business. Budget for them like any other requirement, rather than treating them as a tax to minimise.

Legislation and self-regulation · purpose limitation

Every column has a reason, and every query states one

Data protection law can help preserve individuals’ rights. The GDPR says personal data must be “collected for specified, explicit and legitimate purposes and not further processed in a manner that is incompatible with those purposes”, and must be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary” for those purposes. The second part is called data minimization.

That principle runs straight against the philosophy of big data: collect as much as possible, combine it with other datasets, and experiment and explore to find new insights. Exploring means using data for unforeseen purposes, the opposite of the “specified and explicit” purposes it was collected for. The book’s verdict on how this has gone: the regulation has had some effect on online advertising, but it has been weakly enforced and hasn’t changed culture and practice much across the wider tech industry.

For engineers, purpose limitation is an access-control problem you can actually implement: tag columns with the purposes they were collected for, make each query declare its purpose, and refuse the mismatch.

On the job
  • Record a purpose per column (or per dataset) in the schema registry, next to its type and owner.
  • Make jobs declare a purpose (a session variable, a service identity, a ticket ID) and enforce it in the query layer, not in a wiki page.
  • “Just in case” columns fail minimization by definition. Don’t create them.

Try it: in Big data mode press Run the week’s queries. Then switch to GDPR mode and run them again. Notice what is no longer collected, and which query gets refused.

Legislation and self-regulation · the trade-off

Lives saved by sharing, lives harmed by leaking

Companies that collect lots of personal data broadly oppose regulation as a burden and a hindrance to innovation. The book grants that this is partly justified. Sharing medical data creates clear privacy risks, but also real opportunities: how many deaths could be prevented if data analysis led to better diagnostics or treatments? Overregulation may prevent such breakthroughs. Balancing the opportunities against the risks is genuinely difficult.

There isn’t one “right” setting of the slider, but two things become clear when you play with it. First, the trade-off isn’t a single line: safeguards such as consent, purpose limits and audits shift the whole curve, getting most of the research value for much less harm. Second, safeguards only count if they are enforced. Rules on paper with weak enforcement sit right on top of “no rules at all”, which is roughly the book’s assessment of the GDPR so far.

On the job
  • When someone says “privacy blocks innovation”, ask which safeguard, and what the design would be that keeps the value and drops the risk.
  • Prefer controls the system enforces (access policies, audit logs, expiry) over controls that rely on people remembering a policy.

Legislation and self-regulation · a culture shift

Privacy is a national park

Law alone won’t do it. The book asks for a culture shift in the tech industry: stop regarding users as metrics to be optimised, and remember they are humans who deserve respect, dignity and agency. Self-regulate data collection and processing to earn and keep the trust of the people who depend on our software. Educate users about how their data is used, rather than keeping them in the dark. Let each individual keep their privacy, meaning control over their own data, instead of stealing that control through surveillance.

The book’s image: our individual right to control our data is like the natural environment of a national park. If we don’t explicitly protect and care for it, it will be destroyed: the tragedy of the commons, and we’ll all be worse off. Each company that extracts a bit more gains a bit more, while everyone shares the cost of the trust it burns. But the book ends this thought hopefully: ubiquitous surveillance is not inevitable. We are still able to stop it.

On the job
  • Put a human outcome next to every engagement metric on the dashboard: “goals completed”, “regretted sessions”, “complaints about data use”.
  • Industry norms start as one team’s defaults. Ship the respectful default and document why.

Legislation and self-regulation · a first step

Data you don’t have can’t be leaked

The book’s practical first step is short. Don’t retain data forever: purge it as soon as it’s no longer needed. And minimise what you collect in the first place. Its reason is the sharpest sentence in the chapter: “Data you don’t have is data that can’t be leaked, stolen, or compelled by governments to be handed over.”

For engineers, the hard part of “purge” is that data doesn’t live in one place. The previous chapters built a world of derived data: backups, a warehouse fed by change data capture, a search index, a training snapshot for a model. A retention job that deletes from the primary database and forgets the rest leaves the data exactly as leakable as before. Deletion has to flow through the dataflow just like writes do.

Beyond any single technique, the book says culture and attitudes will have to change, and it ends the argument with a line aimed squarely at its readers: as people working in technology, if we don’t consider the societal impact of our work, we’re not doing our job.

On the job
  • Give every table a retention period and a scheduled job that enforces it; alert when the job doesn’t run.
  • Propagate deletions to derived datasets (as events in the log, if you followed Chapter 13), and let backups expire.
  • Where you need history, keep aggregates and drop the raw per-person rows. For data you can’t easily find in every copy, a common practice is to encrypt each person’s data with their own key and delete the key (crypto-shredding).

What to carry with you

The chapter in twelve habits, one per idea.

Data is about people

Every system has intended and unintended consequences. Look at it through the eyes of everyone it touches, users or not, over years, not just launch day.

Ethics is a loop

Not a checklist: reflect, talk with the people affected, decide, ship, observe, and have a named owner answer for the results.

Count the cost of “no”

“If in doubt, say no” is rational for one organisation. Many of them leaning on one score build an algorithmic prison with no proof and little appeal.

The past is a biased teacher

Models infer rules from history, copy its bias, apply it consistently and amplify it. Dropping a column doesn’t help when proxies remain. A fairer future takes moral imagination.

Build for accountability

Decision records, versions, reasons, owners, appeals. Nobody gets to say “the algorithm decided”. And remember: right on average is still wrong about individuals.

Look for loops

Recommenders narrow, scores fulfil themselves, pricing bots collude. Draw the whole system, people included, and add a balancing loop on purpose.

Ask who the customer is

Data the user gave serves the user. Data tracked as a side effect for advertisers is surveillance; swap the word and see.

Joins reveal more than rows

Harmless signals combine into illness, debt and beliefs. “Nothing to hide” depends on who you are, and who is in power.

Consent must be real

Freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, refusable and withdrawable without detriment. Network effects and dark patterns make most “agreements” anything but.

Privacy is a decision right

It’s choosing what to reveal to whom. Surveillance transfers that right to the company; ad targeting and internal use exercise it for profit.

Data is hazardous material

Valuable, so wanted by thieves, insiders, buyers in bankruptcy and future governments. Design for all of them.

Collect less, keep it shorter

Purpose for every column, purge in every copy, a culture that treats users as humans, not metrics. Data you don’t have can’t be leaked.

Words to know

The chapter’s vocabulary, in plain language.

unintended consequence
An effect of your system that nobody designed, often on people outside the requirements.
ethics (as a process)
Participatory, iterative reflection with the people involved, with accountability for the results. Not a checklist.
predictive analytics
Using data to predict what a person will do: default, reoffend, claim, click.
algorithmic prison
Systematic exclusion from jobs, travel, insurance, housing and finance by automated “no”s, without proof or appeal.
inferred rules
Decision rules a model learns from data rather than ones a person wrote. Often opaque.
bias amplification
A model that learns a bias and applies it more consistently than its human teachers, widening the gap.
protected trait
A characteristic the law forbids treating people differently for: ethnicity, age, gender, sexuality, disability, beliefs.
proxy
A feature, such as postcode or IP address, that predicts a protected trait and lets bias back in.
moral imagination
Choosing a future better than the past. Data can’t supply it; only humans can.
accountability
A person answers for a decision, can explain it, and can fix it. “The algorithm did it” is not an answer.
decision record
What a system logs per automated decision: inputs, versions, reasons, owner, appeal route.
calibration
Predicted probabilities match observed rates across a group. Says nothing about any one person.
feedback loop
A system’s output becomes its own future input, so effects reinforce themselves.
echo chamber
A feed that shows people only what they already agree with, because that’s what gets clicks.
algorithmic collusion
Pricing algorithms that learn to keep prices high without any explicit agreement.
systems thinking
Analysing the whole system, people included, for reinforcing and balancing loops.
tracking
Logging user behaviour as a side effect, rather than storing what the user asked you to store.
surveillance
The book’s word for tracking that serves someone other than the person tracked.
derived dataset
Data combined from the user base, tracking and external sources; nobody can meaningfully consent to it.
shadow profile
Data about a non-user, built from other people’s uploads.
GDPR consent
Freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous; refusable and withdrawable without detriment; never pre-ticked.
lawful basis
The GDPR reason for processing: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, legitimate interest, and others.
network effect
A service is more valuable the more people use it, which makes opting out socially costly.
decision right
Privacy as the right to choose what to reveal to whom. Surveillance transfers it to the collector.
data exhaust
Calling behavioural data waste. The book argues it’s closer to unpaid labor.
data broker
A company that secretly buys, joins and resells personal data.
toxic asset
Data seen as hazardous material: valuable, and dangerous when it reaches the wrong hands.
civic hygiene
Not installing technology that could one day help a police state.
purpose limitation
Using data only for the specified, explicit purposes it was collected for.
data minimization
Collecting only what’s adequate, relevant and necessary, and purging the rest.
tragedy of the commons
A shared resource destroyed because each party gains by taking a bit more.
