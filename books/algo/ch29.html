29 · a political problem

The cheapest way to win

A candidate needs enough votes in three areas and wants to spend as little as possible on advertising.

You are running for office. You must win 50 thousand urban votes, 100 thousand suburban votes and 25 thousand rural votes. You have four issues to advertise. Each $1,000 spent on an issue wins votes in some areas, and loses them in others.

By trial and error you find a plan that works, and it costs $33,000. Is that the cheapest? You can't try every plan, because there are endlessly many. So you write the problem as math instead, in three moves. Name the numbers you get to choose: those are the decision variables. Write each goal as a limit on them: those are the constraints. Name the number you want to shrink: that is the objective function.

All three together make a linear program. Turning a messy problem into this form is called modeling, and it is the main skill this chapter teaches.

Read the maths
−2x₁ + 8x₂ + 0x₃ + 10x₄ ≥ 50
x₁ … x₄
Thousands of dollars spent on zombies, sharks, highways and dolphins. You choose these.
−2, 8, 0, 10
Thousands of urban votes won per $1,000 on each issue. A minus sign means votes lost.
≥ 50
The goal: at least 50 thousand urban votes.

With numbers: the book's plan is x₁ = 20, x₂ = 0, x₃ = 4, x₄ = 9. Then (−2)(20) + 8(0) + 0(4) + 10(9) = −40 + 90 = 50. That is exactly enough.

Try it: press Next to spend the book's $33,000 one issue at a time. Then choose Best plan to see what the solver finds, or Your plan and drag the four sliders until all three bars pass their goals. Can you get close to the best price?

What the book says

The district has urban, suburban and rural areas with 100,000, 200,000 and 50,000 registered voters. The goal is at least half of each. The issues are preparing for a zombie apocalypse, equipping sharks with lasers, building highways for flying cars, and allowing dolphins to vote. Figure 29.1 gives the thousands of voters won or lost per $1,000 of advertising.

The book's trial plan spends $20,000, $0, $4,000 and $9,000, which wins 50, 100 and 82 thousand votes for 33 thousand dollars. It notes that the model gives more rural votes than there are rural voters. The linear program is (29.6)–(29.10): minimize x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + x₄ subject to the three vote limits and x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄ ≥ 0.

29.1 · general linear programs

What counts as linear

Twice the choice must give exactly twice the effect, and every limit must allow its own edge.

In the campaign, spending $2,000 on an issue wins exactly twice the votes of $1,000. Spending on two issues adds up with no surprises. That steady behaviour is what linear means. A linear function takes each variable, multiplies it by a fixed number, and adds up the results. No squares, no roots, and no variables multiplied together.

Real adverts wear off, so the second $1,000 usually wins fewer votes than the first. A model with that curve is not linear, and this chapter's tools do not apply to it directly. Linear is a real restriction, and you choose to accept it.

Limits follow the same rule: a linear function on the left, then ≤, ≥ or =, then a number. A strict < is not allowed. The second mode shows why: under a strict limit, a best value might not exist.

Read the maths
f(x₁, …, xₙ) = a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aₙxₙ
x₁ … xₙ
The n numbers you choose.
a₁ … aₙ
Fixed numbers that come with the problem.

With numbers: take f = 2x₁ + 3x₂. At (1, 2), f = 2 + 6 = 8. At (2, 4), f = 4 + 12 = 16. Twice the choice, twice the result.

Why this restriction pays off: straight-line behaviour is what gives the flat cuts and the corners you will meet in a moment. It is also why the problem can be solved efficiently.

Try it: press Next and watch each formula go through the doubling test. Linear formulas stay on the dashed straight line. Then choose ≤ against < to see a limit with no best value.

What the book says

Given real numbers a₁, …, aₙ and variables x₁, …, xₙ, a linear function is f(x₁, …, xₙ) = Σ aⱼxⱼ. If b is a real number, then f = b is a linear equality, and f ≤ b and f ≥ b are linear inequalities. Linear constraints means either kind.

“Linear programming does not allow strict inequalities.” A linear-programming problem is the problem of minimizing or maximizing a linear function subject to a finite set of linear constraints. It is a minimization or a maximization linear program accordingly.

The doubling test, the wearing-off adverts and the reason given for excluding strict limits are this page's illustrations. The book states the rule without giving a reason, and it gives no pseudocode here.

29.1 · general linear programs, standard form

One shape for every problem

Small rewrites turn any linear program into one fixed shape, and they never change the answer.

Real problems arrive in many shapes. Some minimize, some maximize. Some limits say ≥ or =. Some variables may go negative. Writing a solver for every combination would be silly. So we agree on one shape, and rewrite everything into it.

That shape is standard form: maximize, with limits that all say ≤, and variables that are all at least 0. Those last rules, one per variable, are the nonnegativity constraints. Each rewrite below is one small trick. To minimize a number, maximize its negative. An = limit is a ≤ and a ≥ at once. Multiply a ≥ by −1 and it turns into a ≤.

Read the maths
maximize cᵀx, subject to Ax ≤ b and x ≥ 0
x
The list of n variables you choose.
c
The n numbers in the objective. cᵀx means multiply them with x, pair by pair, and add.
A
A table of the numbers on the left of the limits: one row per limit, one column per variable.
b
The right side of each of the m limits.

With numbers: in the book's exercise 29.1-2 the plan (7, 3, 0) costs 2·7 + 7·3 + 1·0 = 35. In the rewritten form, the matching values give −35 to maximize. Same plan, flipped sign.

Why the answer can't change: every rewrite matches the plans of the old program one-for-one with plans of the new one, at the same value (or its negative). The machine checks this. It solves the program again after every step.

Easy to mix up: a negative variable is not the same as a negative right side. A right side like −7 is fine in standard form. Only the variables themselves must be at least 0.

Try it: press Next and watch one rewrite per step. The box on the right runs a real solver on the current program each time. Its plan never changes. Then switch to Exercise 29.1-1, which needs fewer steps.

What the book says

By convention, a maximization linear program takes n real numbers cⱼ, m real numbers bᵢ and mn real numbers aᵢⱼ. The goal is to find xⱼ that maximize Σ cⱼxⱼ subject to Σ aᵢⱼxⱼ ≤ bᵢ and xⱼ ≥ 0, (29.11)–(29.13). The compact form is (29.14)–(29.16). The book calls this the standard form. It calls expression (29.11) the objective function, the n + m inequalities the constraints, and the n constraints in (29.13) the nonnegativity constraints.

The book leaves the conversions as Exercises 29.1-6 and 29.1-7, and the two programs here are its Exercises 29.1-2 and 29.1-1. So the rewrites are the usual answers to those exercises, not text from the book, and the procedure below is ours.

A setting of the variables with a bar over it, such as x̄, is a feasible solution if it satisfies all the constraints, and an infeasible solution if not. Its objective value is cᵀx̄. A feasible solution with the largest objective value is an optimal solution.

One rewrite at a time
29.1 · a two-variable linear program

Every limit cuts the plane

Each limit throws away everything on one side of a line, and what survives all the limits is the set of allowed plans.

Suppose you have only two numbers to choose, x₁ and x₂. Then any plan is one point on a plane. The point (1, 2) means x₁ = 1 and x₂ = 2. So a question about plans becomes a question about points.

Take the limit 2x₁ + x₂ ≤ 10. The points where the two sides are exactly equal form a straight line. Everything on one side obeys the limit, and everything on the other side breaks it. So each limit is a cut. Lay all the cuts over each other, and one shape survives.

A plan that keeps every limit is a feasible solution. A plan that breaks one is infeasible. The surviving shape is the feasible region. Its corners have a name too: each is a vertex.

Read the maths
4x₁ − x₂ ≤ 8
the line
All the points where 4x₁ − x₂ is exactly 8, such as (2, 0) and (3, 4).
the side
The points where it is less than 8. Test one point to learn which side that is.

With numbers: at (1, 2), 4·1 − 2 = 2, which is at most 8, so (1, 2) survives. At (5, 2), 4·5 − 2 = 18, which is more than 8, so (5, 2) is cut away.

Why the shape has no dents: each cut keeps a flat side, and a flat side has no dents. Overlapping such pieces can't make one. So the segment between any two allowed points stays allowed. The word for this is convex, and the next two scenes depend on it.

Try it: press Next to apply the book's five limits and watch the test plans fall. Then choose Your limits. Switch limits off, drag their numbers, and click anywhere on the plane to test a plan of your own. Can you drag the numbers until nothing survives?

What the book says

The book's example is (29.17)–(29.21): maximize x₁ + x₂ subject to 4x₁ − x₂ ≤ 8, 2x₁ + x₂ ≤ 10, 5x₁ − 2x₂ ≥ −2, and x₁, x₂ ≥ 0. Figure 29.2(a) graphs each constraint as a line and a direction, with the feasible region highlighted.

A linear program with no feasible solutions is infeasible. If it has feasible solutions but no finite optimal objective value, it is unbounded, and its feasible region is unbounded too. A footnote gives the meaning of convex: for any two points in the region, all points on a line segment between them are also in the region.

29.1 · a two-variable linear program

Slide the line until it last touches

All plans with the same value lie on one straight line, so slide that line across the shape and stop where it last touches.

We want x₁ + x₂ as large as possible. The region holds endlessly many points, so we can't test them all. Instead, ask an easier question: which plans have value exactly 4? They form a straight line, x₁ + x₂ = 4. Every plan on it within the shape is a real plan worth 4. Pick a larger value and you get a parallel line, shifted further out.

So picture one line sliding across the shape. As long as it still crosses the shape, that value is reachable. At some moment it touches the shape for the last time. A straight edge meeting a shape with no dents can only last touch at a corner, or along a whole edge. If it is an edge, then that edge's two ends are corners with the same value. Either way, some corner is the best plan.

Read the maths
x₁ + x₂ = z
z
The value we are asking about. Each z gives one line.
objective value
What a plan's x₁ + x₂ comes to. It is the z of the line that plan lies on.

With numbers: the corner (2, 6) gives z = 2 + 6 = 8. The corner (3, 4) gives only 7. No point of the shape reaches 9, so z = 8 is the best.

Why this is a big deal: an endless search has shrunk to a short list. To solve this program you only need to compare corners. The machine's table does exactly that, and it agrees with the sliding line. In standard form, if a best value exists, an optimal solution is somewhere on that list.

Try it: press Next to slide the line. Switch to A tie to see the line land on a whole edge. Then choose Your tilt and drag the two numbers: the winning corner changes as the line tilts.

What the book says

The set of points for which x₁ + x₂ = z is a line with a slope of −1. Figure 29.2(b) shows the lines for 0, 4 and 8. The intersection of such a line with the feasible region is the set of feasible solutions with objective value z. The optimal solution is the vertex x₁ = 2, x₂ = 6, with objective value 8.

The book says it is no accident that an optimal solution occurs at a vertex. The intersection of the line of largest z with the boundary is either a single vertex or a line segment. If a segment, both endpoints are optimal, and each endpoint is a vertex.

The book warns that this graphical procedure does not immediately generalize to an efficient algorithm for larger problems. The tie objective 2x₁ + x₂ is this page's example. The procedure below, trying every pair of limits, is also ours; the book gives no pseudocode.

29.1 · algorithms for linear programs

From corner to better corner

With many variables you can't list every corner, so walk along the edges, always to a better corner, until none is better.

With three variables, each limit is a flat cut through space, and the region is a solid with flat faces. With n variables you can't draw it, but the same facts hold. Each limit keeps a half-space. The book calls the resulting region a simplex. The best plan is still at a vertex. But the number of vertices can be enormous, so comparing them all is hopeless.

The simplex algorithm only compares neighbors. It stands on a vertex, looks along each edge, and moves to a neighboring vertex that is no worse, and usually better. When every neighbor is worse, it stops. In practice this is usually fast. On carefully built inputs it can take exponential time.

Two other families exist. The ellipsoid algorithm was the first to run in polynomial time, but it is slow in practice. The interior-point methods travel through the middle of the region and end at a vertex. For large inputs they can be as fast as simplex, and sometimes faster.

Why stopping at a local best is safe: suppose a better plan existed somewhere else. The straight segment to it stays inside the region, because the region has no dents. The value changes steadily along a straight segment, so it would rise from your first step. Then some neighboring corner would also be better. So if no neighbor is better, nothing anywhere is better.

Easy to mix up: the word simplex names both the shape and the algorithm. Also, a step only has to leave the value no smaller: it can stay the same, though it usually rises.

Try it: press Next to walk. Compare the routes under Best neighbor and Other neighbor: they differ, but they finish in the same place. Drag start corner to begin somewhere else. Then watch Through the middle.

What the book says

The book does not give a linear-programming algorithm in detail. It says all of them are too complicated to show, and it discusses a few ideas. The simplex algorithm starts at some vertex of the simplex and moves along an edge to a neighboring vertex whose objective value is no smaller than that of the current vertex (and usually is larger). It terminates at a local maximum, a vertex from which all neighboring vertices have a smaller objective value, and because the region is convex and the objective linear, this local optimum is global.

Ellipsoid algorithms and interior-point algorithms solve linear programs in polynomial time. The simplex algorithm does not in the worst case, but tends to perform well in practice. Interior-point methods move through the interior: intermediate solutions are feasible but not necessarily vertices, and the final solution is a vertex.

From the chapter notes: G. Dantzig invented the simplex algorithm in 1947. L. G. Khachian gave the ellipsoid algorithm in 1979. Karmarkar's paper describes the first interior-point algorithm. V. Klee and G. J. Minty built an example on which simplex takes 2n − 1 iterations. Spielman and Teng introduced smoothed analysis and applied it to simplex.

The two neighbor rules and the barrier method in Through the middle are this page's illustrations. The book names no such rules, and the barrier path shows the idea of moving through the interior, not any specific algorithm from the literature. The final step to the corner is simple rounding here.

29.1 · integer linear programs

When answers must be whole numbers

Asking for whole numbers sounds like a small change, but it turns a problem we can solve quickly into one nobody knows how to.

You can't hire 2.4 people or drill 3.7 wells. So suppose every variable must be a whole number. That is an integer linear program. It looks like the same problem with one more rule.

It isn't. The corners were the whole trick, and now the best corner may not even be a whole-number point. You might think of rounding it. But the rounded point can fall outside the shape, and the true best whole point can be far away. This shape's tip points up, while the best whole point lies at the far right along the floor.

Just finding any whole-number point that keeps the limits is NP-hard. So no polynomial-time algorithm is known, while plain linear programming has one.

What survives: every whole-number plan is also a plan of the ordinary program, so the ordinary maximum is a ceiling on the whole-number maximum. Here that is 5.10 above 5.

Try it: press Next to try rounding, then check every whole point. Then drag weight of x₂ to tilt the objective. For some tilts, rounding down happens to work, and for others it misses by far.

What the book says

If you add the requirement that all variables take on integer values, you have an integer linear program. Exercise 34.5-3 asks you to show that just finding a feasible solution is NP-hard. Since no polynomial-time algorithms are known for any NP-hard problems, there is no known polynomial-time algorithm for integer linear programming. A general linear-programming problem can be solved in polynomial time.

Problem 29-3 asks you to show that weak duality holds for an integer linear program, that duality does not always hold, and that IP ≤ P = D ≤ ID, where these are the optimal values of the integer primal, the primal, the dual and the integer dual.

The shape and the objective here are this page's toy example, not the book's. The check of every whole point is only workable because the example is tiny.

29.2 · formulating problems, shortest paths

Shortest paths, by maximizing

To find the shortest route, stretch every distance as far as the roads allow: the tightest chain of roads is the answer.

Airlines schedule crews with linear programs, and oil companies choose where to drill. The real skill is spotting that your problem fits. Here is a problem you already know, in the new form. We want the length of the shortest path from s to t. Give each vertex a number: d(v) is how far v is from s.

Every road gives one limit: you can reach v by going to u first and then taking the road, so d(v) can be at most d(u) + the road's length. Also d(s) = 0. That is the whole program, apart from one surprise: it maximizes d(t).

Picture beads on strings. Pin bead s at 0. Each road is a string from u to v, as long as the road. Now pull bead t away as far as it can go. The strings stop it. The chain that goes tight is the shortest path, because the shortest chain of strings is the one that stops t first.

Read the maths
d(v) ≤ d(u) + w(u, v)
d(v)
The number chosen for vertex v. The book writes it d with a small v.
w(u, v)
The length of the road from u to v. It comes with the problem.

With numbers: the road a→b has length 1, so d(b) ≤ d(a) + 1. Once d(a) = 2, that means d(b) can be at most 3.

Easy to mix up: the word shortest feels like minimize, but minimizing fails. With every d at 0, all road limits hold, so the program learns nothing. Without a d ≥ 0 rule it is worse: d(t) can sink forever, since the limits only push down.

Try it: press Next. Each road's limit lights up against its road. Then the numbers get pushed up until a chain goes tight. Drag length of a→b and the chain moves to another route. Then choose Minimize to see the mistake.

What the book says

The book opens this section with two applications: an airline scheduling flight crews and an oil company choosing where to drill. It says perhaps the most important aspect of linear programming is being able to recognize when you can formulate a problem as a linear program. It also notes that an efficient algorithm designed for a problem, such as Dijkstra's, will often be more efficient than linear programming.

Linear programs use subscripts: dv rather than v.d, and fuv rather than (u, v).f. The single-pair program is (29.22)–(29.24): maximize dt subject to dv ≤ du + w(u, v) for each edge, and ds = 0. It has |V| variables and |E| + 1 constraints.

The book explains the surprise: minimizing would be a mistake, because when all edge weights are nonnegative, setting every d to 0 would yield an optimal solution without solving the shortest-paths problem. Taken literally the program has no d ≥ 0 rule, and then this page's solver reports minimizing as unbounded; with that rule added, it gives the book's all-zero answer. The page shows both.

The graph is this page's toy example, the beads are our picture, and PUSH-UP is our illustration of what maximizing does. The book gives only the program. The picture needs nonnegative lengths and every vertex reachable from s.

29.2 · formulating problems, maximum flow

Maximum flow, written as limits

Each pipe gets a number, each number has a ceiling, and what enters a junction must leave it: then maximize what leaves the source.

Chapter 24 solved this with its own special algorithm. A linear program needs no new ideas at all. Let f(u, v) be the flow along the edge from u to v. Those are the variables. Two kinds of limit then say everything a flow must obey.

First, an edge can't carry more than its capacity. Second, at every vertex except s and t, what comes in must go out. The objective is the total leaving the source, minus any flow coming back into it.

Read the maths
Σ f(v, u) = Σ f(u, v)
Σ
Add up over all v.
left side
All flow coming into vertex u.
right side
All flow going out of vertex u.

With numbers: at vertex a with the solved flow, 3 units enter along s→a. Then 1 leaves along a→b and 2 along a→t, and 1 + 2 = 3.

How you know it is the maximum: at the end the machine colours a cut: a wall of edges that all s→t flow must cross. The flow equals the wall's total capacity, so no flow can exceed it. Remember that wall. Section 29.3 shows it is a special case of something much larger.

Try it: press Next to light up each limit against its edge. At the end, drag capacity of a→t. The answer and the wall both change.

What the book says

The maximum-flow program is (29.25)–(29.28): maximize Σfsv − Σfvs subject to fuv ≤ c(u, v) for each u, v, flow conservation at each u other than s and t, and fuv ≥ 0. It assumes c(u, v) = 0 if (u, v) is not an edge, and no antiparallel edges.

This program has |V|² variables, one for each pair of vertices, and 2|V|² + |V| − 2 constraints. The book notes that it is more efficient to solve a smaller program, and Exercise 29.2-4 asks you to rewrite it with O(V + E) constraints. This page writes only one variable per edge, which is that smaller form.

29.2 · formulating problems, minimum-cost flow

Flow with a price tag

Now each edge charges per unit: send the required amount as cheaply as possible, by changing a few lines of the same program.

So far the solver has only repeated what special algorithms already do, and they are usually faster. The real power of linear programming is on new problems. The campaign was one. Here is another. Shipping along an edge now costs money: each unit on edge (u, v) costs a(u, v). You must send exactly d units from s to t. What is the cheapest way?

This is the minimum-cost flow problem. Notice how little changes. The capacity and conservation limits stay exactly as they were. You add one limit, saying the flow's value is exactly d, and you swap the objective for the total cost.

Read the maths
minimize Σ a(u, v) · f(u, v)
a(u, v)
The cost of sending one unit along edge (u, v).
f(u, v)
The units you send along it. You choose this.
Σ
Add the cost of every edge.

With numbers: the book's answer sends 2, 2, 1, 1 and 3 units along edges costing 2, 5, 3, 7 and 1. So the total is 2·2 + 5·2 + 3·1 + 7·1 + 1·3 = 27.

Try it: press Next to build the program and solve it for 4 units, the book's Figure 29.3. Then choose Most expensive: same limits, opposite objective. Then drag demand d up to 6, which the network can't carry.

What the book says

The book says an efficient algorithm designed for a problem will often beat linear programming, and that “the real power of linear programming comes from the ability to solve new problems”. In the minimum-cost-flow problem each edge has a capacity c(u, v) and a real-valued cost a(u, v). Sending fuv units costs a(u, v)·fuv, and the goal is to send d units from s to t at minimum total cost.

Figure 29.3 sends 4 units at a total cost of (2·2) + (5·2) + (3·1) + (7·1) + (1·3) = 27. There are polynomial-time algorithms specifically designed for this problem, beyond the scope of the book. 29.2 · formulating problems, multicommodity flow

Two products, one road network

When several products share the same trucks, the only question is whether all of them fit, and a linear program can answer it.

The Lucky Puck company from Chapter 24 starts selling hockey sticks and helmets. Sticks go from Vancouver to Saskatoon. Helmets go from Edmonton to Regina. They share the same trucks, with the same daily capacities. Each kind of item is a commodity, with its own source, sink and demand. This is a multicommodity flow problem.

Each commodity gets its own set of flow variables, and each obeys its own conservation limits. The one new limit ties them together: on every edge, the aggregate flow, all commodities added up, must stay within the capacity.

There is nothing to maximize. The question is yes or no: does any such flow exist? So the objective is just to minimize 0. Any feasible plan is as good as any other, and a solver answers by finding one or reporting there are none.

Read the maths
Σ fⁱ(u, v) ≤ c(u, v)
fⁱ(u, v)
How much of commodity i goes along edge (u, v). The i marks which product. It is not a power.
Σ
Add over all the commodities.

With numbers: if 6 crates of sticks and 6 of helmets both use Edmonton→Saskatoon, then 6 + 6 = 12, exactly its capacity. One more crate would not fit.

Try it: press Next through the case that fits. Then choose Too much, where a few more sticks break it. Under Your demands, drag the sliders and watch your dot move on the map of possible demands at the right.

What the book says

The Lucky Puck company diversifies into hockey sticks and hockey helmets. Sticks are manufactured in Vancouver and needed in Saskatoon. Helmets are manufactured in Edmonton and must be shipped to Regina. The capacity of the shipping network does not change, and the commodities must share the network.

There are k commodities, each a triple Ki = (si, ti, di). The aggregate flow on each edge is the sum of the commodity flows, and it must be no more than the capacity. The problem has no objective function, so the program has a “null” objective: minimize 0.

29.3 · duality

A proof you can check in ten seconds

A plan shows what is possible. A small sum of the limits shows what is impossible. Together they prove an answer is the best.

A friend says the largest x₁ + x₂ in our shape is 8, at the plan (2, 6). Half of that is easy to check: 2 + 6 = 8, and the plan keeps the limits. So 8 is possible. But how would you know that 9 isn't? The shape holds endlessly many plans, and you can't try them all. Running a solver again only moves your trust to the solver. So how can you know for sure?

Your friend hands you a slip of paper. It says: take 7/9 of limit B, and add 1/9 of limit C. You do the arithmetic. Out comes x₁ + x₂ ≤ 8. That sum is true for every allowed plan, because limits added together are still true. You didn't search. You checked one short sum, and now 9 is impossible.

That slip is a certificate. It doesn't help you find the answer. It proves the answer to anyone, even someone who trusts nobody. You saw the same pattern with maximum flow: a flow of 5, and a wall that allows only 5. The rest of this chapter shows that such a slip always exists.

Read the maths
a·A + b·B + c·C gives a ceiling on x₁ + x₂
a, b, c
Weights you choose. They must be at least 0, since a negative weight turns a limit upside down.
the test
In the sum, the number on x₁ must be at least 1, and so must the number on x₂. Then x₁ + x₂ can't exceed the sum's left side, because x₁ and x₂ are never negative.

With numbers: 7/9 of (2x₁ + x₂ ≤ 10) plus 1/9 of (−5x₁ + 2x₂ ≤ 2) gives (14 − 5)/9 x₁ + (7 + 2)/9 x₂ ≤ (70 + 2)/9. That is 1x₁ + 1x₂ ≤ 8.

Why the sum is safe: if each limit is true, then multiplying it by a non-negative number keeps it true, and adding true inequalities gives a true inequality. So every allowed plan obeys the sum.

Try it: press Next and watch slips of paper push the ceiling down, with one that fails the test. Then choose Write your own and drag the three weights. Can you find the slip that reaches 8?

29.3 · the dual linear program

Finding the best slip is its own linear program

Choosing the weights for the lowest ceiling is a minimum problem, and you can write it down by reading the original table sideways.

Each slip of paper is a list of weights. Weights must not be negative. The sum must cover the objective. And we want the lowest ceiling. Read that again: some numbers to choose, limits on them, and one number to make small. That is a linear program.

It has a name, the dual. The original is then called the primal. Writing the dual takes no thought at all. Lay the primal out as a table. Reading across each row gives a limit of the primal. Reading down each column gives a limit of the dual.

29.3 · weak duality

Why every ceiling sits above every floor

Value a plan's output at the dual's prices: it can't be worth more than what it used, and that can't exceed everything there is.

Any legal weights give a ceiling. That is weak duality: any allowed plan's value is at most any allowed slip's value. Here is why, with a picture you can hold in your head. Read the primal as a workshop. Each xⱼ is how much of product j you make. Each limit is a stock of one material. Then read each weight yᵢ as a price per unit of material i.

The dual's limit says: at these prices, the materials in one unit of product j cost at least what that product earns. So making your plan earns no more than the worth of the materials it used. And it can't use more material than exists. So what you earn is at most the worth of the whole stock. That total is the ceiling.

Two steps, and each can only lose. That is the whole proof. The workshop is our picture and not the book's. It fits this standard-form program and its proof exactly, step for step.

29.3 · linear-programming duality

Floor and ceiling always meet

Better plans raise the floor and better weights lower the ceiling, and they always meet at one number, the best value.

Each plan gives a floor on the best value, and each list of weights gives a ceiling. If a floor ever touches a ceiling, you are finished: nothing can rise above that ceiling, and nothing can sink below that floor. So both are optimal. That is the book's Corollary 29.2.

But does that touching always happen, or might a gap remain forever? The big theorem says it always happens, for any program that has a best value. So a proof like the slip of paper always exists, not just in lucky cases.

The book proves it with Farkas's lemma, the other mode here. A set of ≤ limits either has a solution, or some weighted sum of them says something absurd, such as 0 ≤ −6. Exactly one of those is true. So even the claim that there is no solution can be proved with a short sum.

Read the maths
cᵀx* = bᵀy*
x*, y*
A best plan and best weights.
cᵀx*
The value of the best plan: the highest floor.
bᵀy*
The value of the best weights: the lowest ceiling.

With numbers: x* = (8.25, 0, 1.5) gives 3·8.25 + 4·1.5 = 30.75. And y* = (0, 5/8, 7/16) gives 24·5/8 + 36·7/16 = 15 + 15.75 = 30.75.

Why they can't stop short: suppose the lowest ceiling were z, but no plan reached z. Then the limits, together with the limit value ≥ z, would have no solution. By Farkas's lemma a weighted sum would then prove it, and that sum works out to a ceiling even lower than z. That contradicts z being the lowest.

29.3 · fundamental theorem of linear programming

Only three things can happen

A linear program has a best value, or it has no allowed plans, or its value improves forever: there is no fourth ending.

A solver can't just return a number. It must also say when there is no answer. Happily, the list of possible endings is short. Either the program has a best plan with a finite value. Or it is infeasible: no plan keeps all the limits. Or it is unbounded: plans exist, and their value keeps improving without end.

That is all. There is no case where the value creeps toward 10 forever without reaching it. That is the case the strict < limit would have allowed, and the rule against strict limits is part of why it can't happen.

Easy to mix up: an open region does not mean an unbounded program. The region can run on forever while the objective only gets worse in that direction. The last mode shows one: its region is open, but it still has a best corner.

Try it: switch between the four modes and press Next in each. The verdict comes from a real solver, and the picture shows why.

problem 29-2 · complementary slackness

The gap is made of waste

The gap between ceiling and floor is the waste: priced stock left unused, plus products made at a loss. At the optimum there is none.

Weak duality had two steps, and each could lose something. How much? Exactly this much. In step two, you lose the price of any stock left unused. In step one, you lose wherever a product's materials cost more than it earns.

Add those losses and you get the gap between ceiling and floor, to the last decimal. So the gap is zero only if each loss is zero. Which gives a test you can run on any pair, called complementary slackness: every limit is either used up or has price 0, and every product is either not made or exactly pays for its materials.

slack

What the materials of product j cost at prices ȳ, minus what it earns.

The chapter on one page

The key ideas, in order.

Three ingredients

Decision variables, linear limits, and one linear number to make as large or as small as possible. If your problem fits, you have a linear program.

The feasible region

Each limit keeps one side of a line. What survives all of them is a shape with no dents: the plans you are allowed to use.

The best plan is at a corner

Slide the objective's line across the shape. It last touches at a corner, or along an edge whose two ends are corners.

Standard form

Maximize, only ≤ limits, only variables ≥ 0. A few small rewrites turn any linear program into this shape, and they leave the answer alone.

Simplex and friends

Simplex climbs from corner to better corner, fast in practice but exponential in the worst case. Ellipsoid and interior-point methods run in polynomial time.

Whole numbers change everything

Demand whole-number answers and the problem becomes NP-hard, and the best whole point can be far from the best corner.

Formulating is the skill

Pick the variables, write the limits, name the objective. Shortest paths, flows, and problems with no special algorithm all fit.

Duality is a proof

Add up the limits with non-negative weights and you get a ceiling on the answer. The best ceiling equals the best answer, so a matching pair proves both are optimal.

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

linear program
A problem of making one linear function as large or as small as possible, subject to a finite list of linear limits.
decision variable
A number you get to choose. In the campaign, how many thousand dollars go to one issue.
constraint
A limit on the values the variables can take, written as ≤, ≥ or =.
objective function
The one number you want to make as large or as small as possible.
modeling
Turning a problem into a mathematical form that an algorithm can solve.
linear function
Each variable times a fixed number, all added up. No squares, and no variables multiplied together.
standard form
Maximize, with only ≤ limits and only variables that are at least 0. Written compactly as maximize cᵀx subject to Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0.
nonnegativity constraint
A limit saying one variable must be at least 0.
feasible solution
A choice of values that keeps every limit.
infeasible
A solution is infeasible if it breaks a limit. A whole program is infeasible if no choice of values keeps all of them.
feasible region
The set of all feasible solutions. With two variables, a shape on the plane.
objective value
What the objective function comes to for one particular solution.
optimal solution
A feasible solution whose objective value is the best of all feasible solutions.
unbounded
A program that has feasible solutions but no best one, because the objective can keep improving forever.
convex
A shape with no dents: the straight segment between any two of its points stays inside it.
vertex
A corner of the feasible region.
half-space
Everything on one side of a flat cut. It is what one limit keeps, in any number of dimensions.
simplex
The book's name for the feasible region made by all the half-spaces, in any number of dimensions.
simplex algorithm
A method that walks from a vertex along edges to neighboring vertices that are no worse, until no neighbor is better. Exponential in the worst case, usually fast.
ellipsoid algorithm
The first polynomial-time method for linear programming. It runs slowly in practice.
interior-point method
A polynomial-time method that travels through the inside of the region rather than along its edges.
integer linear program
A linear program that also demands whole-number values. Just finding a feasible solution is NP-hard.
minimum-cost flow
Send a required amount from source to sink, keeping to the capacities, as cheaply as possible.
commodity
One kind of item being shipped, with its own source, sink and demand.
multicommodity flow
Several commodities share one network. The question is only whether all the demands can fit.
aggregate flow
The total of all commodities on one edge. It must stay within that edge's capacity.
certificate
Evidence that lets anyone check a claim quickly. Our word here, not the book's: a dual solution is a certificate that no plan beats a given value.
primal
The original linear program, when we are also talking about its dual.
dual
The linear program formed from the primal by swapping maximum for minimum, right sides for objective numbers, and rows for columns.
weak duality
Any feasible solution of the primal has a value no greater than any feasible solution of the dual.
Farkas's lemma
For a set of ≤ limits, exactly one thing is true: they have a solution, or some weighted sum of them says 0 is below a negative number.
complementary slackness
At the optimum, each limit is either used up or priced at 0, and each variable is either 0 or fully justified by its dual limit.
