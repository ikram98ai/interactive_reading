The chapter in five stops

Each stop asks the same question: how much does not knowing the future cost you?

  1. A way to keep scoreCompare your result with a seer who knows everything. The worst gap is your score. An elevator shows how.
  2. Keep a list in good orderFinding an item costs its position. Moving each found item to the front never costs more than 4 times what the seer pays.
  3. A savings account for the proofCount the pairs two lists disagree on. That count acts like money saved, and it pays for the expensive steps.
  4. A small, fast memoryA full cache must throw something out. Someone who knows your rule can make you miss every time, so a predictable rule scores about the size of the cache.
  5. Roll dice insteadPick at random and nobody can aim at the gap. For a cache of 1000 blocks, the score falls from about 1000 to about 15.
You only needDivision, and the idea of a worst case: the input that makes a plan look as bad as it can look. Later scenes use fractions as chances, like a 1 in 3 chance. Everything else is explained when it comes up.
27.1 · waiting for an elevator

Stairs or elevator?

You must choose before you know the future, so judge a plan by the worst it can ever do against someone who does know.

You walk into a building. Your meeting is 10 floors up. The stairs take one minute per floor, so 10 minutes, every time. The elevator rides up in one minute, but nothing tells you when it will arrive. Do you wait?

Imagine a friend who knows exactly when it will come: the seer. The seer picks whichever way is faster. You can't match that. What you can do is make sure you never fall too far behind. So we score a plan by dividing your time by the seer's time, and taking the worst case. That score is the competitive ratio. A score of 1 would be perfect. Lower is safer.

Always walking risks being 10 times slower when the elevator was right there. Always waiting risks being stuck for ages. The clever plan hedges: wait as long as the stairs would take, then walk.

Read the maths
ratio = largest value of (your time ÷ seer's time)
m
Minutes until the elevator arrives. You don't know it. The seer does.
k
Floors to climb, which is also the stairs time in minutes.
B
The longest the whole elevator trip can ever take. So m is at most B − 1.

With numbers: take k = 10 and B = 300. Always stairs scores 10. Always elevator scores 300 ÷ 10 = 30. Wait, then walk scores 2.

Why hedging scores 2: if the elevator comes while you wait, you ride it, just like the seer would (or within a minute of the seer). If it doesn't, you spent k minutes waiting and k climbing, while the seer spent k climbing. That is exactly double, and it can never get worse.

Try it: pick a plan at the top, then press Next. Each step tries a different arrival time, races you against the seer, and drops a dot on the chart. The last step names your worst case. Then drag B to 300, the book's example: always elevator now scores 30, while hedging stays at 2 whatever you do.

What the book says

You can climb the stairs at one floor per minute, and the elevator ascends all k floors in one minute. The elevator takes at most B − 1 minutes to arrive, for some B considerably higher than k, and the arrival time m is an integer. So taking the elevator costs from one minute up to B minutes.

An optimal seer takes t(m) = m + 1 minutes if m ≤ k − 1, and k minutes if m ≥ k. For a minimization problem, the competitive ratio of an online algorithm A is the maximum of A(I)/F(I) over all inputs I, where F knows the future. An algorithm with ratio c is c-competitive, and the ratio is always at least 1.

“Always take the stairs” has competitive ratio k, reached when the elevator arrives immediately. “Always take the elevator” has ratio B/k, reached when it takes B − 1 minutes. With k = 10 and B = 300 the book prefers the stairs, 10 against 30, because the stairs guard better against the worst-case future.

The hedging strategy waits k minutes and then climbs, so h(m) = m + 1 if m ≤ k, and 2k if m > k. Its competitive ratio is 2, independent of k and B. The book states these as formulas rather than as numbered pseudocode; the listing below writes the same formulas as procedures.

27.2 · maintaining a search list

Move what you just found to the front

Finding an item costs its place in the list, so keep wanted items near the front, without knowing which ones those are.

Here is a list of five items: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. To find an item you start at the front and walk along. Item 5 sits in position 5, so finding it costs 5. You may also reorder the list, but only by swapping two neighbours, and each swap costs 1.

The plan called move-to-front has one rule: when you find an item, swap it forward until it comes first. It can't see what will be asked for next, so it bets on what was asked for last.

The seer here is called FORESEE. It knows every request ahead of time, so it pays for a move only when that saves money later. On the book's four requests the seer pays 13 and move-to-front pays 26. That is twice as much, and the book proves it can never pass 4 times, on any requests at all.

Read the maths
cost of move-to-front = r + (r − 1) = 2r − 1
r
The position of the item when it is asked for. Finding it costs r.
r − 1
The swaps that bring it to the front. Each costs 1.

With numbers: in the list 5, 3, 12, 4, 8, 9, 22, item 8 is in position 5. Finding it costs 5 and the 4 swaps cost 4, so 2 × 5 − 1 = 9. The list becomes 8, 5, 3, 12, 4, 9, 22.

The seer can lose a round. It has the lowest total, not the lowest cost at every request. Pick Seer pays early to see it pay 5 where move-to-front pays 1, then win that back.

Try it: press Next to walk through the book's example, and check it against the table in the book. Then pick Always the last, the worst case for move-to-front: it pays 9 on every request. Or write your own requests with the number buttons, and watch the ratio stay under 4.

What the book says

The list L holds n elements, and the position of element xi is rL(xi). Dropping the asymptotic notation, searching for the ith element costs i. The only way to reorder is to swap two adjacent elements, at a cost of 1 each. The goal is to minimize the total cost of the searches plus the total number of swaps. The problem often arises for hash tables with chaining, since each slot holds a linked list.

With no knowledge of the requests, every search could be for the tail of the list, for a total of Θ(nm) over m searches. So the book compares a reorganization scheme with an optimal offline algorithm instead: if a sequence is fundamentally hard, the offline algorithm finds it hard as well.

MOVE-TO-FRONT(L, x) searches for x and then swaps it with each element before it, for a cost of 2rL(x) − 1. FORESEE knows the future: “after each call it optimally rearranges the list for the future”, and there may be more than one optimal order. The two procedures keep different lists of the same elements.

Figure 27.1 starts from ⟨1, 2, 3, 4, 5⟩ and searches for 5, 3, 4, 4. FORESEE moves 4 to the front after searching for 3, paying 3 swaps, for a cumulative cost of 13. MOVE-TO-FRONT reaches 26. It costs more at each step of this example, “but that is not necessarily always the case”. The book gives no numbered pseudocode here; the listing below writes its description as procedures, and this machine finds the seer's moves by trying every order of the list at every step.

27.2 · counting disagreements

Pairs two lists disagree on

Compare two lists of the same items by counting the pairs they put in opposite order.

Take two lists: 5, 3, 1, 4, 2 and 3, 1, 2, 4, 5. Pick two items, say 1 and 5. The first list has 5 ahead of 1. The second has 1 ahead of 5. They disagree about that pair. Now pick 1 and 3: both lists put 3 first, so they agree.

A pair the lists disagree on is an inversion. The number of such pairs is the inversion count. Here 5 of the 10 pairs disagree, so the count is 5.

There is a picture for this. Tie a thread from each item in one list to the same item in the other. Two threads cross exactly when the lists disagree on that pair. So the count is the number of crossings. When several threads meet at one point, the dot there shows how many crossings it stands for.

Read the maths
pairs among n items = n × (n − 1) ÷ 2
n
How many items are in the list.

With numbers: 5 items make 5 × 4 ÷ 2 = 10 pairs, so the count can be anything from 0 (same order) to 10 (exactly reversed).

Why one swap moves the count by exactly 1: swapping two neighbours changes the order of those two and nothing else. Every other item stays ahead of both or behind both. So exactly one pair flips, and it goes from agree to disagree, or the other way.

Try it: in Count the pairs, press Next to check the 10 pairs one at a time. Then pick Swap neighbours and press the swap buttons in any order. A crossing slides up to the top list and vanishes, or slides down out of it. The count never jumps by more than 1.

What the book says

An inversion is a pair of elements a and b in which a appears before b in one list, but b appears before a in another. For lists L and L′, the inversion count I(L, L′) is the number of pairs whose order differs in the two lists.

With L = ⟨5, 3, 1, 4, 2⟩ and L′ = ⟨3, 1, 2, 4, 5⟩, exactly five of the 10 pairs are inversions: (1, 5), (2, 4), (2, 5), (3, 5), (4, 5). So I(L, L′) = 5.

If two consecutive elements swap positions in a list L, then for any other list L′, the value of I(L, L′) either increases by 1 or decreases by 1. The threads are this page's own picture, not the book's. The listing writes the book's definition as procedures.

27.2 · why move-to-front is within 4 times

A savings account pays for the expensive steps

Cheap steps save up and expensive steps spend, so move-to-front's total can never pass 4 times the seer's.

Look at the third request in the book's example, item 4. Move-to-front finds it at position 5 and pays 9. The seer finds it first and pays 1. That one step is 9 times worse, yet the promise is 4 times. How can both be true?

Because the seer already paid earlier, and we can keep track of that. Give move-to-front an allowance: every time the seer pays 1, move-to-front receives 4. From that it pays its own bill. It must also keep 2 in a savings account for every crossing between the two lists. Anything still left goes in a jar marked spare. The book's name for this account is a potential function: a number worked out from the current state, which acts as money stored for later.

Before that third request, the two lists have 8 crossings, so the account holds 16. Four of those crossings join item 4 to the items ahead of it. Moving 4 to the front undoes them and releases 8. The allowance gives 4 and the account gives 8, which pays the bill of 9 and leaves 3 spare. If the money always covers the bills, then the bills add up to at most the total allowance, which is 4 times the seer's cost.

Read the maths
account = 2 × inversions
inversions
The crossings between move-to-front's list and the seer's list right now.
2
What one crossing can cost move-to-front: 1 to walk past the other item, and 1 to swap past it.

With numbers: after the seer's 3 swaps in the example, the lists have 8 crossings, so the account holds 2 × 8 = 16.

Read the maths
bill + change in account ≤ 4 × seer's cost
bill
What move-to-front actually pays for this request: 2r − 1.
change in account
Positive when crossings appear, negative when they vanish.
≤
“At most”. What is left over goes to spare, and it is never negative.

With numbers: at the third request the bill is 9 and the account falls by 8, so 9 − 8 = 1. The seer's cost is 1, and 4 × 1 = 4. Since 1 ≤ 4, it is covered, with 3 spare.

Why the allowance always covers it: take any item ahead of x in move-to-front's list. If it is ahead in the seer's list too, the seer also walked past it, which earned 4: that pays 2 for walking and swapping, and 2 for the new crossing. If it is behind in the seer's list, that is a crossing the move undoes, and its 2 pays the walk and the swap. Each seer swap earns 4 and changes one crossing, worth 2, so it can't run short either.

Where the savings picture breaks. A real account holds money someone chose to put in. Here nobody saves anything, and neither list knows the account exists: it is only our way of adding up. The balance is not a choice either. It is always exactly 2 × crossings, which is why it can't go below 0. And the spare jar is never used: it is the reason 4 is a ceiling, and real runs often land below it.

Try it: press Next and stop at request 3 of the book's example: the blue account shrinks to pay the amber bill. Then pick other requests or add your own. Watch the mint spare bar: it never shrinks, and that is the whole proof in one picture.

What the book says

Theorem 27.1: “Algorithm MOVE-TO-FRONT has a competitive ratio of 4.” The proof uses the potential function Φi = 2I(LiM, LiF), as in the amortized analysis of Chapter 16. The factor of 2 embodies the idea that each inversion represents a cost of 2 for MOVE-TO-FRONT relative to FORESEE: 1 for searching and 1 for swapping. The lists start the same, so Φ0 = 0, and Φi ≥ 0 for all i.

For a search for x, the book sorts the other elements into three sets, using the lists before the search. BB holds those before x in both lists, BA those before x in MOVE-TO-FRONT's list but after it in FORESEE's, and AB those after x in MOVE-TO-FRONT's list but before it in FORESEE's. So x's position is |BB| + |BA| + 1 in the first list and |BB| + |AB| + 1 in the second.

A swap past an element of BB raises the inversion count by 1, and a swap past an element of BA lowers it by 1, so the count changes by |BB| − |BA|. Each of FORESEE's ti swaps changes the potential by 2 at most. The amortized cost is then at most 4|BB| + 1 + 2ti, which is at most 4(|BB| + |AB| + 1 + ti) = 4ciF.

Because the potential starts at 0 and stays nonnegative, the total actual cost is at most the total amortized cost, so MOVE-TO-FRONT's total is at most 4 times FORESEE's. On a particular sequence, it “could cost much less than 4 times the optimal algorithm, perhaps even matching the optimal algorithm”. The allowance, account and spare are this page's words for the amortized bound, the potential and the slack. The listing writes one step of that bookkeeping as a procedure.

27.3.1 · deterministic caching algorithms

What do you throw out?

A small fast memory fills up, and each rule for making room is a different guess about the future.

Picture a desk with room for 3 books, and a library down the hall. A book on the desk costs nothing to use. Any other book means a trip to the library, and if the desk is full, one book must go back first. Which one?

A computer's cache is that desk: a small, fast memory with room for k blocks of data. A request for a block already there is a hit. Anything else is a miss, and when the cache is full, some block must be evicted. The goal is the fewest misses.

The book names four online rules. FIFO evicts the block that has been inside longest. LIFO evicts the newest one. LRU evicts the block whose last use is oldest. LFU evicts the block used the fewest times, with ties going to the one inside longest. The seer plays furthest-in-future: it evicts the block whose next request is furthest away, which is the best anyone can do.

Read the maths
LIFO's ratio on its trap = n ÷ (k + 1)
n
How many requests there are.
k
How many blocks the cache holds.
k + 1
The seer's misses on the trap: it fills the cache, then evicts once and never again.

With numbers: with k = 3 and n = 18, LIFO misses all 18 requests and the seer misses 4, so 18 ÷ 4 = 4.5. Twice the requests give twice the ratio. There is no limit, and that is what the book means by an unbounded ratio.

Why the trap catches LIFO: when the requests swing between two blocks, LIFO evicts the one that just came in, which is the very one asked for next. It never gets out of that loop. FIFO and LRU do escape: they get a ratio tied to k, however long the requests run.

A bad worst case can still win the day. On the book's requests LIFO misses 7 times, tying the seer and beating LRU's 9. The score doesn't ask which rule usually wins. As with the stairs, it asks how badly a rule can be caught out.

Try it: pick a rule on top and press Next. Under each slot is the fact that rule looks at. Then choose LIFO's trap under the picture with LIFO selected: every mark turns coral. Change k, or switch to LRU on the same trap, and compare the totals in the tiles.

What the book says

The input is a sequence of n memory requests for blocks b1, b2, …, bn, not necessarily distinct, and the cache holds up to k blocks and starts out empty. The algorithms differ only in which block they evict on a miss, and prefetching is not considered.

The policies are first-in, first-out (FIFO); last-in, first-out (LIFO); Least Recently Used (LRU), which evicts “the block whose last use is furthest in the past”; and Least Frequently Used (LFU), breaking ties by choosing the block that has been in the cache the longest. The optimal offline algorithm is furthest-in-future from Section 15.4.

Theorem 27.2: LIFO has a competitive ratio of Θ(n/k). On the requests 1, 2, …, k, k + 1, k, k + 1, …, it misses on every one of the n requests, while the optimal algorithm misses k + 1 times. Exercise 27.3-2 asks you to show that LFU has the same ratio, and Exercise 27.3-3 that FIFO has a competitive ratio of O(k).

The book calls such a ratio unbounded, because it grows with the input size, and prefers a ratio that does not. It states the policies in words; the listing writes them as procedures. The request list called LFU's trap is this page's own example, built for that exercise. LFU's counts here include every earlier request for a block, even from before it was last evicted, which the book leaves open.

27.3.1 · why LRU stays within k

Cut the requests into epochs

Cut the requests into stretches: the oldest-use rule misses at most once per slot in each, and overall the seer misses at least once per stretch.

Take the book's 18 requests and a cache of 3 blocks. Read from the left and keep a list of the different blocks you meet. As soon as a fourth different block turns up, draw a line before it and start again. You get four stretches: 1 2 1 5 | 4 4 1 2 4 2 | 3 4 5 | 2 2 1 2 2.

Each stretch is an epoch. Inside one, LRU can miss only on a block's first request. Once used, a block is among the 3 most recent different blocks for the rest of the epoch, so LRU won't evict it. That means at most 3 misses per epoch.

Now the seer. Start at the first request of an epoch, and stop at the first request of the next. That stretch asks for the first block and 3 other different blocks. Those 4 blocks can't all sit in a cache of 3, so even the seer misses at least once.

Read the maths
LRU's misses ÷ seer's misses ≤ (k × epochs) ÷ epochs = k
k × epochs
The most LRU can miss: k in each epoch.
epochs
The least the seer can miss: one per epoch.

With numbers: the book's requests have 4 epochs with k = 3. LRU misses 9, and 9 ≤ 3 × 4 = 12. The seer misses 7, and 7 ≥ 4.

Why this works without knowing what the seer did: the epochs depend only on the requests, not on anyone's cache. Each one forces a price on everyone, so the bound holds without tracing the seer's choices.

Try it: press Next and watch the epoch's list fill up until a new block starts a new band. The pips at the bottom never overflow k. At the last epoch, see the seer hit where the book's sentence says it must miss, and read the caption to see where it paid instead. Change k, or try Other requests.

What the book says

Theorem 27.3: LRU has a competitive ratio of O(k). Epoch 1 begins with the first request, and epoch i begins upon the (k + 1)st distinct request since the beginning of epoch i − 1. With k = 3, the requests (27.10) split into the four epochs of (27.11).

Only the first request for a block within an epoch can cause an LRU miss, so each epoch incurs at most k misses. The book says the first request in each epoch “must cause a cache miss, even for an optimal algorithm”, because k other blocks have been accessed since the last access to that block. So the optimal algorithm has at least one miss per epoch, and the ratio is at most k/1.

That sentence is stronger than the proof needs, and on the book's own example it does not hold for furthest-in-future: block 2, which opens epoch 4, is a hit, because the seer kept it. Its miss for that stretch comes earlier, at block 5. The counting survives, as the story above shows: the seer pays at least one miss between the first requests of two neighbouring epochs. This note is this page's reading, not the book's. The book gives no pseudocode for epochs, so the listing is written here.

27.3.1 · the lower bound

An opponent who asks for what you just threw out

If your rule can be predicted, an opponent can make every single request a miss.

Say the cache holds 3 blocks and only 4 blocks exist: 1, 2, 3, 4. Requests for 1, 2, 3 fill the cache. Then 4 arrives, and you must evict something. Call it block b. What comes next? A request for b, of course. You just threw it out, so it's a miss, and you must evict another block. The next request is for that one.

This opponent is the adversary. It knows your rule, so it can work out which block you will evict. With 4 blocks and 3 slots, exactly one block is ever outside, and it always asks for that one. So n requests cost you n misses, whichever rule you use. Your rule can be deterministic and as clever as you like: if its choice can be predicted, it can be attacked.

Now give the seer the same list. Only the one block outside can cause a miss, and the seer makes sure that is the block needed furthest off. The 2 old blocks it kept get asked for first, so its next miss is at least 3 requests away. The seer misses at most once every 3 requests.

Read the maths
competitive ratio ≥ n ÷ (k + n/k)
n
Your misses: every request, all n of them.
k
The seer's first misses, which fill the cache.
n/k
The seer's other misses: at most one every k requests.

With numbers: with k = 3 and n = 12, that is 12 ÷ (3 + 4) ≈ 1.71. With n = 300 it is 300 ÷ (3 + 100) ≈ 2.91. Longer runs climb toward k = 3. Once n is at least k × k, the ratio is at least k ÷ 2.

Why no fixed rule escapes: the attack uses only one fact, that the adversary can tell which block is out. Any rule that decides without chance gives that away, however it decides. To get a better score, a rule must stop being predictable.

Try it: pick a rule and press Next: the coral arrow shows the block outside turning into the next request. Then pick Your own picks and try to beat it by choosing each eviction yourself. You can't, because the adversary moves after you do.

What the book says

“Any deterministic online caching algorithm must have a competitive ratio of Ω(k).” The proof relies on an adversary who knows the online algorithm being used and can tailor future requests to cause more misses.

The cache has size k, and the possible blocks are 1, 2, …, k + 1. The first k requests are for 1, 2, …, k, and the next is for k + 1, which forces an eviction of some block b1. The adversary then requests b1, which forces an eviction of b2, and so on. The online algorithm incurs n misses over n requests.

Furthest-in-future evicts a block that will not be accessed during at least the next k requests, so after the first k misses it misses at most once every k requests, for at most k + n/k misses. The ratio is at least n/(k + n/k) = nk/(n + k2), which is at least k/2 for n ≥ k2 (Theorem 27.4). Exercise 27.3-5 extends this to algorithms that can look ahead at a constant number of requests.

The book calls these results “somewhat unsatisfying”: all of these competitive ratios are rather high, and it is determinism that the adversary is able to exploit. The listing writes the book's description as a procedure.

27.3.2 · randomized caching algorithms

Mark what you use, then roll dice

Protect the blocks used lately with a mark, and when one must go, choose at random among the rest.

The cache holds 3 blocks: 1, 2 and 5. All three have just been used, so each carries a mark. Now block 4 is requested. It is a miss, and every block is marked, so there is nothing to choose from. The rule says: wipe all the marks and start a new epoch.

Now any block may go, and a die picks which one: 1, 2 or 5, each with the same chance. Block 4 takes that slot and gets a mark. Each block requested from here on gets a mark too, and marked blocks are safe until the next wipe.

This is MARKING. Think of the mark as meaning “recently used”. Choosing with a die makes it RANDOMIZED-MARKING. If you drop the die and always take the first unmarked block, the rule is deterministic again, and the adversary from the last scene gets every request as a miss.

Why the marks help: a marked block can't be evicted until the epoch ends. So during an epoch, only a block's first request can miss. Repeat requests are always free.

MARKING is not LRU. LRU keeps a full order of who was used when. MARKING keeps one bit per block, which splits the blocks into just two groups: used this epoch, or not. Among the second group it makes no ranking at all, and that is what leaves room for the dice.

Try it: press Next and follow the marks. Lilac slots with a dot are safe. When all three are lilac and a miss comes, watch them wipe. Then press Roll again: the same requests, different dice, a different result. The tiles show the exact average over all possible rolls.

What the book says

The book starts from a deterministic algorithm, MARKING, “an approximation of LRU”, which keeps a 1-bit attribute mark for each block. On a hit, the block is marked. On a miss, if all blocks are marked they are all unmarked, then an arbitrary unmarked block is evicted, and the new block enters and is marked.

RANDOMIZED-MARKING chooses the block to evict uniformly at random among the unmarked blocks. A new epoch begins immediately after each time line 5 executes. An epoch starts with no marked blocks, and these epochs are the same as in the proof of Theorem 27.3.

Deterministic MARKING has a competitive ratio of Θ(k), since Theorem 27.4 provides the lower bound and Exercise 27.3-4 the upper one. While the cache still has an empty slot, the machine fills it and evicts nothing, as the book assumes when it says the cache starts out empty.

27.3.2 · what the adversary can see

Dice hide the gap

An opponent who can't see your dice can't tell which block is missing, so many of its attacks hit nothing.

You flip a fair coin 10 times. One onlooker watches every flip and knows exactly how many heads you got. Another knows only that you are flipping a fair coin. That one can say “about 5 heads”, but not the real count.

Adversaries come in the same two kinds. A nonoblivious adversary sees your dice as they land, so dice don't help you: it always knows which block is out. An oblivious adversary knows your algorithm but not the rolls. It has to fix its requests without seeing them. That is the adversary the book plays against.

Take a cache of 3. A new block has just evicted one of the 3 old blocks at random. Which? The adversary can only guess, and each guess is right 1 time in 3. It guesses, the guess is served, and the next guess is right 1 time in 2. Then the epoch is full, and a new block must miss. That is 1 + 1/3 + 1/2 misses, where a deterministic rule would pay all 3.

Read the maths
Hk = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/k
Hk
The harmonic number: add up one over each whole number from 1 to k.
k
How many blocks the cache holds.

With numbers: H3 = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 ≈ 1.83, against 3 for a predictable rule. H1000 ≈ 7.49, against 1000. Doubling a large k adds only about 0.7.

Why the dice work: a deterministic rule leaves one known block outside. Dice spread that knowledge thin: every unmarked block might be the missing one, so no request can be sure of a miss.

Dice don't help if they are visible. Pick Sees your dice: the bars collapse to one block at 100%, and every request misses again. Randomness works only because it stays hidden until after the requests are fixed.

Try it: press Next and watch the bars. The adversary asks for the tallest one. In Can't see your dice, the chance of a miss runs 1, 1/3, 1/2, over and over. Change k and see the same pattern stretch out.

What the book says

“An adversary who does not know the random choices is oblivious, and an adversary who knows the random choices is nonoblivious.” The book would prefer algorithms that hold up against a nonoblivious adversary, which is stronger, but such an adversary “typically can act as if the online algorithm is deterministic”. So the oblivious adversary is the one typically used.

With a fair coin flipped n times, a nonoblivious adversary knows how many heads came up. An oblivious one can reason only that the expected number of heads is n/2 and the variance is n/4.

The key to the improved ratio is that the adversary cannot always make a request for a block not in the cache, “since an oblivious adversary does not know which blocks are in the cache”. The book doesn't compute this adversary's best plan. Asking for the likeliest missing block, and the exact chances behind it, are worked out on this page by tracking every state the cache can be in. The listing writes it as procedures.

27.3.2 · the book's bound

From k down to about 2 ln k

Count the blocks new to each stretch: on average you miss a small multiple of that, and the seer at least half of it.

Take one epoch with a cache of 5. Some requests are for new blocks, which were not used in the last epoch and so can't be in the cache. A new block always misses. Say there are 2 of them, and they come first.

The rest are old blocks, in the cache when the epoch began. The two new blocks evicted two of them at random, so the first old block is gone with chance 2/5. Once it is served and marked, it is out of the draw. So the next old block's chance is 2/4, and the next is 2/3. Fewer blocks are left to share the same 2 evictions, so the chance climbs.

Add it all up: at most r × Hk expected misses per epoch, where r counts the new blocks. And the seer can't dodge those r: two neighbouring epochs ask for k + r different blocks, and only k fit. So it misses at least r times across the pair. A single epoch would prove nothing, because the seer may have loaded the right blocks early, but each epoch sits in two pairs. That is where the factor of 2 comes from.

What the book says

Theorem 27.5: RANDOMIZED-MARKING “has an expected competitive ratio of O(lg k)” for a cache of size k, against an oblivious adversary. The expected competitive ratio is c if E[A(I)] ≤ cF(I) for all inputs, where the expectation is taken over the random choices of A.

Lemma 27.6: a bag holds x + y balls: x − 1 blue, y white and 1 red. Choosing balls at random until m ≤ x of them are blue or red, the red ball is chosen with probability m/x.

Only the first request for a block in an epoch can miss, so each epoch is treated as k requests for distinct blocks: ri ≥ 1 new and k − ri old. Every new request misses. The jth old request misses with probability nij/(k − j + 1), where nij is the number of new requests before it, and that is at most ri/(k − j + 1). Summing gives E[Xi] ≤ riHk, so all the epochs expect at most Hk times the sum of the ri.

For the offline algorithm, epochs i − 1 and i together have k + ri distinct requests, so mi−1 + mi ≥ ri, and since m1 = r1, its total is at least half the sum of the ri. The ratio is at most 2Hk = 2 ln k + O(1) = O(lg k). The listing writes the sum for one epoch as a procedure. This machine puts the new blocks first, the worst order, where the bound on each old request is exact.

problem 27-1 · cow-path problem

Find the book you lost on the trail

When you don't know which way or how far, go back and forth and double your reach each time.

You are hiking a long trail and your book is gone. It could be behind you or ahead of you, and you have no idea how far. If it turns out to be x miles away, you would like to walk not much more than x. Walking one way forever fails: it may be the other way.

So hedge, as with the elevator. Walk 1 mile one way and come back. Walk 2 miles the other way and come back. Then 4 the first way, 8 the other, and so on. Each round trip is as long as everything before it, so no earlier walk gets large compared with the distance you are about to try.

The book sets this as a problem and gives no answer. This doubling walk and its number 9 are worked out on this page: they are one good answer, not the book's words.

problem 27-2 · online scheduling

Run tasks without knowing what comes next

Imagine a schedule that may pause tasks, then run the real tasks whole, in the order that schedule finishes them.

One computer, four tasks. Task A arrives at time 0 and needs 5 units of work. B arrives at 1 and needs 2. C arrives at 2 and needs 1. D arrives at 6 and needs 3. Once a task starts, it must run to the end. You want the finishing times to add up to as little as possible, and you learn about a task only when it arrives.

The obvious plan is to run the shortest task available whenever the computer is free. It fails: a long task that arrives alone gets started, and then every short task behind it waits. The book asks you to show that this plan has no constant ratio.

The fix uses an imaginary schedule that is allowed to pause. SRPT always runs the task with the least work left, pausing others as needed. It is easy to run online, and no schedule that runs tasks whole can have a lower total. Now run the real tasks whole, in the order SRPT finishes them. You can do that online too: put each task in a queue at the moment SRPT finishes it.

Three schedules on one clock

The chapter on one page

The key ideas, in order.

Score against a seer

The competitive ratio is your cost divided by the cost of someone who knows the future, in the worst case. It is never below 1, and lower is safer.

Hedge your bets

Waiting guards against an elevator that comes soon. Walking guards against one that takes forever. Doing the first and then the second scores 2, whatever k and B are.

Move it to the front

In a list, finding an item costs its position and each swap costs 1. Moving every found item to the front costs 2r − 1, and on the book's example 26 against the seer's 13. It is 4-competitive.

Count the disagreements

An inversion is a pair two lists put in opposite order. One swap of neighbours changes the count by exactly 1, so a swap can be priced.

Save for the expensive steps

Keep 2 per inversion in an account paid for by 4 per seer's coin. The account is never below 0 and always covers the bill, so move-to-front's total is at most 4 times the seer's.

Rules for a full cache

FIFO, LIFO, LRU and LFU differ only in what they evict. LRU and FIFO have ratio Θ(k). LIFO and LFU are unbounded: their ratio grows with the number of requests.

Epochs price everyone

Cut requests where a (k + 1)st different block appears. LRU misses at most k times an epoch and the seer at least once per epoch, so LRU is within k.

Predictable means attackable

An adversary who knows your rule always asks for the block you just threw out. Every deterministic rule then scores at least k/2 on long runs, Ω(k).

Hide your choice with dice

RANDOMIZED-MARKING protects marked blocks and picks a random unmarked block to evict. Against an adversary who can't see the dice, its expected ratio is at most 2Hk, which is O(lg k). An adversary who can see them gets every request as a miss.

Double, or pause in your head

Two problems end the chapter. Searching both ways and doubling keeps a lost book within a fixed multiple of its distance. Running tasks whole, in the order SRPT finishes them, is 2-competitive for total finishing time.

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

online algorithm
An algorithm that gets its input a piece at a time and must act on each piece before seeing the next.
offline algorithm
An algorithm that has its whole input before it starts.
seer
An imaginary competitor who knows the future and always makes the best choice. It sets the score to beat.
competitive ratio
Your cost divided by the seer's cost, on the input that makes that fraction largest. A score of 1 is perfect.
c-competitive
Never worse than c times the seer, on any input.
hedges
Doing a bit of both, so that neither bad future can hurt you too much.
move-to-front
The online rule for a list: after you find an item, swap it forward until it is first.
inversion
A pair of items that two lists put in opposite order.
inversion count
How many pairs two lists disagree on. Draw threads between the lists and it is the number of crossings.
potential function
A number worked out from the current state that acts as money stored for later. Here it is 2 × the inversion count.
cache
A small, fast memory that keeps a few blocks of data from a large, slow one.
block
One chunk of data, the unit a cache stores and fetches.
hit
A request for a block that is already in the cache.
miss
A request for a block that is not in the cache, so it must be fetched from the slow memory.
evict
To remove a block from a full cache to make room for another.
furthest-in-future
The seer's rule for caching: evict the block whose next request is furthest away. No rule misses fewer times.
unbounded
Said of a competitive ratio that grows with the number of requests, with no limit.
epoch
A stretch of requests that uses at most k different blocks. The next different block starts the next epoch.
adversary
An imaginary opponent who knows your algorithm and picks the requests that hurt it most.
MARKING
A caching rule that keeps one mark per block: a block used in this epoch is marked and safe, and only unmarked blocks may be evicted.
oblivious
Said of an adversary who knows your algorithm but can't see its random choices.
nonoblivious
Said of an adversary who sees your random choices as they are made.
harmonic number
Hk = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/k. It grows very slowly, about as fast as ln k.
new block
A block requested in this epoch but not in the last one, so it can't be in the cache. It always misses.
old block
A block in the cache when this epoch began. It may or may not still be there when requested.
expected
The average over all the ways the dice can land. An expected competitive ratio compares that average with the seer.
SRPT
Shortest remaining processing time: always run the task with the least work left, pausing others if needed.
deterministic
Making its choices by a fixed rule, with no chance involved, so the same requests always give the same choices.
