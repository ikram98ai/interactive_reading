The chapter in five stops

One goal: multiply two polynomials quickly. Every stop exists to serve it.

  1. The slow waySchool multiplication pairs every term with every term. Double the length, and the work goes up four times.
  2. A better formA polynomial can also be written as a few dots on its curve. Dots multiply one pair at a time, which is almost no work.
  3. The catchTurning a polynomial into dots, and dots back into a polynomial, is slow. Unless we choose very special points.
  4. The special pointsPoints evenly spaced on a circle. Squaring them makes half of them disappear, again and again. This is the one idea the whole chapter rests on.
  5. The FFTSplit, solve two half-size problems, combine. It runs forwards to make the dots and backwards to read them. It even fits in a circuit.

30 · polynomials, and the goal of this chapter

Why multiplying is slow

Adding two long polynomials is quick, but multiplying them pairs every term with every term, and that work explodes as they grow.

Take 6x³ + 7x² − 10x + 9. Underneath, it is a list of four numbers: 9, −10, 7, 6, one for each power of x. Those numbers are its coefficients, and the whole expression is a polynomial.

To add two polynomials, add matching numbers: one sum for each power. To multiply them, every term of one must meet every term of the other. Four terms by four terms is 16 products. A thousand terms by a thousand terms is a million.

This chapter cuts that million down to something like ten thousand. Everything on this page serves that goal.

Read the maths
A(x) = a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + … + aₙ₋₁xⁿ⁻¹

The coefficients. The little number says which power of x it goes with.
n
How many coefficients there are. The book calls it a degree-bound: any whole number bigger than the highest power used.

With numbers: for 6x³ + 7x² − 10x + 9, the highest power is 3, so n = 4, and a₀ = 9, a₁ = −10, a₂ = 7, a₃ = 6.

Read the maths
cⱼ = a₀bⱼ + a₁bⱼ₋₁ + … + aⱼb₀

With numbers: the answer's x term has powers adding to 1: 9 × 4 + (−10) × (−5) = 36 + 50 = 86.

What the book says

A polynomial in the variable x over a field represents a function A(x) as a formal sum of ajxj for j = 0 to n − 1. The coefficients and x are drawn from a field, typically the complex numbers. The polynomial has degree k if its highest nonzero coefficient is ak, and any integer strictly greater than the degree is a degree-bound.

Adding two polynomials of degree n the straightforward way takes Θ(n) time; multiplying them takes Θ(n²) time. The goal of the chapter is to reduce multiplication to Θ(n lg n) using the fast Fourier transform.

The book's example: A(x) = 6x³ + 7x² − 10x + 9 and B(x) = −2x³ + 4x − 5 have the sum 4x³ + 7x² − 6x + 4 and the product −12x⁶ − 14x⁵ + 44x⁴ − 20x³ − 75x² + 86x − 45. If A and B have degree-bound n, the product has degree-bound 2n − 1; because a degree-bound of k is also a degree-bound of k + 1, the book normally says 2n.

The resulting coefficient vector is also called the convolution of the input vectors, written c = a ⊗ b. The book states the method as equations (30.1) and (30.2); the listing below writes them as a procedure.

30.1 · coefficient representation

The list of coefficients

Writing a polynomial as its list of numbers makes finding one value quick and adding quick. Only multiplying is slow.

What is 6x³ + 7x² − 10x + 9 when x = 2? You could work out 2³ and 2², multiply each, and add. There is a neater way. Start with the top number, 6. Then repeat one move: times x, plus the next number.

That gives 6, then 19, then 28, then 65. Three multiplications, and no powers worked out at all. This is Horner's rule, and its cost grows in step with n.

Read the maths
A(x₀) = a₀ + x₀(a₁ + x₀(a₂ + … + x₀(aₙ₋₁)))
x₀
The value of x we are asking about.
( … )
Work from the innermost bracket outwards. Each bracket is one "times x₀, plus the next number".

With numbers: 9 + 2 × (−10 + 2 × (7 + 2 × 6)) = 9 + 2 × (−10 + 2 × 19) = 9 + 2 × 28 = 65.

Why it works: pull one x out of every term that has one, again and again, and the powers vanish into nested brackets. It is the same sum, rearranged so nothing is computed twice.

Try it: press Next to climb the ladder of steps and see the dot land on the curve. Drag x₀ and do it again. Then pick Add two to see why adding is the easy job.

What the book says

A coefficient representation of a polynomial of degree-bound n is the vector of coefficients a = (a0, a1, …, an−1). Matrix equations in the chapter treat vectors as column vectors.

Evaluating at a point x0 takes Θ(n) time with Horner's rule. Adding two polynomials takes Θ(n) time: cj = aj + bj. Multiplying with equations (30.1) and (30.2) takes Θ(n²) time, since each coefficient in a is multiplied by each coefficient in b.

The book gives Horner's rule as a formula in this chapter (and refers to Problem 2-3); the listing below writes it, and adding, as procedures.

30.1 · point-value representation

A curve as a few dots

A few dots pin a curve down exactly, so a list of dots can replace the list of coefficients and lose nothing.

Two dots fix one straight line: you can't draw a second line through both. The same holds further up. Three dots fix one curve of the form ax² + bx + c. In general, n dots with different x values fix exactly one polynomial with n coefficients.

So there is a second way to write a polynomial: list n dots on its curve. That list is its point-value form. Getting the dots from the coefficients means evaluating at n points. Going back, from dots to coefficients, is interpolation.

Read the maths
A(x) = Σₖ yₖ · Lₖ(x)
Lₖ(x)
A helper curve, built to equal 1 at dot k's x and 0 at every other dot's x. It is a product of factors (x − xj) ÷ (xk − xj).
yₖ
The height of dot k. It stretches the helper curve up to that height.
Σₖ
Add the result up for every dot.

With numbers: dots at x = −2, 0, 2. The helper for x = 0 is (x + 2)(x − 2) ÷ ((0 + 2)(0 − 2)). At x = 0 that is (2)(−2) ÷ (2)(−2) = 1. At x = 2 it is 0.

Why the helpers work: at any dot's x, only that dot's helper is awake and the others are exactly 0. So each dot sets its own height without disturbing the rest.

Try it: press Next and see how each new dot throws out curves, until only one fits. Move the three heights with the sliders. Then pick Back to the list and watch three helper curves add up to the answer.

What the book says

A point-value representation of a polynomial A(x) of degree-bound n is a set of n pairs (xk, yk) with all the xk distinct and yk = A(xk). A polynomial has many such representations, since any n distinct points can serve. With Horner's method, evaluating at n points takes Θ(n²) time.

Theorem 30.1 (uniqueness of an interpolating polynomial): for any n pairs with distinct xk, there is a unique polynomial of degree-bound n through them. The proof writes equation (30.3) as a matrix equation (30.4) with a Vandermonde matrix, whose determinant is nonzero when the xk are distinct, so it is invertible.

Solving those equations takes O(n³) time. Lagrange's formula (30.5) gives a faster algorithm, with interpolation in Θ(n²) time (Exercise 30.1-5). A footnote warns that interpolation is a notoriously tricky problem for numerical stability: small differences in the inputs or round-off errors can cause large differences in the result.

The book gives evaluation and Lagrange's formula as equations; the listing below writes them as procedures.

30.1 · multiplying in point form

Multiply dot by dot

When both polynomials are written as dots at the same places, multiplying means multiplying each pair of heights. One product per dot.

Say A(1) = 3 and B(1) = 0. What is the product polynomial at 1? It is 3 × 0 = 0, because the product of two polynomials at a point is the product of their heights at that point. Nothing else is involved. No grid. No pairing every term with every term.

This is the chapter's big surprise. With 1,000 terms, the school way needs 1,000,000 products. In point form, we need 2,000: one per dot. That is called multiplying pointwise.

There is one trap. The product has about twice as many coefficients as A or B, so it needs about twice as many dots to be pinned down. Start with 2n dots, not n.

Read the maths
C(xₖ) = A(xₖ) · B(xₖ)
xₖ
One of the agreed places. A and B must use the same ones.
C
The product polynomial. If A and B have n coefficients each, the book uses 2n for C's degree-bound, so we need 2n dots.

With numbers: A(x) = x + 2 and B(x) = 1 − x. At x = −1: A = 1, B = 2, so C(−1) = 2. The real product 2 − x − x² at −1 gives 2 + 1 − 1 = 2.

Try it: press Next and multiply one pair at a time. Each pair makes one new dot on the product's curve. Then pick Too few dots and watch the answer go wrong, with the true curve drawn beside it.

What the book says

The point-value form suits many operations. For addition, C(xk) = A(xk) + B(xk) at any point, in Θ(n) time, as long as A and B are evaluated at the same n points. For multiplication, C(xk) = A(xk)B(xk).

Multiplication differs in one key aspect: degree(C) = degree(A) + degree(B), so if A and B have degree-bound n, then C has degree-bound 2n. Multiplying n pairs by n pairs gives only n pairs, but 2n are necessary. So begin with extended representations of 2n point-value pairs each. Then the product takes Θ(n) time, much less than the Θ(n²) needed in coefficient form.

To evaluate a polynomial in point-value form at a new point, the simplest approach known is to convert to coefficient form first.

30.1 · fast multiplication of polynomials in coefficient form

The detour, and its catch

Turn both polynomials into dots, multiply the dots, turn the result back. It only pays if those two conversions are fast.

We start and end with coefficients, because that is what we are given and what we want. So the plan is a detour with four legs: make room, go down to dots, multiply the dots, come back up.

The middle leg is nearly free. The catch is the legs either side. With Horner's rule, finding 2n heights costs about 2n × 2n products. That is worse than the school way we wanted to avoid.

So everything hangs on one question. Can we choose the places so that going down and coming back are cheap? The book's answer: yes, with very special places. Finding out why those places work is the rest of this page.

Read the maths
Θ(n²) against Θ(n lg n)
Θ( … )
"Grows like". It ignores constant factors and looks only at how the work grows with n.
lg n
How many times you can halve n before reaching 1. lg 8 = 3 and lg 1024 = 10.

With numbers: for n = 1024, n² = 1,048,576 and n lg n = 1024 × 10 = 10,240.

Try it: press Next to walk the four legs while the bar adds up the cost. Compare Any points with Special points, and drag n. Look for the size where the detour starts to win.

What the book says

Any points can serve as evaluation points, but certain ones allow conversion between representations in only Θ(n lg n) time. If complex roots of unity are the evaluation points, then the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) evaluates and the inverse DFT interpolates, and the FFT does both in Θ(n lg n) time. Figure 30.1 draws this strategy.

The procedure, for n an exact power of 2 (add high-order zero coefficients if it isn't): (1) double the degree-bound by adding n high-order zero coefficients; (2) evaluate each polynomial with an FFT of order 2n; (3) pointwise multiply; (4) interpolate by applying the FFT to compute the inverse DFT. Steps 1 and 3 take Θ(n) time, and steps 2 and 4 take Θ(n lg n) time.

Theorem 30.2: two polynomials of degree-bound n, with input and output in coefficient form, can be multiplied in Θ(n lg n) time.

The bars in this machine are not from the book. They are a rough tally of multiplications: 2n per Horner or helper evaluation, and n lg 2n for one FFT of order 2n.

30.2 · which points should we pick?

Opposite points share their work

A point and its opposite need nearly the same sums, so we want points that stay in opposite pairs however often we square them.

Take A(x) = 3 + 2x + 5x² + x³. Split it into the even powers, 3 + 5x², and the odd powers, 2x + x³ = x(2 + x²). Both brackets use only x².

Now find A(2). The brackets give 23 and 6, so A(2) = 23 + 2 × 6 = 35. Next find A(−2). The square is the same, so the brackets are the same: 23 and 6. The only change is a minus sign: A(−2) = 23 − 2 × 6 = 11. Two answers for the work of one.

Those two brackets are polynomials half as long, asked about x². If the squared points also came in opposite pairs, we could do the same trick on them, then again and again. That is the wish. Ordinary numbers can't grant it: a square is never negative, so after one round the opposite pairs are gone.

Read the maths
A(x) = Aeven(x²) + x · Aodd(x²)
Aeven
A new, half-length polynomial made from the coefficients a₀, a₂, a₄, …
Aodd
The same, from a₁, a₃, a₅, …

With numbers: Aeven(y) = 3 + 5y and Aodd(y) = 2 + y. At x = 2 we have y = 4, so 23 + 2 × 6 = 35. At x = −2 we have y = 4 again, so 23 − 2 × 6 = 11.

Try it: press Next through One pair and drag x. Then pick Ordinary numbers and watch the pairs die after one round. The wished-for counts are in the bars.

What the book says

This scene is a bridge written for this page. The book does not present the argument in this order. It first defines the complex roots of unity and then uses the even and odd split in equation (30.9), A(x) = Aeven(x²) + xAodd(x²). The polynomial here and the points ±1 to ±4 are our own example.

Aeven contains all the even-indexed coefficients of A and Aodd contains all the odd-indexed coefficients, each with degree-bound n/2. So evaluating A at n points reduces to evaluating two half-size polynomials at the squares of those points, then combining with (30.9).

The book says the reason the subproblems are only half as large is the halving lemma, which guarantees that the squares consist of only n/2 distinct values.

warm-up · numbers that turn

The number whose square is negative

A complex number is a point on a flat map, and multiplying by a point on the circle turns things around the middle.

Picture the number line. Multiplying by −1 flips everything to the other side of 0: a half-turn. Do it twice and you are back, which is why (−1) × (−1) = 1.

So what would do half of a half-turn? A quarter-turn. It lifts you off the number line, to the spot one step straight up from 0. Give that spot a name: i. Two quarter-turns make a half-turn, so i × i = −1. That's all i means. It is the number with a negative square, the very thing the last scene asked for.

Now every spot on the flat map is a number, a complex number, written as how far right plus how far up: 0.71 + 0.71i is 0.71 right and 0.71 up.

Read the maths

Measured counterclockwise, from the point 1 on the right.

With numbers: 45° is 0.71 + 0.71i. Multiplying it by itself should give 90°, which is i. Check: (0.71 + 0.71i)(0.71 + 0.71i) = 0.5 + 0.5i + 0.5i + 0.5(i × i) = 0.5 + i − 0.5 = i.

Read the maths
(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i

How far right. They add together.
b, d
How far up. They add together, separately.

With numbers: (2 + i) + (−1 + 2i) = 1 + 3i. Think of walking one trip, then the other.

30.2 · complex roots of unity

Evenly spaced points on a circle

Mark evenly spaced points on a circle, starting at 1. Raise any of them to the number of points, and you get exactly 1.

You have met this set already, for 4 points: 1, i, −1, −i. Now use 8 marks, one-eighth of a turn apart. Call the first mark after 1 by the Greek letter ω ("omega"). Multiplying by ω moves any mark one place along, since angles add.

Multiply by ω eight times and you have gone one full turn: ω⁸ = 1. The same holds for every mark. So every mark is a number whose 8th power is 1. The book calls such a number a complex 8th root of unity. "Unity" just means 1. The ω we started with is the principal root.

Read the maths
ωₙᵏ = e^(2πik/n) = cos(2πk/n) + i sin(2πk/n)
ωₙ
The principal nth root: one nth of a turn from 1.
ωₙᵏ
That step, taken k times: mark number k.
2πk/n
The mark's angle. 2π is the usual way to write a full turn (360°), so this is k/n of a turn.
e^(iu)
The book's shorthand for the point at angle u on the circle: cos u right and sin u up.

With numbers: n = 8, k = 1. The angle is 1/8 of a turn, or 45°. So ω₈ = cos 45° + i sin 45° = 0.71 + 0.71i.

A common worry: high powers sound huge, but here they don't grow at all. Every mark is at distance 1 from the middle, so powers only turn. ω³⁰⁰ is still on the circle.

Try it: press Next to step around the circle, then drag n to change how many marks there are. Pick Two names to see why ω₈² and ω₄¹ are the same point.

What the book says

A complex nth root of unity is a complex number ω such that ωn = 1. There are exactly n of them: e2πik/n for k = 0, 1, …, n − 1, using eiu = cos(u) + i sin(u). They are equally spaced around the circle of unit radius centered at the origin (Figure 30.2).

ωn = e2πi/n is the principal nth root of unity, and all the others are powers of it. A footnote says many other authors define ωn with a minus sign in the exponent, especially for signal processing, and that the mathematics is substantially the same.

The roots form a group under multiplication with the same structure as the integers modulo n under addition: ωjωk = ω(j+k) mod n.

Lemma 30.3 (cancellation): ωdndk = ωnk for integers n > 0, k ≥ 0, d > 0. Corollary 30.4: for even n > 0, ωnn/2 = ω2 = −1.

30.2 · the halving lemma

Square them, and they collapse

Squaring doubles every angle, so opposite points land on one spot: eight points become four, then two, then one. This is why these points are chosen.

Squaring a point means multiplying it by itself, and multiplying adds angles. So squaring doubles the angle. The point at 45° goes to 90°.

Its opposite, at 225°, goes to 450°. That is a full turn plus 90°: the same spot. Opposite points always meet. They were half a turn apart, so after doubling they are a full turn apart, and a full turn is nothing.

So the 8 points land on just 4 spots, and those spots are evenly spaced again: they are the 4th roots of unity. Here is the wish from earlier, granted. The squared points come in opposite pairs, so the trick works again: 4 become 2, and 2 become 1. No set of 4 or more ordinary numbers can do this, because their squares are never negative.

Read the maths
(ωnk)² = ωn/2k  and  (ωnk+n/2)² = (ωnk)²
ωnk
Mark number k of the n marks.
ωn/2k
Mark number k on the half-size circle of n/2 marks. The first equation: squaring lands on a root of the half-size set.
k + n/2
The opposite mark, half a turn further along. The second equation: opposite marks have the same square.

With numbers: n = 8, k = 1. ω₈¹ is at 45°, and its square is at 90°, which is ω₄¹. The opposite mark ω₈⁵ is at 225°, and its square is at 450° = 90°. The same point.

Why this makes the FFT fast: each round, the polynomial gets half as long and the number of points to visit halves too. So each half really is the same problem at half the size. If the points did not collapse, every round would still need all 8 of them, and nothing would be saved.

Try it: press Next and follow the two coloured dots until they meet. Then watch all the rest fall in. Drag n to 16. Then pick Uneven points: the same doubling, but no pairs ever meet.

What the book says

Lemma 30.5 (halving lemma): if n > 0 is even, then the squares of the n complex nth roots of unity are the n/2 complex (n/2)th roots of unity.

The proof: by the cancellation lemma, (ωnk)² = ωn/2k for any nonnegative integer k. Squaring all the roots produces each (n/2)th root exactly twice, since ωnk+n/2 squared is ωn2k+n = ωn2k. So ωnk and ωnk+n/2 have the same square.

Corollary 30.4 gives the same result: ωnn/2 = −1 implies ωnk+n/2 = −ωnk, so the two are negatives of each other, with the same square.

“As we’ll see, the halving lemma is essential to the divide-and-conquer approach for converting between coefficient and point-value representations of polynomials”, since it guarantees that the recursive subproblems are only half as large. The uneven points in this machine are our own contrast, not the book's.

30.2 · the DFT

The DFT, the slow way

The list of a polynomial's values at those circle points has a name, the DFT. Adding each value up term by term is slow.

We have our points. Now put them to use. Evaluate the polynomial at all n roots of unity, and you get a list of n answers. That list is the DFT, the discrete Fourier transform. It is the point-value form from before, at these special points.

Each answer is a sum, and each term in it is an arrow. Its length is the coefficient. Its direction is a mark on the circle. Lay the arrows tip to tail: where you end up is the answer.

This is the slow way: n arrows for each of n answers. Next comes the fast way, which gets the same list.

Read the maths

yₖ
The value of the polynomial at mark number k.

One arrow: length aj, pointing at mark number k × j.

With numbers: a = (0, 1, 2, 3), n = 4, k = 1, and ω₄ = i. So y₁ = 0 + 1 × i + 2 × (−1) + 3 × (−i) = −2 − 2i.

Try it: press Next and lay each arrow. The table shows which mark each term points at. Change the numbers, then pick Size 8, where each step does a whole row at once and the count reaches 64.

What the book says

The goal is to evaluate a polynomial of degree-bound n at ωn0, ωn1, …, ωnn−1, given in coefficient form. Define yk = A(ωnk) = Σ ajωnkj, equation (30.8). The vector y is the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of the coefficient vector a, written y = DFTn(a).

The straightforward method takes Θ(n²) time. As a matrix product, y = Vna, where the (k, j) entry of the Vandermonde matrix is ωnkj. The exponents form a multiplication table for factors 0 to n − 1.

30.2 · the FFT

The FFT: split in two

Split the coefficients into even and odd positions, solve both at half the size, then combine. The collapsing points make this possible.

You have all the pieces now. Take the coefficients in even positions as one polynomial, and those in odd positions as another. Together they give the original back: A(x) = even part at x² + x × odd part at x².

We need this at all 8 marks. But the squares of those 8 marks are only the 4 marks of the half-size circle. So we solve each half at just 4 points: two copies of the same problem, half as big. That's the whole trick, and it can repeat until one coefficient is left, where the answer is that coefficient.

The final step uses one more fact. A mark and its opposite are negatives of each other: ω⁵ = −ω¹. So one solved pair gives two answers, with a plus and a minus. The multiplier ωk is called the twiddle factor, and this whole method is the FFT.

Read the maths
yk = ek + ωnk ok  and  yk+n/2 = ek − ωnk ok
eₖ
The even polynomial's value at the square of mark k.
oₖ
The odd polynomial's value at that same spot.
ωₙᵏ
Mark k itself: the twiddle factor.

With numbers: a = (0, 1, 2, 3), so even (0, 2) and odd (1, 3). At the 2 marks for n = 2, the even part gives (2, −2) and the odd part gives (4, −2). For k = 1: y₁ = −2 + i × (−2) = −2 − 2i, and y₃ = −2 − i × (−2) = −2 + 2i.

What the book says

The FFT takes advantage of the special properties of the complex roots of unity to compute DFTn(a) in Θ(n lg n) time, assuming n is an exact power of 2. It defines Aeven from the even-indexed coefficients and Aodd from the odd-indexed ones, each of degree-bound n/2, so that A(x) = Aeven(x²) + xAodd(x²), equation (30.9).

So evaluating A at the roots reduces to evaluating both polynomials at the squares (30.10) and then combining. By the halving lemma those squares are only the n/2 complex (n/2)th roots of unity, each occurring exactly twice. The problems have exactly the same form as the original but are half the size.

Lines 1–2 of the procedure are the base case, since the DFT of one element is the element itself. Lines 3, 4 and 12 keep ω = ωnk. Line 11 works because ωnk+(n/2) = −ωnk. A footnote warns that updating ω round by round can accumulate round-off errors, and suggests a precomputed table of the n/2 values if several FFTs run on inputs of the same size.

Because each factor ωnk appears in both its positive and negative forms, the book calls these factors twiddle factors. Excluding recursive calls, each invocation takes Θ(n) time, so T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(n lg n) by case 2 of the master theorem.

The same machine, backwards

To turn dots back into coefficients, run the same FFT with the circle walked the other way, then divide by how many points there are.

We still have to come back up from dots to coefficients. At these special points it takes almost no new thinking. Feed the dots into the FFT as if they were coefficients. Walk the circle clockwise instead of counterclockwise. At the end, divide everything by n.

Why does that work? Because going forwards and then backwards makes arrows that mostly cancel. Walk round the circle in equal steps, adding one arrow per step. The arrows close into a loop and you end where you started: the sum is 0. The one exception is when every arrow points the same way. Then the sum is n, which is why we divide by n.

Coefficients in, coefficients out
30 · the famous job: signal processing

A sound is a stack of pure tones

Feed the FFT a recording instead of coefficients, and it tells you how much of each pure tone is inside.

A chord on a piano is several notes at once, added into one wobbling line. The line tells you how strong the sound is at each moment. That is the time domain.

Run the FFT on those moments, and each answer tells you how much of one pure tone is in the chord. That is the frequency domain. Same sound, written differently: like coefficients and dots, it is two forms of one thing, with the FFT going between them.

This is why the FFT is everywhere. A long signal may be made mostly of a few strong tones. Keep those, drop the rest, and you need far fewer numbers to store nearly the same sound.

Read the maths

With numbers: if the only sound is 2 × a pure tone with 3 wobbles over 64 samples, then |y₃| = 64, and 64 × 2 ÷ 64 = 2: the strength we put in.

Why this works: at mark k, the samples are laid as arrows turning k steps each. A tone wobbling at that same rate keeps lining up, so its arrows add up. Any other tone keeps turning and its arrows cancel. That is the summation lemma again.

Try it: press Next and watch a messy wave become a few clean spikes. Change the strengths and the noise, and choose how many tones to keep. Try keeping only one: the wave gets much simpler.

What the book says

The most common use for Fourier transforms, and hence the FFT, is in signal processing. A signal is given in the time domain: as a function mapping time to amplitude. Fourier analysis expresses the signal as a weighted sum of phase-shifted sinusoids of varying frequencies, and the weights and phases characterize the signal in the frequency domain.

The book names compression of digital video and audio, including MP3 files, among everyday uses, but it does not explain how they work. This machine is our own toy with invented tones: keeping the strongest tones shows the general idea, not what MP3 does. Keeping one tone takes two entries of y, one at mark k and one at mark n − k.

The chapter notes add that Fourier analysis is not limited to 1-dimensional data and is widely used in image processing. Cooley and Tukey are widely credited with devising the FFT in the 1960s, though an article by Heideman, Johnson and Burrus traces its history as far back as C. F. Gauss in 1805. Frigo and Johnson developed FFTW, which takes Θ(n lg n) time for any problem size n, even a large prime.

The butterfly: one turn, two answers

The combine step needs its turned value twice, so compute it once and reuse it. That small piece is the FFT's building block.

Sound and video need the FFT at top speed, so it is often built as a hardware circuit. A circuit is made of small identical parts. The FFT's part comes from the combine step.

Lines 10 and 11 of the FFT both multiply by the twiddle factor. That product is the same in both lines, so do it once and store it as t. Then add t to the even value for one answer, and subtract it for the other. This little piece is called a butterfly, because of the crossing shape it makes when drawn.

What the book says

Many of the FFT's applications in signal processing require the utmost speed, so the FFT is often implemented as a circuit in hardware. Its divide-and-conquer structure gives a parallel circuit of depth Θ(lg n), where depth is the maximum number of computational elements between any output and any input that can reach it.

The for loop of lines 9–12 computes ωnkykodd twice per iteration. A good optimizing compiler evaluates this common subexpression once and stores it in a temporary variable: t = ωykodd, yk = ykeven + t, yk+(n/2) = ykeven − t. This is a butterfly operation.

30.3 · permuting the inputs

Mirror the bits

The inputs must enter the circuit in a scrambled order, and there is a neat rule: write each position in binary and read it backwards.

The recursive FFT keeps splitting its list into even and odd positions. A circuit can't shuffle on the fly, so all of that splitting has to happen up front. Which order do the inputs end up in, once every split is done?

Look at the splits in binary. Even or odd is the lowest bit. The first split sorts by the lowest bit, and the next split sorts by the next bit. So the lowest bit ends up deciding the most: it acts like the top bit of the final position. That is bit reversal: input a₃, at 011, goes to position 110, which is 6.

30.3 · the full FFT circuit

The whole circuit

Mirror the inputs, then push them through a few stages of butterflies. Every butterfly in a stage works at once, so the answer comes fast.

Now we can build the entire thing. The inputs enter in bit-reversed order. Then come lg n stages, and each stage holds n/2 butterflies. Stage 1 makes 2-element answers, stage 2 makes 4-element answers, and so on until one n-element answer leaves on the right.

No butterfly in a stage needs the result of another in that same stage, so they can all run at the same moment. The time a value takes to cross the circuit, its depth, is only lg n stages, even though the circuit holds about n lg n butterflies.

FFT₈, with values on the wires

The chapter on one page

The key ideas, in order.

Two ways to write it

A polynomial is a list of coefficients, or a list of dots on its curve. Enough dots fix exactly one polynomial, so neither list loses anything.

Dots multiply fast

In coefficient form, multiplying takes about n² products. In dot form it takes one product per dot, as long as you have 2n dots.

Opposites share work

Evaluating at x and at −x uses the same two half-size answers. So we want points that come in opposite pairs, and keep doing so after squaring.

Why these points

The nth roots of unity are evenly spaced on a circle. Squaring doubles angles, so opposite points meet, and n points become n/2 points of the same kind. Ordinary numbers can't do this.

Split, solve, combine

Split into even and odd coefficients, solve both at half the size, then combine with a twiddle factor. That is n work on each of lg n levels: n lg n in all.

Backwards is almost the same

Walk the circle the other way and divide by n. So reading the dots back into coefficients costs the same n lg n.

A circuit of butterflies

Mirror the bits of the input positions, then run lg n stages of n/2 butterflies each. All the butterflies in a stage can work at once.

Where the book's problems go next

Problem 30-1 multiplies in n to the power lg 3 with no complex numbers. 30-2 takes the DFT into many dimensions. 30-3 finds all derivatives at a point. 30-4 evaluates at n arbitrary points. 30-5 replaces complex numbers with arithmetic modulo a prime, so integer answers come out exact.

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

polynomial
A sum of powers of x, each with its own multiplier, such as 6x³ + 7x² − 10x + 9.
coefficient / coefficients
The multipliers in a polynomial. Listed in order, they are one way to write the polynomial down.
degree-bound
Any whole number bigger than the highest power used. A polynomial up to x³ has degree-bound 4, which is its number of coefficients.
convolution
The list of coefficients you get by multiplying two polynomials. Each entry adds up all the products whose powers add to that position.
Horner's rule
A way to work out a polynomial's value with one multiply and one add per coefficient: start at the highest, then repeat "times x, plus the next".
point-value form
A polynomial written as a list of pairs (x, y): dots on its curve. n dots with different x fix a polynomial of degree-bound n.
interpolation
Going back: from dots to the coefficients of the one polynomial that passes through them.
pointwise
One dot at a time: multiply the two values that belong to the same x.
complex number
A point on a flat map, written as (how far right) + (how far up) × i.
i
The point one step up from 0. Multiplying by it is a quarter-turn, so i × i = −1.
root of unity / roots of unity
A number that gives exactly 1 when raised to the nth power. There are n of them, evenly spaced around the circle of radius 1.
principal root
The first root after 1, going counterclockwise. It is written ωn, and every other root is a power of it.
halving lemma
Squaring all n of the nth roots of unity gives the n/2 roots of the half-size circle, each one twice. This is why the FFT is fast.
DFT
Discrete Fourier transform: the list of a polynomial's values at the nth roots of unity.
FFT
Fast Fourier transform: the split-in-two method that computes the DFT in about n lg n steps instead of n².
twiddle factor
The root ωk that the odd half is multiplied by when two halves are combined. It is used once with a plus and once with a minus.
butterfly
The combine step in one piece: multiply the odd value by the twiddle factor, then add it to and subtract it from the even value.
bit reversal
Writing a position in binary and reading it backwards. It gives the order the inputs must enter an FFT circuit.
time domain / frequency domain
Two ways to describe a signal: its strength at each moment, or how much of each pure tone it contains.
depth
The largest number of computing parts a value can pass through in a circuit. It measures how long the circuit takes.
