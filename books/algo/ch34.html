The chapter in six stops

Each stop adds one idea. Together they explain why thousands of different problems stand or fall together.

  1. Easy and hard look alikeShortest route: fast. Longest route: no fast method known. A tiny change in the question can make a huge difference in the work.
  2. Ask a yes-or-no questionThe theory only asks questions with a yes-or-no answer, and it measures time against the length of the written-down input.
  3. Checking is easier than findingFinding a route through every city seems hard. Checking a route someone hands you is quick. Problems that can be checked quickly form a class called NP.
  4. Translate one problem into anotherA reduction rewrites any question of one kind as a question of another kind, and keeps the answer the same. A fast method for the second now answers the first.
  5. The hardest in the classA problem is NP-complete if it is in NP and every problem in NP can be translated into it. Circuit satisfiability was the first one proved.
  6. A chain of translationsEach new translation adds a problem to the family. You will run seven of them on tiny examples, solve both sides, and watch the answers agree.
You only needA little programming, like a loop and an if. A graph here is just dots joined by lines. Powers such as 210 = 1024 come up. Everything else is explained when it comes up.

Two misreadings to drop now. NP does not mean not polynomial. The letters stand for nondeterministic polynomial time, and in this book NP means one plain thing: a yes-answer can be checked quickly. Every problem with a fast method is in NP too. NP-complete does not mean impossible. These problems can all be solved, as this page does in your browser on small examples. What is missing is a method that stays fast as the input grows. Nobody has found one, and nobody has proved there is none. Whether P equals NP is still an open question.

34 · easy and hard look alike

One small change, a huge jump in work

Many pairs of problems look almost the same, yet one has a fast method and the other has none that anyone knows.

A courier has a map of 15 street corners. What is the shortest trip from the depot to the last stop? A short program answers that in under 100 steps. Now her boss asks for the longest trip that never uses a corner twice. One word changed. The plain way to answer this one is to walk every possible trip, and on the same map that takes nearly 2,000 steps.

Add a few corners and the gap gets far wider. The book names three such pairs. Shortest against longest simple path. An Euler tour, which uses every line once, against a Hamiltonian cycle, which visits every dot once. And formulas whose groups have two variables against those with three.

In each pair, the second problem is NP-complete. You will meet the exact meaning soon. For now it means this: nobody has a fast method, and nobody has proved that none exists.

Why such a gap? A shortest trip to the end is made of shortest trips to the corners along the way, so each corner is worked out once. A longest trip has no such shortcut that anyone knows: the best way on depends on which corners you already used.

Try it: pick a pair at the top and press Next. First each problem is solved on a small example. Then the same kind of input grows, and the bars show the steps each method really took.

What the book says

The class of problems called NP-complete has a status that is unknown: no polynomial-time algorithm has yet been discovered for one, and no one has proved that none can exist. This P ≠ NP question was first posed in 1971.

Three pairs differ only slightly, yet one of each pair is solvable in polynomial time and the other is NP-complete. Shortest versus longest simple paths: single-source shortest paths take O(VE) time, while merely determining whether a graph contains a simple path with at least a given number of edges is NP-complete. Euler tour versus Hamiltonian cycle: an Euler tour of a strongly connected, directed graph traverses each edge exactly once and can be found in O(E) time, while determining whether a directed graph has a Hamiltonian cycle is NP-complete. 2-CNF versus 3-CNF satisfiability: there is a polynomial-time algorithm for 2-CNF formulas, while the 3-CNF problem is NP-complete.

The book's example of a 2-CNF formula is (x1 ∨ x2) ∧ (¬x1 ∨ x3) ∧ (¬x2 ∨ ¬x3), satisfied by x1 = 1, x2 = 0, x3 = 1. The growing grids, rings and step counts are this page's own. They count one plain method each. They do not show that no faster method exists, since that is exactly what nobody knows. The book gives no pseudocode here; the listing writes the plain methods as short procedures.

34.1 · polynomial time

The wall that grows faster than any computer

Work that grows like a fixed power of the input stays manageable. Work that doubles with each extra item soon outruns any computer.

Take a computer doing a billion steps each second. A method that needs n² steps handles 60 items in about 3.6 millionths of a second. A method that needs 2n steps handles the same 60 items in about 36 years. Add 10 more items and that becomes tens of thousands of years.

So the line is drawn here. A method whose steps are at most some fixed power of the input size runs in polynomial time, and the book treats problems with such a method as tractable. Anything growing faster than every power is superpolynomial, and a problem that needs that much is called intractable.

Is n¹⁰⁰ really fine? No. The book says so plainly. It draws the line here for three reasons. Few real problems need such a high power, and better methods tend to follow the first one. The class doesn't change between reasonable computer models. And feeding one polynomial method into another stays polynomial.

Read the maths
polynomial time: at most c · nk steps
n
The size of the input: how long it is when written down.
k
A fixed number. It must not grow when n grows.
c
A fixed multiplier, also not growing with n.

With numbers: at n = 30, n³ = 27,000 steps, and 230 = 1,073,741,824 steps. At n = 31, n³ grows by about a tenth, while 2n exactly doubles.

Try it: press Next to raise n by 10 and read the clock for each method. Then drag k up to 6. The polynomial starts out higher, but 2n still crosses it and leaves it behind.

What the book says

Problems solvable in polynomial time are generally regarded as tractable, for three reasons. First, although no reasonable person considers a problem requiring Θ(n100) time tractable, few practical problems need so high a degree, and once a first polynomial-time algorithm is discovered, more efficient ones often follow.

Second, for many reasonable models of computation, a problem solvable in polynomial time in one model is solvable in polynomial time in another: the serial random-access machine, abstract Turing machines, and parallel computers when the number of processors grows polynomially with the input size.

Third, polynomials are closed under addition, multiplication, and composition, so if the output of one polynomial-time algorithm is fed into another, the composite is polynomial.

The billion-steps-a-second clock is this page's own illustration, and the book states no such speed. The listing is also this page's own, since the book gives only the definitions.

34.1 · abstract problems

Ask for a yes or a no

The theory only asks yes-or-no questions. That loses little, because a yes-or-no version is never harder than finding the best answer.

You could ask a map app for the route with the fewest roads. Or you could ask: is there a route from a to g with at most 2 roads? The first wants a best value. It is an optimization problem. The second only wants yes or no. It is a decision problem, and the bound in it, here 2, is called k.

One filled-in question, with its map, start, end and k, is called an instance. Solve the best-value problem and comparing with k answers the decision at once. So if the decision is hard, finding the best must be hard too. That is why studying only yes-or-no questions still tells us about real problems.

Read the maths
PATH(G, u, v, k) = 1 if G has a path from u to v with at most k edges, else 0
G
The graph: dots (vertices) joined by lines (edges). Here, the map.
u, v
The start and the end.
k
The most edges allowed.
1, 0
Yes and no, written as numbers.

With numbers: the shortest path from a to g on this map uses 3 edges. So PATH with k = 2 is 0, and PATH with k = 3 is 1.

Try it: press Next to watch the search spread out from a. Then drag k from 3 to 2 and press Next to the end. The answer flips. The second tab goes the other way: it finds the shortest length by asking only yes-or-no questions.

What the book says

In an optimization problem each feasible solution has an associated value, and the goal is a feasible solution with the best value. SHORTEST-PATH takes an undirected graph G and vertices u and v and finds a path that uses the fewest edges. NP-completeness applies directly not to optimization problems but to decision problems, where the answer is yes or no.

An optimization problem is usually cast as a related decision problem by imposing a bound on the value. PATH asks whether a path from u to v exists with at most k edges. You can solve PATH by solving SHORTEST-PATH and comparing with k, so if a decision problem is hard, its related optimization problem is hard too.

Formally an abstract problem Q is a binary relation on a set I of problem instances and a set S of problem solutions; an abstract decision problem is a function from I to {0, 1}. The second tab, recovering the length by asking for k = 0, 1, 2 and so on, is in the spirit of Exercise 34.1-1. The map is this page's own. The listing is too, since the book states these in words.

34.1 · encodings

How you write the input changes the verdict

Time is measured against how long the input is when written down, so the same loop can look fast or slow.

Picture a program that takes one number k and loops k times. Is it fast? That depends on what "size of the input" means. Write 17 as seventeen 1s, tally style, and the input is 17 symbols long. The program takes 17 steps. That looks fast. Write 17 in binary as 10001, and the input is only 5 symbols long, yet the program still takes 17 steps.

Writing a number as a tally is unary. A way of writing things as strings is an encoding. Add one digit to a binary number and its value doubles, so the loop's work doubles too. Measured against a binary input, this loop is exponential. Measured against a unary input, it is linear. The program never changed.

So the book rules out wasteful encodings such as unary, and assumes any reasonable, concise one. Among those the choice doesn't matter: base 3 and base 2 convert into each other in polynomial time, so they agree on what is polynomial.

Read the maths
n = ⌊lg k⌋ + 1, so steps = k = Θ(2n)

The number. The loop runs this many times.
n

The power of 2 that gives k.
⌊ ⌋

With numbers: k = 17. lg 17 is about 4.09, which rounds down to 4, and 4 + 1 = 5 digits: 10001. The loop takes 17 steps, and 25 = 32 is within a factor of 2 of that.

Why it matters later: a method for subset sum that loops up to the target looks polynomial, but the target is written in binary, so the loop can be exponential in the input length. The book's standard encoding assumes binary integers.

Try it: press Next. Each step adds one binary digit, which doubles k. Watch the unary row overflow while the binary row grows by one cell. On the chart the binary dot climbs a steep curve and the unary dot runs off the right side.

What the book says

An encoding of a set S of abstract objects is a mapping e from S to the set of binary strings; for example e(17) = 10001. A concrete problem is one whose instance set is the set of binary strings, and the size of an instance i is the length of its string, written |i|. The complexity class P is the set of concrete decision problems that are polynomial-time solvable.

The book's example: an algorithm with sole input k runs in Θ(k) time. If k is in unary, the running time is O(n) on length-n inputs. In binary the input length is n = ⌊lg k⌋ + 1 and the running time is Θ(k) = Θ(2n).

Two encodings are polynomially related if each can be computed from the other in polynomial time. Lemma 34.1: if e1 and e2 are polynomially related, then e1(Q) ∈ P if and only if e2(Q) ∈ P. The book assumes that integers are encoded polynomially related to binary, and writes ⟨G⟩ for the standard encoding of a graph.

34.1 · a formal-language framework

Saying yes is not the same as always answering

A yes-or-no problem is just the set of strings whose answer is yes. Saying no out loud matters, but a stopwatch can add that.

Think of a door guard with a guest list. A guard who waves in everyone on the list, and stands silent forever in front of anyone else, is never wrong. Yet you could wait at that door for the rest of your life. A guard who also says "not on the list" does a bigger job.

Formally, a decision problem is a language: the set of input strings whose answer is yes. An algorithm accepts a language if it answers yes on every string in it. It need not answer anything on other strings. An algorithm decides a language if it also says no on every other string.

For fast algorithms the two turn out to be the same. Suppose an accepter always says yes within a known time. Set a stopwatch for that time. If it has not said yes when the watch runs out, it never will, so you can say no. That is Theorem 34.2. With it, P can be defined either way.

Read the maths
P = { L : some algorithm decides L in polynomial time }
L
A language: the set of strings whose answer is yes.
{ … : … }
"The set of all things such that…".
P
The class of languages that have a fast deciding algorithm.

With numbers: an input string of 5 symbols and a stopwatch of 2n² steps gives 2 × 5 × 5 = 50 steps before the watch says no.

Try it: keep Accept only and press Next: three inputs never get an answer. Switch to Stopwatch and run the same inputs. Now every input ends. Drag the stopwatch down until it is too short, and see a correct yes turn into a wrong no.

What the book says

An alphabet Σ is a finite set of symbols, and a language L over Σ is any set of strings made up of its symbols. A decision problem Q can be viewed as the language of strings for which Q(x) = 1.

An algorithm accepts x if its output on x is 1 and rejects x if the output is 0. A language is decided if every string in it is accepted and every string not in it is rejected. The book's example is an accepting algorithm for PATH that verifies the encoding, uses breadth-first search, compares with k, and otherwise runs forever. For problems such as Turing's Halting Problem, there exists an accepting algorithm, but no decision algorithm exists.

Theorem 34.2 says P = {L : L is accepted by a polynomial-time algorithm}. The proof is a simulation: if A accepts L within cnk steps, then A′ simulates that many steps and rejects if A has not accepted. The proof is nonconstructive, since we may not know the bound. The six inputs and the 2n² watch are this page's own.

34.2 · Hamiltonian cycles and verification

Finding is hard, checking is quick

Nobody knows a fast way to find a route through every dot, but checking a route someone hands you takes only a few moments.

Hamilton's game from 1856 runs on the corners of a dodecahedron: 20 dots and 30 lines. Find a round trip that visits every dot once and ends where it began. That trip is a Hamiltonian cycle. To find one by the plain method you try orderings of the dots. There are 20! of them, meaning 20 × 19 × … × 1, about 2.4 billion billion.

Now a friend hands you a route and says "here is one." You don't need to search at all. Is each dot in the list once? Does a line join each dot to the next? Does the last dot join the first? That's about 21 checks. The route is called a certificate, and the short program that tests it is a verification algorithm.

Here is the definition this chapter depends on. NP is the class of yes-or-no problems where every yes-instance has a short certificate that a fast program can check, and every no-instance has no certificate that passes. A lie can't fool the checker.

Read the maths

With numbers: for the dodecahedron, y lists 20 dots. A makes 1 check that every dot appears once, then 19 checks for lines along the route and 1 for the line home: 21 checks. Trying all orderings could take 20! = 2,432,902,008,176,640,000.

Easy to mix up: NP is not not polynomial, and it says nothing about being hard. It only says a yes can be checked quickly. The name stands for nondeterministic polynomial time, from an older way of defining the same class. A quick checker also says nothing about a no: on the third tab, no route exists, and it takes a full search to learn that.

Try it: press Next and follow the checker along the route. Then press Clear and click dots to build your own route, or press Sneaky route to watch a lie get caught. On the No cycle exists tab, try to beat the checker.

What the book says

A Hamiltonian cycle of an undirected graph G = (V, E) is a simple cycle that contains each vertex in V. The name honors W. R. Hamilton, who described a mathematical game on the dodecahedron (Figure 34.2(a)). Figure 34.2(b) shows a bipartite graph with an odd number of vertices, and any such graph is nonhamiltonian. The formal language is HAM-CYCLE = {⟨G⟩ : G is a hamiltonian graph}.

Listing all permutations of the vertices takes Ω(m!) time for m vertices, which is not O(nk) for any constant k in the encoding length n. In fact the problem is NP-complete. Yet a proof that a Hamiltonian cycle exists can be verified in O(n2) time.

A verification algorithm is a two-argument algorithm A, with an ordinary input string x and a binary string y called a certificate. A verifies x if there exists a certificate y such that A(x, y) = 1. The class NP is the class of languages that can be verified by a polynomial-time algorithm, with certificates of polynomial length. The name stands for nondeterministic polynomial time; the book uses the simpler yet equivalent notion of verification.

The dodecahedron is the book's. The 8-dot graph and the smaller 7-dot bipartite graph are this page's own, and the 7-dot one has the same property as Figure 34.2(b). The listing writes the book's description as a procedure.

34.2 · the complexity class NP

Three clubs, and four possible maps

Everything with a fast method is also quick to check. Whether the reverse holds is the famous open question, and nobody knows the answer.

Sort problems into clubs. P holds those you can solve quickly. NP holds those where you can check a yes quickly. And co-NP holds those where you can check a no quickly. For example, a formula that is claimed to be true for every input, called a tautology, is refuted by one input that makes it false. That one input is a quick proof of no.

Everything in P is also in NP. If you can solve it fast, you can check a yes by ignoring the certificate and solving it. So P sits inside NP. The same reasoning puts P inside co-NP. Beyond that, the honest answer is that nobody knows.

The book draws four possible maps. Maybe all three clubs are one. Maybe NP and co-NP are equal but bigger than P. Maybe P is exactly their overlap. Or, as most researchers expect, the three are all different. Each tab shows one map as a what-if. None of them is known to be the true one.

Why people expect them to differ: checking someone's solution usually feels easier than producing it from scratch. Researchers believe that feeling extends to P and NP. It is a belief backed by evidence, not a proof.

Read the maths
P ⊆ NP ∩ co-NP

"Is inside, or equal to."
∩
The overlap: what is in both.
L̄
The complement of L: the same question with yes and no swapped. A language is in co-NP when its complement is in NP.

With numbers: PATH is in all three clubs. An instance with k = 2 and shortest length 3 is a no. Running the search shows that directly, with no certificate needed.

Try it: pick a map at the top and press Next to add one club at a time. Watch where HAM-CYCLE and TAUTOLOGY land in each world. Their places change from map to map, and PATH always stays in P.

What the book says

If L ∈ P then L ∈ NP, since a polynomial-time algorithm to decide L can be converted to a two-argument verification algorithm that ignores any certificate. Thus P ⊆ NP.

That leaves the question of whether P = NP. A definitive answer is unknown, but most researchers believe that P and NP are not the same class.

The class co-NP is the set of languages L such that the complement of L is in NP. No one knows whether NP is closed under complement, that is, whether NP = co-NP. Since P is closed under complement, P ⊆ NP ∩ co-NP, and no one knows whether P = NP ∩ co-NP.

Figure 34.3 shows four possibilities. (a) P = NP = co-NP, which most researchers regard as the most unlikely. (b) NP = co-NP, though it need not be the case that P = NP. (c) P = NP ∩ co-NP, but NP is not closed under complement. (d) NP ≠ co-NP and P ≠ NP ∩ co-NP, which most researchers regard as the most likely.

34.3 · reducibility

Translate the question, keep the answer

A reduction rewrites any question of one kind as a question of another kind, quickly, so that the answer stays exactly the same.

Does the list 5, 2, 8, 2 hold two equal numbers? Comparing every pair works. Here is another way: sort it, giving 2, 2, 5, 8, and now ask a different question. Are two neighbours equal? Whatever the list, both questions get the same answer, and sorting is quick. You have translated problem A into problem B.

That is a reduction. It has two jobs. It must run in polynomial time, and it must keep the answer: yes goes to yes and no goes to no. It is a translator who knows nothing about the answer, only how to rewrite the question.

Once you have one, a fast method for B gives a fast method for A: translate, then run B's method. So A is no harder than B. Turn that around: if A is hard, then B must be hard too, since otherwise A would have a fast method after all. This turned-around use is the tool for the rest of the chapter.

Read the maths
L₁ ≤P L₂ means: x ∈ L₁ exactly when f(x) ∈ L₂
L₁, L₂
The two problems, each as its set of yes-inputs.
f
The translator: a function that a polynomial-time program can compute.
≤P
"Reduces to, in polynomial time." Read it as L₁ is no harder than L₂.

With numbers: x = 5, 2, 8, 2 is a yes for A. f(x) = 2, 2, 5, 8 is a yes for B. With x = 5, 2, 8, 3, it is no and no.

The mistake everyone makes: the arrow points the wrong way. To show that a new problem Y is at least as hard as a known NP-complete problem X, translate X into Y. So X → Y. Translating Y into X shows only that Y is no harder than X, and that proves nothing: even an easy problem can be rewritten as a hard-looking one. The third tab shows this with a real case.

Try it: press Next to follow one list through the pipe. Drag last number and the answer flips on both sides at once. On the Which way? tab, step through what you may and may not conclude from an arrow.

What the book says

A problem Q is reducible to Q′ if any instance of Q can be recast as an instance of Q′, and the solution to that instance provides a solution to the instance of Q. The book's example: a linear equation ax + b = 0 reduces to the quadratic equation ax2 + bx + 0 = 0, whose solutions are x = 0 and x = −b/a. If Q reduces to Q′, then Q is, in a sense, no harder to solve than Q′.

A language L1 is polynomial-time reducible to L2, written L1 ≤P L2, if there exists a polynomial-time computable function f such that for all x, x ∈ L1 if and only if f(x) ∈ L2 (34.1). Lemma 34.3: if L1 ≤P L2, then L2 ∈ P implies L1 ∈ P.

Figures 34.1 and 34.5 show the pipeline: transform the instance, run the decision algorithm on the result, and use its answer. The book warns: “Make sure that you don’t get the reduction backward.” The equal-numbers example is this page's own; the equation tab is the book's. The listing is this page's too.

34.3 · NP-completeness

The hardest problems in NP stand or fall together

An NP-complete problem is in NP, and every NP problem translates into it. A fast method for one would be a fast method for all.

Picture every problem in NP as a dot. Some dots have a special property: every other dot has a translation arrow into them. Such a dot is NP-complete. Since all of NP can be rewritten as that one problem, it is at least as hard as anything in NP.

So imagine that someone found a fast method for just one of these. Any NP problem could be translated into it and solved, and P would equal NP. Decades of effort have found no such method for any of them. Nobody has proved it can't be done, either. That is why proving a problem NP-complete counts as strong evidence it is hard, and not as a proof.

If a problem meets only the second condition, so everything translates into it but it may not be in NP itself, it is NP-hard. To add a new member you don't need to reduce every problem in NP. One reduction from a known member is enough, since translations chain: anything to X, then X to your problem.

Read the maths
L is NP-complete: (1) L ∈ NP, and (2) L′ ≤P L for every L′ ∈ NP
L
The problem being judged.
L′
Any problem in NP at all.
≤P
A polynomial-time translation from the left problem to the right one.

With numbers: say a translation takes n² steps and its output has length n², and L has a method taking m³ steps on length m. Then the whole job takes n² + (n²)³ = n² + n⁶ steps. For n = 10 that is 100 + 1,000,000. Still a fixed power of n.

Easy to mix up: NP-complete does not mean impossible. These problems can be solved, and small cases are solved in this very page. It means nobody knows a method that stays fast as inputs grow. NP-complete also doesn't mean the same as NP. PATH is in NP and has a fast method.

Try it: step through The definition first. Then press If one falls and watch all of NP slide into P. That tab is a what-if, and the page says so at each step. If one is stuck plays the other possible future.

What the book says

A language L is NP-complete if (1) L ∈ NP, and (2) L′ ≤P L for every L′ ∈ NP. If L satisfies property 2 but not necessarily property 1, then L is NP-hard. NPC is the class of NP-complete languages.

Theorem 34.4: if any NP-complete problem is polynomial-time solvable, then P = NP. Equivalently, if any problem in NP is not polynomial-time solvable, then no NP-complete problem is polynomial-time solvable.

Most theoretical computer scientists believe that P ≠ NP, which leads to the relationships of Figure 34.6: P and NPC both wholly inside NP, with P ∩ NPC empty. For all we know, however, someone may yet come up with a polynomial-time algorithm for an NP-complete problem. Since none has been discovered, a proof that a problem is NP-complete provides excellent evidence that it is intractable.

Lemma 34.8: if L′ ≤P L for some L′ ∈ NPC, then L is NP-hard; if also L ∈ NP, then L ∈ NPC. The chapter notes credit the notion to Cook and, independently, Levin, with Karp introducing the methodology of reductions in 1972. The listing and the n² and n³ figures are this page's own illustration.

34.3 · circuit satisfiability

Can any input switch this circuit on?

A circuit has a few input switches and one output. The question: does any setting of the switches make the output 1?

A little box has three switches and one light. Inside are three kinds of logic gate. NOT flips its input. AND gives 1 only if all its inputs are 1. OR gives 1 if any input is 1. The wires never loop back. Can you make the light come on?

If a setting lights it, the box is satisfiable, and that setting is a satisfying assignment. The question is CIRCUIT-SAT. It shows up in real life: a designer who learns that part of a chip can only ever output 0 can replace it with a plain 0.

With 3 switches, you can try all 8 settings. With k switches there are 2k settings, and that gets out of hand fast. But checking one setting is quick: run each gate once. So CIRCUIT-SAT is in NP, and a setting that lights the box is the certificate.

34.3 · why circuit-sat is NP-hard

Any checker can be unrolled into a circuit

Any quick checking program can be unrolled step by step into one big circuit, which lights up exactly when some proof passes the check.

Why is circuit satisfiability the hardest in NP? Take any problem in NP. By definition it has a checker: a program that takes the input and a certificate, and says 1 or 0, in polynomial time. Everything in a computer is made of gates, so one step of that program is a chunk of circuit.

The whole state of the computer at a moment is one configuration: the program, its position, the input, the certificate and the scratch memory. The hardware, called M, maps one configuration to the next. Paste a copy of M for each step, feeding one into the next, and the whole run becomes one big circuit with no loops.

Now wire in the known input, and leave only the certificate bits as switches. The output light is the checker's answer. So this circuit is satisfiable exactly when some certificate passes, which is exactly when the original answer is yes. The size stays polynomial, because the run is polynomial in length.

With numbers: x is the numbers 3, 5, 2 with target 7. With y = 1 0 1, the checker adds 3 + 2 = 5, which isn't 7, so C(y) = 0. With y = 0 1 1, it adds 5 + 2 = 7, so C(y) = 1.

Why it is a real reduction: it only builds the circuit. It never runs a search and never needs to know the answer. Building takes polynomial time even when solving might not.

Lemma 34.6: the circuit-satisfiability problem is NP-hard. Let L be any language in NP, verified by an algorithm A in polynomial time. Let T(n) be the worst-case running time on length-n strings, with T(n) = O(nk) and certificates of length O(nk).

A configuration comprises the program for A, the program counter and auxiliary machine state, the input x, the certificate y, and working storage (Figure 34.9). A combinational circuit M implements the hardware and maps each configuration to the next. The reduction algorithm F pastes together T(n) copies of M, wires in the known values, leaves only the certificate bits as inputs, and keeps only the output bit. Then C(y) = A(x, y).

C is satisfiable if and only if some certificate has A(x, y) = 1, and C has size polynomial in n. With Lemma 34.5 this gives Theorem 34.7: the circuit-satisfiability problem is NP-complete. The book calls this a sketch, since the formal proof requires technical detail beyond its scope.

34.4 · formula satisfiability

Rewrite the circuit as a formula

Give every wire its own variable and describe each gate in one short formula. The circuit and the formula then get the same answer.

We now have one NP-complete problem, so we can use reductions. To show a new problem is NP-complete, you do two things: show it is in NP, and translate a known NP-complete problem into it. Here the new problem is SAT: can this boolean formula be made true?

The obvious translation is to write each gate's formula with its inputs' formulas inside it. That fails. When a wire feeds two gates, its formula gets copied twice, and copies of copies grow exponentially. The second tab shows it. A reduction must run in polynomial time, so this one isn't allowed.

The trick: name every wire, and let each gate say only one short thing about its wires. For a NOT gate, "x₄ has the opposite value of x₃". AND those short statements together with "the output wire is 1". Each gate adds one small piece, so the size stays small. Any values that make all the pieces true are exactly a correct run of the circuit that lights the output.

What the book says

An instance of SAT is a boolean formula composed of n boolean variables, m boolean connectives such as ∧, ∨, ¬, → and ↔, and parentheses. SAT = {⟨φ⟩ : φ is a satisfiable boolean formula}. This problem has the historical honor of being the first problem ever shown to be NP-complete.

The straightforward induction fails, since shared subformulas can make the formula grow exponentially (Exercise 34.4-1). Proof outline: if C has a satisfying assignment, each wire has a well-defined value and each clause evaluates to 1. 34.4 · 3-CNF satisfiability

Press any formula into one tidy shape

Any formula can be rewritten as an AND of small groups with exactly three items each, and that tidy form stays just as hard.

A wild formula is awkward to work with, because a reduction has to handle every possible shape. So we press it into a neat form. A literal is a variable or its negation, like x₁ or ¬x₁. A clause is an OR of literals. A formula is in 3-CNF if it is an AND of clauses with exactly three different literals each. For example (x₁ ∨ ¬x₁ ∨ ¬x₂) ∧ (x₃ ∨ x₂ ∨ x₄).

There are three moves. First, draw the formula as a tree and name the output of every operator, y₁, y₂ and so on, just as we named wires. Second, rewrite each small piece with a truth table. It has at most 8 rows, so this is cheap. Find the rows that come out 0 and forbid each one with a clause. Third, top up any clause with fewer than 3 literals, using two spare variables p and q.

Read the maths

row
One setting of the piece's variables, here y₁ = 1, y₂ = 0, x₂ = 1.

Flip each value in the row: it is false for that row alone, and true for all others.
p, q
Spare variables. (a ∨ b) becomes (a ∨ b ∨ p) ∧ (a ∨ b ∨ ¬p). Whatever p is, one clause reduces to a ∨ b and the other is true.

With numbers: the book's first piece has 8 rows, and 4 of them are 0, so it becomes 4 clauses. The book's whole formula has 4 variables and 6 operators, and it ends up as 28 clauses over 12 variables.

Why restrict at all? A hard problem stays useful only if the restricted version is still hard. Each move keeps the answer the same, so 3-CNF-SAT is as hard as SAT. And its regular shape makes the next reductions much simpler.

What the book says

A literal is an occurrence of a variable or its negation. A clause is the OR of one or more literals. A formula is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) if it is an AND of clauses, and in 3-CNF if each clause contains exactly three distinct literals. Theorem 34.10: satisfiability of boolean formulas in 3-conjunctive normal form is NP-complete.

The reduction SAT ≤P 3-CNF-SAT has three steps. (1) Build a binary parse tree (Figure 34.11), introduce a variable yi for each internal node, and rewrite the formula as φ′, the root variable ANDed with a clause for each node. (2) For each clause, build a truth table (Figure 34.12), form a DNF formula equivalent to its negation from the rows that evaluate to 0, and apply DeMorgan's laws to get a CNF formula φ″ with at most three literals per clause. (3) Pad to exactly three distinct literals using auxiliary variables p and q to get φ‴.

Proof outline: the first step preserves satisfiability, the second is algebraically equivalent, and the third is effectively equivalent for any values of p and q. Size: at most one variable and clause per connective, at most eight clauses per clause of φ′, and at most four per clause of φ″, so the result is polynomial in the length of the original.

34.5.1 · the clique problem

A formula turns into a graph

Each group in a formula becomes three dots. Lines join dots that can be true together. A fully joined set of dots solves the formula.

At a party, a clique is a group where everybody knows everybody. In a graph it is a set of dots with a line between every two of them. CLIQUE asks: does this graph have a clique of size k?

Logic formulas and graphs seem to have nothing in common, which is what makes this reduction surprising. To satisfy a 3-CNF formula, you need one true literal in every clause, with no contradictions. So draw one dot per literal, in groups by clause. Join two dots if they're in different clauses and don't contradict, so never x₁ with ¬x₁. Ask for a clique with one dot per clause.

A line now means "these two can be true at the same time". A clique of size k picks one literal from each of the k clauses, and all of them agree. That is a way to satisfy the formula. And any way to satisfy the formula gives such a clique.

Why it isn't cheating: the reduction only looks at the formula's text. It never finds out whether the formula can be satisfied, which we don't know how to do quickly. It builds the same kind of graph either way.

What the book says

A clique in an undirected graph is a subset of vertices, each pair of which is connected by an edge. CLIQUE = {⟨G, k⟩ : G is a graph containing a clique of size k}. Listing all k-subsets takes Ω(k2 · (|V| choose k)) time, which is polynomial if k is constant but superpolynomial when k is near |V|/2.

Theorem 34.11: the clique problem is NP-complete. The certificate is the set of vertices. For 3-CNF-SAT ≤P CLIQUE, place a triple of vertices for each clause, and add an edge when two vertices are in different triples and their literals are consistent. Figure 34.14 uses φ = (x1 ∨ ¬x2 ∨ ¬x3) ∧ (¬x1 ∨ x2 ∨ x3) ∧ (x1 ∨ x2 ∨ x3).

Proof outline: a satisfying assignment makes one literal true in each clause, and those vertices form a clique, since true literals can't be complements. Conversely, a clique of size k has exactly one vertex per triple, and assigning 1 to those literals satisfies every clause. The book adds that restricted graphs suffice, and warns against a reduction that relies on knowing whether φ is satisfiable.

34.5.2 · the vertex-cover problem

Swap lines for gaps

Draw exactly the lines the graph doesn't have. The members of a big clique are now mutually unjoined, so everyone else touches every line.

Picture a museum whose corridors must each be watched from one end. How few guards can watch them all? A guard at a dot watches every line touching it. A set of dots that touches every line is a vertex cover. VERTEX-COVER asks: can k dots do it?

This reduction takes one simple move: flip the graph. Its complement has a line exactly where the original has none. Take a clique in the original: every two members are joined. In the complement, no two members are joined. So every line of the complement must touch somebody outside the clique, and those outsiders are a vertex cover.

So a clique of size k in the graph means a cover of size |V| − k in its complement, and the other way round. Flipping the lines takes polynomial time, and the answer stays the same.

34.5.3 · the Hamiltonian-cycle problem

A small maze that forces a choice

Each line becomes a small maze of 12 dots. A round trip has only three ways through, and those ways record which dots were picked.

This is the cleverest translation of the chapter. We start with a vertex cover question: can k dots touch every line? We must build a graph that has a round trip through all its dots exactly when the answer is yes. The tool is a gadget: a small piece of a graph that forces a certain behaviour.

Each line (u, v) becomes one gadget, with a u side and a v side of 6 dots each. It is joined to the rest only at its four corners. Because of how it's wired, a trip visiting all its dots has just three choices. Enter on the u side and visit all 12. Enter on the v side and visit all 12. Or visit one side on one pass and the other on a second pass. So the gadget asks: is u, or v, or both, in the cover?

Then for each dot u, chain together all the gadgets for its lines, on its side. A trip that picks u runs down that whole chain. Finally add k selector dots, each joined to the start and end of every chain. There are k selectors, so the trip picks exactly k chains. It can reach every dot only if those k dots touch every line. That is a vertex cover.

|V′| = 12 |E| + k   |E′| = 16 |E| + (2k − 1) |V|

What the book says

Theorem 34.13: the hamiltonian cycle problem is NP-complete. For VERTEX-COVER ≤P HAM-CYCLE, the book assumes that G has no isolated vertices and that k ≤ |V|. Each edge (u, v) gets a gadget Γuv with 12 vertices [u, v, i] and [v, u, i], for 1 ≤ i ≤ 6, and 14 edges (Figure 34.16). Only the four corners have edges from outside.

Any hamiltonian cycle must traverse a gadget in one of the three ways of Figures 34.16(b)–(d). It is impossible to construct two vertex-disjoint paths, one from [u, v, 1] to [v, u, 6] and the other from [v, u, 1] to [u, v, 6], that together contain all of the gadget's vertices.

The other vertices are the selector vertices s1, …, sk. Edges join consecutive gadgets on each vertex's chain, and each selector joins the first and last vertex of each chain. The sizes are polynomial, as the box above shows.

Proof outline: from a cover of size k, a cycle starts at s1, passes all gadgets incident on u1, then visits s2, and so on back to s1, visiting each gadget once or twice. Conversely, a cycle splits into paths between selectors, each passing along one chain, and every gadget's vertices are visited, so those chains' vertices form a cover. 34.5.5 · the subset-sum problem

Logic written in the digits of numbers

Turn each part of the formula into a long number, and pick a target so that adding the right numbers spells out a solution.

Here's a question for a shopkeeper. Given a bag of numbers, can some of them add up to exactly this target? That is the subset-sum problem. It looks like arithmetic. Yet a 3-CNF formula fits into it, by treating each digit as its own little register.

Write numbers in base 10, with one digit for each variable and one for each clause. Each variable gets two numbers, one for true and one for false. Both have a 1 in that variable's digit. The target has a 1 there too, so you must pick exactly one of the two. That picks a value for the variable. Each number also has a 1 in the digit of every clause its literal appears in.

The target has a 4 in each clause digit. Picked literals can supply at most 3, so at least one must be true. Slack numbers worth 1 and 2 then top up that digit to exactly 4. They can add 3 at most, so they can never satisfy a clause alone. No digit ever sums past 6, so nothing carries, and each digit works on its own.

Read the maths

vᵢ, vᵢ′
The two numbers for variable xᵢ: pick vᵢ for xᵢ = 1, and vᵢ′ for xᵢ = 0.
sⱼ, sⱼ′
The slack numbers for clause Cⱼ, with a 1 and a 2 in its digit.

Why binary matters: these numbers are huge compared with their length, and the standard encoding writes them in binary. A loop up to the target would be exponential in the input's length, the same trap as the encoding scene.

What the book says

The subset-sum problem takes a finite set S of positive integers and a target t > 0, and asks whether some subset of S sums to exactly t. The standard encoding codes the integers in binary. Theorem 34.15: the subset-sum problem is NP-complete. The certificate is the subset.

For 3-CNF-SAT ≤P SUBSET-SUM, assume that no clause contains both a variable and its negation, and that each variable appears in some clause. Numbers are in base 10 with n + k digits. For each clause, sj and s′j have a 1 and a 2 in its digit. The greatest digit sum is 6, so no carries occur. The instance with t = 138457 at the start of the section is this one read in base 7.

34.5.6 · reduction strategies

Is this proof right?

A valid proof needs a quick checker, plus a fast translation that keeps the answer, starting from a problem already known to be NP-complete.

Say a colleague brings you a problem, Y, and says "it's NP-complete, here's my proof." You now know what to ask. Is there a quick checker, so that Y is in NP? Does the translation start at a known NP-complete problem and end at Y? Does it run in polynomial time, without peeking at the answer? Does it send yes to yes and no to no?

How do you find a reduction in the first place? The book offers a few habits. Go from general to specific: handle any input of the known problem, but aim at a special shape of Y. Pick a source with structure, such as 3-CNF rather than a general formula. Stay in the same field when you can, and use 3-CNF to cross between fields. Look for your problem containing a known one as a special case. Use big rewards and penalties, as TSP did with prices. And build gadgets, as Hamiltonian cycle did.

Then what? Having proved a problem NP-complete, you stop hunting for a fast exact method that nobody has ever found. You look for an approximation, which is the next chapter, or a special case with a fast method. That isn't giving up. It is knowing where to dig. And never say a proof shows that a problem has no fast method, because that part remains unknown.

What the book says

Lemma 34.8 gives the method: prove L ∈ NP, then prove L NP-hard by selecting a known NP-complete language L′, describing an algorithm that computes a function f mapping every instance of L′ to an instance of L, proving x ∈ L′ if and only if f(x) ∈ L, and proving that the algorithm runs in polynomial time.

Pitfalls: don't get the reduction backward; it should go from X to Y. Reducing a known NP-complete problem to Y proves only that Y is NP-hard; you also need to prove it's in NP. The book also warns that a reduction must not rely on knowing whether the formula is satisfiable. Exercise 34.4-3 rejects Professor Jagger's truth-table strategy for failing to run in polynomial time.

Strategies: go from general to specific; take advantage of structure in the problem you are reducing from; look for special cases; select an appropriate problem to reduce from; make big rewards and big penalties; design gadgets. A gadget is a component that enforces certain properties. The slack variables in subset sum can be viewed as simple gadgets.

The book closes with hope: “you shouldn’t give up hope just because a problem is NP-complete.” Chapter 35 gives approximation algorithms. The chapter on one page

The key ideas, in order.

Look-alikes

Shortest against longest path, Euler tour against Hamiltonian cycle, 2-CNF against 3-CNF: one of each pair has a fast method and the other is NP-complete.

Polynomial is the line

A method with at most c · nk steps, for fixed c and k, runs in polynomial time. Doubling for every extra item soon beats any fixed power.

Yes or no, in a sensible encoding

The theory asks only yes-or-no questions, which are no harder than finding the best answer. Time is measured against input length, so tally-style numbers are ruled out.

P and NP

P: can be solved quickly. NP: a yes can be checked quickly, given a certificate. P sits inside NP. Whether they are equal is unknown.

NP does not mean not polynomial

It stands for nondeterministic polynomial time, and in this book it means quick to check. Everything in P is in NP as well.

Reductions keep the answer

A polynomial-time translation from A to B, with yes going to yes and no going to no. Then B fast means A fast, and A hard means B hard. The direction matters.

NP-complete means the hardest in NP

In NP, and every NP problem translates into it. One fast method for any one of them would make P equal NP. It does not mean impossible.

A first problem, then a chain

Any checker unrolls into a circuit, so circuit satisfiability is first. After that one reduction from a known NP-complete problem, plus a quick checker, is enough for each new one.

Now what?

Stop hunting for a fast exact method that nobody has found. Look for an approximation, as in Chapter 35, or a special case with a fast method.

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

simple path
A path that never visits the same vertex twice.
Euler tour
A round trip that uses every line of a graph exactly once. It may pass through a vertex several times.
Hamiltonian cycle
A round trip that visits every vertex of a graph exactly once. Finding one is NP-complete.
polynomial time
At most c · nk steps on an input of length n, where c and k are fixed numbers.
tractable
The book's word for solvable in polynomial time. Informally, easy.
superpolynomial
Growing faster than every fixed power of n, as 2n does.
optimization problem
A problem that asks for the best value, such as the fewest edges.
decision problem
A problem whose answer is yes or no, such as: is there a path with at most k edges?
instance
One filled-in question: a particular graph, particular vertices and a particular k.
encoding
A rule for writing things, such as numbers or graphs, as strings of symbols.
unary
Writing a number as a tally: 5 is 11111. It is too wasteful to count as a reasonable encoding.
language
A set of strings. A decision problem is the language of inputs whose answer is yes.
accepts
An algorithm accepts a language if it answers yes on every string in it. It may never answer on other strings.
decides
An algorithm decides a language if it answers yes on every string in it and no on every other string.
P
The class of decision problems that can be solved in polynomial time.
NP
The class of decision problems where a yes can be checked in polynomial time, given a short certificate. It does not mean not polynomial.
certificate
A short piece of evidence for a yes answer, like a route. Also called a proof.
verification algorithm
A program that takes an input and a certificate, and answers 1 if the certificate proves the input is a yes.
co-NP
The class of decision problems where a no can be checked quickly: its yes and no swapped version is in NP.
tautology
A formula that comes out true for every setting of its variables.
reduction
A polynomial-time translation of every instance of one problem into an instance of another, keeping the answer the same.
NP-complete
In NP, and every problem in NP translates into it. One of the hardest in NP. It does not mean impossible.
NP-hard
Every problem in NP translates into it. It need not be in NP itself.
logic gate
A small part of a circuit: NOT flips a bit, AND is 1 when all inputs are 1, and OR is 1 when any input is 1.
satisfiable
Said of a circuit or formula that some setting of its inputs makes come out 1.
satisfying assignment
A setting of the inputs that makes a circuit or formula come out 1.
CIRCUIT-SAT
The problem: can some setting of the inputs make this circuit output 1? The first problem this chapter proves NP-complete.
configuration
The whole state of a computer at one moment: program, position, input, certificate and working memory.
SAT
The problem: can some setting of the variables make this formula true?
boolean formula
Variables holding 0 or 1, joined with connectives such as AND, OR and NOT, and parentheses.
literal
A variable or its negation, such as x₁ or ¬x₁.
clause
In 3-CNF, an OR of literals. The book also uses the word for each small piece of a formula.
3-CNF
An AND of clauses, each with exactly three different literals. 3-CNF-SAT asks if such a formula can be made true.
clique
A set of vertices with an edge between every two of them. CLIQUE is the problem: does this graph have a clique of size k?
vertex cover
A set of vertices that touches every edge of a graph.
VERTEX-COVER
The problem: does this graph have a vertex cover of size k?
complement
The graph with the same vertices and exactly the edges the original lacks.
gadget / gadgets
A small piece of a graph, or of any construction, that enforces a property.
selector
One of k extra vertices in the Hamiltonian-cycle reduction. Each one picks a chain, and so one member of the cover.
traveling-salesperson problem
Visit every city once and return, at the lowest total price.
TSP
The yes-or-no form of the traveling-salesperson problem: is there a tour of cost at most k?
tour
A round trip that visits every city exactly once.
subset-sum problem
Given some positive numbers and a target, do some of the numbers add to exactly the target?
slack
In the subset-sum reduction, extra numbers worth 1 or 2 in a clause's digit that top it up to the target's 4.
