Maximum Flow

Crates, water and data can all be pushed from a source to a sink through routes that each have a limit. This chapter finds the most that can get through, one path at a time, and shows that the answer always equals the capacity of the tightest bottleneck.

“Flow networks can model many problems, including liquids flowing through pipes, parts through assembly lines, current through electrical networks, and information through communication networks.” — Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest & Stein

24.1 · flow networks

Crates from Vancouver to Winnipeg

The Lucky Puck Company makes hockey pucks in Vancouver and stores them in Winnipeg. It leases space on trucks between cities, and each route can carry only so many crates a day. Draw the cities as dots and the routes as arrows labelled with their daily limit and you have a flow network: a directed graph where every edge (u, v) has a capacity c(u, v) ≥ 0, one vertex is the source s (the factory) and one is the sink t (the warehouse).

A shipping plan is a flow f: how many crates go along each edge. It must obey two rules. The capacity constraint says 0 ≤ f(u, v) ≤ c(u, v): a truck can't carry a negative load, or more than it holds. Flow conservation says that at every city except s and t, what arrives equals what leaves, or crates would pile up. The plan's value |f| is what leaves s minus what comes back into it: here 19 crates a day.

The maximum-flow problem asks for a legal flow of the greatest value. Lucky Puck can't change the routes or the capacities, so that value is the number of crates a day worth producing.

Try it: click an arrow, then drag the slider to change its flow. Add one crate to (s, v2): v2 turns coral, because more arrives than leaves. Push an edge past its capacity and the edge turns coral. Press Step to watch the checking procedure walk the edges, then the vertices, and stop at the first broken rule. Then try to raise |f| above 19 with every check green; each vertex you change needs a partner change somewhere else.

What the book says

A flow network G = (V, E) is a directed graph in which each edge (u, v) ∈ E has a nonnegative capacity c(u, v) ≥ 0. If E contains an edge (u, v) it contains no edge (v, u) in the reverse direction; the next scene shows how to work around that. If (u, v) ∉ E, then c(u, v) = 0, and self-loops are disallowed. The network has a source s and a sink t, and the book assumes every vertex lies on some path s ⤳ v ⤳ t, which gives |E| ≥ |V| − 1.

A flow is a real-valued function f : V × V → ℝ with two properties. Capacity constraint: for all u, v, 0 ≤ f(u, v) ≤ c(u, v). Flow conservation: for all u ∈ V − {s, t}, the sum of f(v, u) over v equals the sum of f(u, v) over v; informally, “flow in equals flow out.” When (u, v) ∉ E there can be no flow, so f(u, v) = 0. The chapter notes that flow conservation is Kirchhoff's current law when the material is electrical current.

The value of a flow is |f| = Σv f(s, v) − Σv f(v, s), equation (24.1): the flow out of the source minus the flow into it. A flow network usually has no edges into the source, so the second sum is 0; it is included because in residual networks the flow into the source can be positive. In the maximum-flow problem the input is a flow network G with source s and sink t, and the goal is a flow of maximum value.

In the book's example the Lucky Puck Company has a factory (the source) in Vancouver and a warehouse (the sink) in Winnipeg, and ships at most c(u, v) crates per day between each pair of cities. The company cares only that p crates per day leave the factory and p crates per day arrive at the warehouse; conservation holds because in a steady state crates would otherwise accumulate at intermediate cities. Figure 24.1(b), the flow on the machine at the start, has value 19.

24.1 · flow rules, antiparallel edges, multiple sources and sinks

Two tidy-ups for awkward networks

The rules of the last scene assumed a tidy network: no edge (v, u) when (u, v) exists, and exactly one source and one sink. Real problems break both. Say the trucking firm offers 10 more crates a day from Edmonton to Calgary, while Calgary to Edmonton already has 4. The two edges point opposite ways between the same pair of cities: they are antiparallel.

The fix is to put a stop in the middle of one of them. Choose one edge (u, v), add a new vertex x, and replace the edge by (u, x) and (x, v), both with the old capacity. A crate still gets from u to v with the same limit, and no pair of antiparallel edges is left.

The second awkward case is several factories and several warehouses. Add a supersource s with an edge of capacity ∞ to every real source, and a supersink t with an edge of capacity ∞ from every real sink. The supersource can supply whatever the factories want to send, and the supersink can take whatever the warehouses receive, so one ordinary source and sink stand in for the many.

Try it: in Antiparallel edges, choose which of the two edges to split, then press Step through the six lines and watch the two max-flow tiles. Press Shuffle capacities and run it again: new numbers, and the two tiles still agree. Then switch to Many sources and sinks and build the supersource and supersink; the last tile is the biggest flow the whole network can carry.

What the book says

Suppose the trucking firm offers Lucky Puck space for 10 crates in trucks going from Edmonton to Calgary. Adding it forms Figure 24.2(a), which violates the assumption that if (v1, v2) ∈ E then (v2, v1) ∉ E. The two edges are antiparallel, so the network must be transformed into an equivalent one without them.

To transform the network, choose one of the two antiparallel edges, in this case (v1, v2), and split it by adding a new vertex v′ and replacing the edge with the pair (v1, v′) and (v′, v2). The capacity of both new edges is the capacity of the original edge. The resulting network satisfies the property that if an edge belongs to the network, the reverse edge does not. Exercise 24.1-1 asks the reader to prove that the resulting network is equivalent to the original one.

A maximum-flow problem may have several sources and sinks. The company might have a set of m factories {s1, …, sm} and a set of n warehouses {t1, …, tn}, as in Figure 24.3(a). The book's verdict: “Fortunately, this problem is no harder than ordinary maximum flow.”

The problem reduces to an ordinary one. Add a supersource s and a directed edge (s, si) with capacity c(s, si) = ∞ for each i = 1, …, m; likewise create a supersink t and an edge (ti, t) with capacity ∞ for each i = 1, …, n. Intuitively, any flow in network (a) corresponds to a flow in network (b), and vice versa: the single supersource provides as much flow as desired for the multiple sources, and the single supersink consumes as much as desired for the multiple sinks. Exercise 24.1-2 asks for a formal proof that the two problems are equivalent.

24.2 · residual networks

What can still change

Once crates are on the road, ask each truck route two questions: how many more could go along it, and how many could be taken back off? The residual network Gf draws those answers as edges. A route (u, v) with capacity 16 carrying 11 gives a forward edge (u, v) with residual capacity 16 − 11 = 5, the room left, and a reverse edge (v, u) with residual capacity 11, the flow that could be sent back.

Sending flow along a reverse edge is cancellation: 5 crates one way and 2 the other is the same, in the end, as 3 crates one way. A full route has no forward edge, an empty route has no reverse edge, and an edge of residual capacity 0 simply isn't drawn, so Gf has at most 2|E| edges.

A flow in Gf is a roadmap for improving f. Adding it, the augmentation f ↑ f′, gives a legal flow of value |f| + |f′|. This scene only builds Gf; the next ones use it.

Try it: press Step to build Gf one edge of G at a time, or Play. Then click a residual edge on the right to push one unit along it: a grey forward edge adds a crate to the original edge, a blue reverse edge takes one off. One push upsets the vertices at its two ends (they turn coral), because a real augmentation pushes along a whole path from s to t. You can also click an edge on the left and use the slider.

What the book says

Given a flow network G and a flow f, the residual network Gf consists of edges whose capacities represent how the flow can change on edges of G. An edge can admit an amount of additional flow equal to its capacity minus its flow; if that value is positive, the edge goes into Gf with residual capacity cf(u, v) = c(u, v) − f(u, v). Edges whose flow equals their capacity have cf = 0 and do not belong to Gf.

The residual network can also contain edges that are not in G. To represent a possible decrease in the positive flow f(u, v), Gf contains an edge (v, u) with residual capacity cf(v, u) = f(u, v). These reverse edges let an algorithm send back flow it has already sent. Equation (24.2) puts the three cases together: c(u, v) − f(u, v) if (u, v) ∈ E, f(v, u) if (v, u) ∈ E, and 0 otherwise. Because a flow network has no antiparallel edges, exactly one case applies to each ordered pair.

As an example, if c(u, v) = 16 and f(u, v) = 11, then f(u, v) can increase by up to cf(u, v) = 5 units before exceeding the capacity, or up to 11 units can return from v to u, so cf(v, u) = 11. The edge set is Ef = {(u, v) : cf(u, v) > 0}, equation (24.3), and because its edges are edges of E or their reversals, |Ef| ≤ 2|E|. Figure 24.4(a) and (b) show the flow of Figure 24.1(b) and its residual network.

Gf is similar to a flow network with capacities cf, but it can contain antiparallel edges, so it isn't one; a flow in it is defined the same way, with respect to cf. If f is a flow in G and f′ is a flow in Gf, the augmentation f ↑ f′ is (f ↑ f′)(u, v) = f(u, v) + f′(u, v) − f′(v, u) for (u, v) ∈ E, and 0 otherwise, equation (24.4). Pushing flow on the reverse edge is also known as cancellation: if 5 crates go from u to v and 2 crates go from v to u, that is equivalent to sending 3 crates from u to v and none the other way. Lemma 24.1 shows that f ↑ f′ is a flow in G with value |f ↑ f′| = |f| + |f′|.

24.2 · augmenting paths

One more trip, as big as the tightest road

The trucks are already rolling, and the residual network shows what is left: every road with room to spare, plus a reverse road wherever trucks could be called back. Now look for a route from the depot s to the shop t that follows only those edges. That route is an augmenting path: a simple path from s to t in the residual network Gf. There can be several, and any one will do.

How many extra trucks can one trip carry? As many as its tightest leg allows. The residual capacity of the path, cf(p), is the smallest cf(u, v) along it. Push that many units over every leg. A leg that is a real road (u, v) of G gets cf(p) more flow. A leg that is a reverse edge means send some back: the flow on the real road (v, u) goes down by cf(p), which frees capacity elsewhere.

Nothing breaks. Every road stays within its capacity, every junction still sends out what it takes in, and the flow value rises by exactly cf(p). The new flow is the augmentation of f by the path.

Try it: in the lower picture click the yellow edges one after another, starting at s, to build a path to t (click the last edge again to take it back), or press Find a path again and again to see every augmenting path in turn. The tightest edge turns coral. Press Augment and watch the flow in the upper picture change, then check the green tick. With Zero flow every edge is forward, so the path is simply a route through G.

What the book says

Given a flow network G and a flow f, an augmenting path p is a simple path from s to t in the residual network Gf. The flow on an edge (u, v) of an augmenting path may increase by up to cf(u, v) without violating the capacity constraint on whichever of (u, v) and (v, u) belongs to the original network. The maximum amount by which the flow on each edge of p can grow is the residual capacity of p, cf(p) = min {cf(u, v) : (u, v) is in p}. In Figure 24.4(b) the smallest residual capacity on the blue path is cf(v2, v3) = 4.

The augmentation f ↑ f′ of a flow f by a flow f′ in Gf is (f ↑ f′)(u, v) = f(u, v) + f′(u, v) − f′(v, u) for (u, v) ∈ E, and 0 otherwise (equation 24.4). Pushing flow on the reverse edge in the residual network is also known as cancellation: if 5 crates of hockey pucks go from u to v and 2 crates go from v to u, that is equivalent to sending 3 crates from u to v and none from v to u. Lemma 24.1 says f ↑ f′ is a flow in G with value |f| + |f′|.

Lemma 24.2 defines fp(u, v) = cf(p) if (u, v) is on p and 0 otherwise, and states that it is a flow in Gf with value |fp| = cf(p) > 0. Corollary 24.3 follows at once from Lemmas 24.1 and 24.2: f ↑ fp is a flow in G with value |f| + |fp| > |f|. Figure 24.4(c) shows the flow of Figure 24.4(a) augmented along the blue path by 4, and Figure 24.4(d) the residual network that results.

24.2 · the Ford-Fulkerson method

Top up the trucks until no route is left

Start with no trucks rolling at all. Find any augmenting path, send as many extra trucks along it as its tightest leg allows, and look again. Repeat until the residual network has no path from s to t. That loop is the Ford-Fulkerson method, and the next scene shows why the flow it stops with is a maximum.

The book calls it a method rather than an algorithm because line 3 says only “a path”. Depth-first search, breadth-first search, or a clever guess are all allowed, and they do not cost the same. With integer capacities every round adds at least 1 to the flow value, so there are at most |f*| rounds, and each round costs O(E): the total is O(E |f*|). That is a good bound when the best flow is small and a poor one when it is huge.

Figure 24.7 makes the point. Two roads leaving s and two entering t carry 106 trucks each, and a single middle road between u and v carries 1. If every path unluckily crosses the middle road, forwards and then backwards, each round adds one unit and the run takes 2,000,000 rounds. The same method with a different search for line 3 finishes in four.

Try it: on Figure 24.6, press Step repeatedly and follow the highlighted lines: the search walks forward (and backs up from dead ends), the bottleneck turns coral, then each edge is updated. In the Rust view the highlight also follows the recursion, and the memory strip shows one stack frame per vertex on the path. Then pick Figure 24.7 · unlucky and press Play: after a few rounds adding 1 each, the counter races on. Try M = 4 to watch every round, then Figure 24.7 · DFS: same network, same method, four rounds.

What the book says

The Ford-Fulkerson method iteratively increases the value of the flow. It starts with f(u, v) = 0 for all u, v, a flow of value 0, and each iteration finds an augmenting path in the associated residual network Gf; the edges of that path say on which edges of G to update the flow. The flow on a particular edge may increase or decrease, and decreasing the flow on one edge may let more flow travel from the source to the sink. FORD-FULKERSON-METHOD repeats until the residual network has no more augmenting paths, and the max-flow min-cut theorem shows that the result is then a maximum flow. The book calls it a “method” because it encompasses several implementations with differing running times.

FORD-FULKERSON implements the method. Lines 1–2 set the flow on every edge to 0. The while loop of lines 3–8 repeatedly finds a path p in Gf and augments the flow along it by the residual capacity cf(p), a temporary variable. Each residual edge of p is either an edge of the original network or the reversal of one; lines 6–8 add flow in the first case and subtract it in the second.

The running time depends on the path chosen in line 3. If the capacities are irrational numbers, the path can be chosen so that the algorithm never terminates. With integer capacities and any augmenting path, the loop of lines 3–8 runs at most |f*| times, since the value increases by at least 1 each time. A path in the residual network takes O(V + E′) = O(E) with depth-first or breadth-first search, so the total is O(E |f*|).

Figure 24.7(a) shows a network where this is slow: a maximum flow has value 2,000,000, with 1,000,000 units on s → u → t and 1,000,000 on s → v → t. If the first augmenting path found is s → u → v → t, the flow has value 1 after one iteration; if the second is s → v → u → t, the value is 2. Alternating these two paths performs 2,000,000 augmentations, each increasing the flow by only 1 unit.

24.2 · cuts and the max-flow min-cut theorem

A line across the map

Draw a line across the map that keeps the depot s on one side and the shop t on the other. The vertices on s's side are S, the rest are T, and the pair is a cut (S, T). Count the trucks that cross the line towards the shop, then subtract the trucks that cross back. That is the net flow f(S, T), and it is the same whichever line you draw: it always equals the flow value |f|. Nothing is lost or made between the depot and the shop.

Now count what the roads could carry across the line. The capacity c(S, T) adds up only the roads that lead from S to T: a road pointing back can't carry anything forwards. The net flow is at most the forward flow, which is at most the capacity, so every cut caps the flow: |f| ≤ c(S, T). No flow can beat the cheapest cut.

The max-flow min-cut theorem says the cap is tight. A flow is a maximum flow exactly when the residual network has no augmenting path, and exactly when some cut has c(S, T) = |f|. The cut comes for free: let S be everything still reachable from s in Gf.

Try it: click v1 to v4 to move them between S (blue) and T (lilac), and watch the two numbers: the net flow never changes, the capacity does. Each bar is one of the 16 possible cuts, smallest first; the dashed line is |f|, and no bar dips below it. Press Count edge by edge to see the sums built. Then press Reachable from s in G_f: on flow 19 the search reaches t, so an augmenting path exists. Switch to Maximum flow and press it again: the reachable set is a cut that touches the dashed line.

What the book says

A cut (S, T) of a flow network G = (V, E) is a partition of V into S and T = V − S such that s ∈ S and t ∈ T. The net flow across the cut is f(S, T) = Σu∈S Σv∈T f(u, v) − Σu∈S Σv∈T f(v, u), and its capacity is c(S, T) = Σu∈S Σv∈T c(u, v). A minimum cut is a cut whose capacity is minimum over all cuts. Flow counts edges going in both directions across the cut, but capacity counts only edges going from the source side toward the sink side; the book calls this asymmetry intentional and important.

Figure 24.5 shows the cut ({s, v1, v2}, {v3, v4, t}) in the network of Figure 24.1(b). Its net flow is f(v1, v3) + f(v2, v4) − f(v3, v2) = 12 + 11 − 4 = 19, and its capacity is c(v1, v3) + c(v2, v4) = 12 + 14 = 26.

Lemma 24.4: for any cut (S, T) of a flow network with flow f, the net flow across it is f(S, T) = |f|. Corollary 24.5: the value of any flow is bounded from above by the capacity of any cut, so the value of a maximum flow is at most the capacity of a minimum cut.

Theorem 24.6 (max-flow min-cut theorem): for a flow f in G the following are equivalent: (1) f is a maximum flow in G; (2) the residual network Gf contains no augmenting paths; (3) |f| = c(S, T) for some cut (S, T) of G. To prove (2) ⇒ (3), let S be the set of vertices reachable from s in Gf and T = V − S. Then s ∈ S and t ∉ S. For u ∈ S and v ∈ T, an edge (u, v) of G must have f(u, v) = c(u, v), and an edge (v, u) must have f(v, u) = 0, since otherwise v would be reachable. Hence f(S, T) = c(S, T), and by Lemma 24.4 |f| = c(S, T).

24.2 · the Edmonds-Karp algorithm

Always take the shortest detour

Ford-Fulkerson lets you pick any augmenting path. Edmonds-Karp adds one rule: find the path with breadth-first search from s in the residual network, so it always has the fewest edges. That single rule removes the two-million-round disaster of the last scene: on the network of Figure 24.7 it needs just two rounds.

Why does it stay fast? Write δf(s, v) for the number of edges on a shortest path from s to v in Gf. An augmentation can push these distances up but never down (Lemma 24.7): watch the rows of the table grow. In every round at least one edge of the path is critical, the bottleneck that disappears from the residual network. For it to come back, its reverse edge must lie on a later shortest path, and that forces the distance of one end to grow by at least 2. Distances are at most |V| − 2, so each edge is critical at most |V|/2 times.

With |E| edges that is O(V E) augmentations. Each one costs a breadth-first search, O(E), so the total is O(V E2), whatever the capacities are.

Try it: press Step. First the breadth-first search runs, wave by wave (the numbers under the vertices are δ). Then the shortest path turns green and its critical edges coral, then the flow is updated and the coral edge vanishes. Each search adds a column to the table, and no row ever goes down. On Figure 24.7 compare two rounds with two million. With Random capacities the same guarantee holds for any numbers: roll again and look at the table.

What the book says

In the example of Figure 24.7 the Ford-Fulkerson algorithm never chooses the augmenting path with the fewest edges, and the book says it should have. By using breadth-first search to find an augmenting path in the residual network, the algorithm runs in polynomial time, independent of the maximum flow value. The Ford-Fulkerson method implemented this way is called the Edmonds-Karp algorithm, and the book proves that it runs in O(V E2) time.

The analysis depends on the distances δf(u, v), the shortest-path distance from u to v in Gf where each edge has unit distance. Lemma 24.7: if the Edmonds-Karp algorithm is run on a flow network with source s and sink t, then for all vertices v ∈ V − {s, t} the shortest-path distance δf(s, v) in the residual network increases monotonically with each flow augmentation.

Theorem 24.8: the total number of flow augmentations performed by the algorithm is O(V E). An edge (u, v) in Gf is critical on an augmenting path p if cf(p) = cf(u, v). After the flow is augmented, any critical edge on the path disappears from the residual network, and at least one edge of any augmenting path is critical. The proof shows each of the |E| edges can become critical at most |V|/2 times: from one time it is critical to the next, the distance of u from the source increases by at least 2, and a shortest path from s to u has at most |V| − 2 edges.

Each iteration takes O(E) time when breadth-first search finds the augmenting path, so the total running time of the Edmonds-Karp algorithm is O(V E2). The breadth-first-search listing under the machine is the one from Section 20.2.

24.3 · maximum bipartite matching

Machines, jobs and a water pipe

A workshop has five machines and four jobs. An edge joins a machine to a job it can do, and nobody doubles up: each machine takes at most one job and each job gets at most one machine. Such a choice of edges is a matching, and a maximum matching keeps as many machines busy as possible. The graph is bipartite: every edge runs between the left side L (machines) and the right side R (jobs).

Now turn it into plumbing. Add a source s with a pipe to every machine and a sink t with a pipe from every job, point each original edge from L to R, and give every pipe capacity 1. One unit of flow runs s → machine → job → t and uses up that machine and that job, so a flow is a matching and the flow value |f| counts the pairs. When Ford-Fulkerson later finds a better route that walks backwards over a pipe already carrying flow, that is a machine giving its job away so that another machine can have it.

Why is the answer always whole pairs and never half a machine? With integer capacities the Ford-Fulkerson method only ever makes integer flows (the integrality theorem). At most min(|L|, |R|) = O(V) units can flow and each augmentation costs O(E), so the whole matching costs O(VE).

Try it: press Step once to build the flow network from the graph, then keep stepping: each augmenting path is found, then its edges are updated one by one (blue = 1 unit of flow). Watch for the path that walks backwards over a blue edge, drawn in red: that edge loses its flow. Play runs it all. Before you start, click a machine and then a job to add or remove that edge, or click an edge to remove it, or try the other graph.

What the book says

A matching in an undirected graph G = (V, E) is a subset M of the edges such that every vertex has at most one edge of M incident on it; a vertex is matched if some edge of M touches it. A maximum matching is a matching of maximum cardinality. The section restricts itself to bipartite graphs, where V = L ∪ R and every edge goes between L and R, and assumes every vertex has at least one incident edge. Its example matches a set of machines with a set of tasks: an edge (u, v) says machine u can perform task v.

The corresponding flow network G′ = (V′, E′) has two new vertices s and t. Its edges are (s, u) for every u in L, the edges of E directed from L to R, and (v, t) for every v in R, all with unit capacity. Since every vertex has an incident edge, |E| ≥ |V|/2, so |E′| = |E| + |V| ≤ 3|E| and |E′| = Θ(E).

Lemma 24.9 says that a matching M in G gives an integer-valued flow in G′ of value |M| (one unit along s → u → v → t for each matched edge, vertex-disjoint except at s and t), and conversely an integer-valued flow gives a matching of the edges that carry positive flow, with |M| = |f|. The catch is that a maximum-flow algorithm might return fractional flows. Theorem 24.10, the integrality theorem, rules that out for the Ford-Fulkerson method when the capacities are integers; its proof is induction on the iterations (Exercise 24.3-2).

Corollary 24.11 follows: the cardinality of a maximum matching in G equals the value of a maximum flow in G′. So build G′, run Ford-Fulkerson, and read off the matching. Any matching in a bipartite graph has at most min(|L|, |R|) = O(V) edges, so the maximum flow is O(V) and the method takes O(VE′) = O(VE) time. Section 25.1 gives an algorithm designed for this problem.

problem 24-1 · the escape problem

Everyone out of the grid

A town is a square grid of crossings, and some people are standing on some of them. Can every one of them walk to the edge of the town so that no two people ever use the same crossing, and no two leave through the same edge crossing? That is the escape problem: m starting points, m vertex-disjoint paths to m different boundary points.

It smells like maximum flow: give every street capacity 1 and push m units from a source s into the starting points, out of the boundary into a sink t. But a flow network limits edges, and here the limit sits on crossings. The cure is to split every crossing v into vin and vout joined by one edge of capacity 1. Streets enter at vin and leave from vout, so the capacity of the crossing is now the capacity of an ordinary edge. Everyone can escape exactly when the maximum flow is m.

The machine lets you see why the split is needed. Without it, a crossing with four streets can carry two walkers through different streets, and the flow cheerfully reports an escape that does not exist.

Try it: on Surrounded with Split vertices, press Play: four walkers get out and the fifth, in the middle, is boxed in by the others, so the flow stops at 4 of 5. Now press Reset, switch to Edges only and play again: the flow reaches 5, but a crossing turns red because two walkers share it. On Escape everyone gets out in both modes. Before you start, click a crossing to add or remove a starting point.

What the book says

An n × n grid is an undirected graph with n rows and n columns of vertices; (i, j) is the vertex in row i and column j. Every vertex has exactly four neighbours except the boundary vertices, those with i = 1, i = n, j = 1 or j = n.

Given m ≤ n² starting points, the escape problem is to determine whether there are m vertex-disjoint paths from the starting points to any m different points on the boundary. In the book's Figure 24.9, one grid has an escape, shown by paths, and the other has none.

Part (a) asks you to consider a flow network in which vertices as well as edges have capacities, so that the total positive flow entering a vertex is subject to a capacity constraint, and to show how to reduce maximum flow in such a network to an ordinary maximum-flow problem on a network of comparable size. Part (b) asks for an efficient algorithm for the escape problem and its running time. The book leaves both parts to the reader; the vertex splitting in the machine is one standard answer to part (a), not the book's text.

problem 24-2 · minimum path cover

Fewest vans to visit every depot

A courier company has six depots joined by one-way roads, and the roads never lead back to where they started. Vans may start and stop at any depot, but every depot must be visited by exactly one van. What is the smallest fleet? The vans' routes are vertex-disjoint paths that use every vertex once, a path cover; the fewest paths make a minimum path cover. A van may park at a single depot, a path of length 0.

Count links instead of vans. With no links you need one van per depot, and every link i → j that you use joins two vans into one. So fewest vans means most links. A set of links is legal when no depot has two outgoing links or two incoming links, and that is exactly a matching between an out-copy xi and an in-copy yj for each road i → j. Section 24.3 already solves that with a flow: a source x0 into every xi, every yi into a sink y0, unit capacities. The answer is n − |M| vans.

The roads matter. If they may form a cycle, the matching can chain links all the way round it, and a cycle is not a path. The count n − |M| then still comes out, but the paths it counts no longer cover every depot.

Try it: press Step to build the network on the right from the roads on the left, and keep stepping: each augmenting path adds links (blue), and a path that runs backwards over a link takes it back (red). Then step through M, the paths and the count. Now press Reset, pick With a cycle (it adds the road 6 → 1) and step again: three depots end up on a cycle that no van reaches.

What the book says

A path cover of a directed graph G = (V, E) is a set P of vertex-disjoint paths such that every vertex in V is included in exactly one path in P. Paths may start and end anywhere, and may be of any length, including 0. A minimum path cover is a path cover containing the fewest possible paths.

Part (a) asks for an efficient algorithm that finds a minimum path cover of a directed acyclic graph. The hint: assuming V = {1, 2, …, n}, build a flow network from the graph G′ = (V′, E′) with V′ = {x0, …, xn} ∪ {y0, …, yn} and E′ = {(x0, xi) : i ∈ V} ∪ {(yi, y0) : i ∈ V} ∪ {(xi, yj) : (i, j) ∈ E}, and run a maximum-flow algorithm. Part (b) asks whether the algorithm works for directed graphs that contain cycles, and to explain.

The book states no capacities and does not say how to read the paths off the flow. The machine gives every edge capacity 1 and follows the matched edges xi → yj, which is the reading Section 24.3's matching reduction suggests; this is the page's own illustration, not the book's text.

The chapter on one page

Chapter 24 in eight cards. Each one points at the scenes that act it out.

Pipes with limits

A flow network is a directed graph with a capacity c(u, v) ≥ 0 on every edge, a source s and a sink t. A flow f keeps 0 ≤ f(u, v) ≤ c(u, v) and, at every vertex except s and t, flow in equals flow out. Its value |f| is what leaves s minus what enters it, and the maximum-flow problem asks for the largest. → 24.1 Flow networks

Making a network legal

Two edges (u, v) and (v, u) are antiparallel; splitting one of them with a new vertex x removes the pair and keeps the answer. Several sources and sinks become one of each by adding a supersource and a supersink joined by edges of capacity ∞. → 24.1 Flow rules

What can still change

The residual network Gf has a forward edge with room c − f and a reverse edge with capacity f for every edge that has either, so |Ef| ≤ 2|E|. Sending flow along a reverse edge cancels flow, and augmenting f by a flow f′ in Gf gives a flow of value |f| + |f′|. → 24.2 Residual networks

One more trip

An augmenting path is a simple path from s to t in Gf. Its residual capacity cf(p) is the smallest residual capacity on it, the tightest leg. Pushing that much along the path keeps every rule and raises the flow value by exactly cf(p). → 24.2 Augmenting paths

Keep augmenting

The Ford-Fulkerson method starts at flow 0 and augments along any path until none is left. With integer capacities it needs at most |f*| rounds of O(E) work, O(E |f*|), which is poor when |f*| is huge: a middle edge of capacity 1 between two roads of 1,000,000 can cost 2,000,000 augmentations. → 24.2 Ford-Fulkerson

A line across the map

A cut (S, T) splits the vertices with s in S and t in T. The net flow across it is |f| for every cut, the capacity counts only edges from S to T, so |f| ≤ c(S, T). The max-flow min-cut theorem says three things are equivalent: f is maximum, Gf has no augmenting path, and |f| = c(S, T) for some cut. → 24.2 Max-flow min-cut

Shortest path first

Choosing each augmenting path by breadth-first search, the fewest edges, is the Edmonds-Karp algorithm. Shortest-path distances to every vertex never decrease in Gf, each edge becomes critical at most |V|/2 times, so there are O(VE) augmentations and the total is O(VE²), whatever the capacities. → 24.2 Edmonds-Karp

Matching, grids and vans

A maximum bipartite matching is a maximum flow in a network with unit capacities, s → L → R → t, found in O(VE) because integer capacities give integer flows. Splitting a vertex in two gives it a capacity (the escape problem), and a matching of size |M| covers a dag with n − |M| paths. → 24.3 Bipartite matching, 24-1 Escape problem, 24-2 Path cover

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

flow network G = (V, E)
A directed graph where each edge has a nonnegative capacity. It has no edge in both directions between a pair of vertices, no self-loops, and every vertex lies on some path from s to t.
capacity c(u, v)
The most that can go along the edge (u, v). It is 0 when there is no such edge.
source s, sink t
The vertex where the material is produced and the vertex where it is consumed.
flow f
A number f(u, v) for every pair of vertices that obeys the capacity constraint and flow conservation.
capacity constraint
0 ≤ f(u, v) ≤ c(u, v): no negative flow, and no more than the edge can carry.
flow conservation
At every vertex except s and t, the flow in equals the flow out, so nothing piles up.
value |f|
The flow out of the source minus the flow into it. Equation (24.1).
maximum-flow problem
Given a flow network, find a flow of the greatest value.
antiparallel edges
An edge (u, v) and its reverse (v, u). A flow network doesn't allow them; splitting one edge with a new vertex removes them.
supersource, supersink
A new source joined to every real source, and a new sink joined from every real sink, by edges of capacity ∞. They turn many sources and sinks into one of each.
residual network Gf
The edges that could still carry something: cf(u, v) = c − f on an edge with room, f(v, u) on the reverse of an edge with flow. Edges with residual capacity 0 are left out.
cancellation
Pushing flow along a reverse edge of Gf, which lowers the flow on the original edge. Sending 5 crates one way and 2 the other is the same as sending 3.
augmentation f ↑ f′
Adding a flow f′ found in Gf to f: f(u, v) + f′(u, v) − f′(v, u). The result is a flow of value |f| + |f′|.
augmenting path
A simple path from s to t in the residual network.
cf(p)
The residual capacity of a path: the minimum residual capacity of its edges. The most that can be added along the path.
cut (S, T)
A split of the vertices into S and T = V − S with s in S and t in T.
f(S, T) and c(S, T)
The net flow across a cut counts flow in both directions; the capacity counts only edges from S to T. The net flow is always |f|, and the capacity is always at least that.
minimum cut
A cut of least capacity. Its capacity is an upper bound on the value of any flow.
max-flow min-cut theorem
Theorem 24.6: f is a maximum flow, Gf has no augmenting path, and |f| = c(S, T) for some cut are all the same statement.
Ford-Fulkerson method
Start with flow 0 and augment along augmenting paths until none is left. A method, not an algorithm, because it doesn't say how to pick the path.
Edmonds-Karp
Ford-Fulkerson with the augmenting path found by breadth-first search. It runs in O(VE²) time.
critical edge
An edge whose residual capacity equals the residual capacity of the augmenting path, so it disappears from Gf after the augmentation.
matching, bipartite graph
A set of edges no two of which share a vertex. In a bipartite graph the vertices split into L and R, and every edge joins one to the other.
integrality theorem
With integer capacities, Ford-Fulkerson returns a maximum flow whose value and every edge flow are integers. This is why a flow of unit capacities is a matching.

Rust words from the code panels

For readers coming from another language. Each one shows up in a listing above.

Vec<Vec<i64>> as a matrix
The capacity and flow tables are an outer Vec of row Vecs, each its own heap block. cap[u][v] is two hops, a row is contiguous and a column is not, and vec![vec![0; n]; n] builds an all-zero one where 0 means no edge.
i64 and i64::MAX / 4
Flows and residuals are signed, because a flow below 0 has to exist to be rejected. For ∞ a quarter of the range leaves room to add capacities without overflowing.
&[T] and &mut
A procedure that only reads the capacities takes &[Vec<i64>]; one that changes the flow in place takes &mut. Two sequential writes, flow[u][v] += b then flow[v][u] -= b, are fine.
enum with data, match
Broken::Capacity(u, v) and Broken::Conservation(u) say which rule failed and where; match takes the data out of whichever variant it finds.
struct, type alias
struct Edge { u, v, cap, flow } names the fields, so e.cap reads better than e.2; type Net = Vec<Vec<i64>> gives a long type a short name.
iter_mut, flatten, sum
cap.iter_mut() lends each row mutably; iter().flatten().filter(…).count() counts edges over every cell; iter().sum::<i64>() adds a row.
find returns Option
iter().find(|r| …) gives Some(item) or None. None is a natural way to say "not an edge", the book's residual capacity 0.
windows(2) and min
path.windows(2) slides over a path two vertices at a time, one edge per window; .min() gives the bottleneck as an Option, None only for an empty path.
Option<Vec<usize>> for a path
A search returns Some(path) when it reaches t and None when it can't. Inside it, dist[t]? hands a None straight back to the caller, and the caller owns the path it gets.
closures that borrow
A closure that only reads cap and flow needs no move. A closure that outlives its creator needs move to take its own copy.
recursion with &mut state
Depth-first search passes the same seen and path buffers down every call, so the recursion stack is the path so far and path.pop() backs up from a dead end.
index arithmetic for split vertices
Crossing v becomes nodes 2 * v and 2 * v + 1, with one edge of capacity 1 between them: a vertex capacity made from an ordinary edge.
