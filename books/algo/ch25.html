Matchings in Bipartite Graphs

A matching pairs up the vertices of a graph so that nobody is used twice. This chapter finds the largest matching in a bipartite graph by swapping along augmenting paths, then asks for pairings nobody wants to leave and for the pairing of greatest total weight.

“Many real-world problems can be modeled as finding matchings in an undirected graph.” — Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest & Stein

25.1 · matchings in bipartite graphs

Pairs that never share

You have interview slots to fill and candidates who can only come at certain times. Put the candidates on the left, the slots on the right, and draw a line when a candidate is free for that slot. A schedule picks some lines so that nobody is booked twice and no slot holds two people. That is a matching: a set of edges M in which every vertex has at most one incident edge. Because every line joins a left vertex to a right one, the graph is bipartite.

A vertex with a chosen edge is matched; the others are unmatched, or free. A matching is maximal when no further edge can be added, and maximum when no matching anywhere in the graph is bigger. Every maximum matching is maximal, but a maximal one can fall well short. A matching is perfect when every vertex is matched, which needs |L| = |R|.

The quickest plan is greedy: walk down L and give each vertex any free neighbour. It never undoes a choice, so an early choice can strand a later vertex, and the result is only maximal. The book asks you to show it is always at least half the size of a maximum matching. Finding a true maximum takes the rest of this section.

Try it: click edges to build a matching. An edge whose end is already matched is refused, and the edge in the way flashes. Press Show a maximum matching for the benchmark, then Step or Play to run greedy from the empty set: it stops at 6 of 7. Switch to Greedy's trap: with the lowest right vertex first it finds 2 of a possible 4, and with the highest first the same procedure finds all 4.

What the book says

For an undirected graph G = (V, E), a matching is a subset of edges M ⊆ E such that every vertex in V has at most one incident edge in M. The chapter opens with a scheduling story: candidates and time slots are the vertices of a bipartite graph, an edge means the candidate is available then, and the goal is a maximum matching, one of maximum cardinality. One of the authors used the Hopcroft-Karp algorithm of Section 25.1 to schedule interviews for teaching assistants.

The algorithms in this chapter take a bipartite graph with V = L ∪ R, the vertex sets L and R disjoint, and every edge incident on one vertex in L and one in R. A matching therefore matches vertices in L with vertices in R; the two sets may or may not have equal size. Figure 25.1 draws such a graph with |L| = 7 and |R| = 8.

A vertex that has an incident edge in the matching M is matched under M, and otherwise it is unmatched. A maximal matching is a matching to which no other edges can be added. “A maximum matching is always maximal, but the reverse does not always hold.” A perfect matching, defined in Exercise 25.1-5, is a matching under which every vertex is matched.

In Section 25.3 the book gives a greedy way to start: GREEDY-BIPARTITE-MATCHING goes through the vertices of L and, whenever one has an unmatched neighbour in R, adds the edge to any such neighbour. It describes this as a greedy method for finding a maximal bipartite matching, and Exercise 25.3-2 asks you to show that it returns a matching at least half the size of a maximum matching.

25.1 · augmenting paths and alternating paths

Swap along the path

Say a matching M is not the best you can do. Pick a free vertex on the left and walk: take an edge outside M, then an edge of M, then outside, then in, and so on. That is an M-alternating path. If it starts and ends with edges outside M, both ends are free, and the path is M-augmenting. It has one more edge outside M than inside, so its length is odd.

An augmenting path is a free upgrade. Throw out the path's edges that are in M, take in the ones that are not: M′ = M ⊕ P, the symmetric difference. Every vertex on the path stays matched, the two free ends become matched, and the matching grows by exactly one. The converse holds too (Berge): M is a maximum matching if and only if no augmenting path exists.

So searching is the whole algorithm. Run a breadth-first search from a free left vertex that may only alternate: edges outside M from L to R, an edge in M from R back to L. Reaching a free right vertex means success. Each search costs O(E) and the matching grows at most |V|/2 times, so the total is O(VE).

Try it: in Figure 25.1 press Step twice: the book's five-edge path appears, then flips, and |M| goes from 4 to 5. Keep stepping to watch the search find short paths on its own. From scratch builds a maximum matching from nothing. In Stuck, l2 and r3 are both free but no alternating path joins them, so the matching is already maximum.

What the book says

Given a matching M in an undirected graph, an M-alternating path is a simple path whose edges alternate between being in M and being in E − M. An M-augmenting path is an M-alternating path whose first and last edges belong to E − M. Since it contains one more edge in E − M than in M, it must consist of an odd number of edges. Figure 25.1(b) shows one, between l6 and r8.

The symmetric difference of sets is X ⊕ Y = (X − Y) ∪ (Y − X): the elements that belong to X or Y, but not both. Lemma 25.1 says that if P is an M-augmenting path, then M′ = M ⊕ P is also a matching, with |M′| = |M| + 1. Corollary 25.2 extends this to k vertex-disjoint augmenting paths, which add k edges.

For two matchings M and M*, Lemma 25.3 shows that M ⊕ M* is a disjoint union of simple paths, simple cycles and isolated vertices whose edges alternate between the two, and that if |M*| > |M| it contains at least |M*| − |M| vertex-disjoint M-augmenting paths. Corollary 25.4 follows: a matching M is a maximum matching if and only if the graph contains no M-augmenting path. The chapter notes credit it to Berge.

The book then sketches an algorithm: “Start with the matching M empty. Then repeatedly run a variant of either breadth-first search or depth-first search from an unmatched vertex that takes alternating paths until you find another unmatched vertex.” The resulting augmenting path increases the size of M by 1, and the whole algorithm runs in O(VE) time. The book gives no pseudocode for it, so the listing below is a sketch of the loop and the search.

25.1 · the Hopcroft–Karp algorithm

Many paths at once

One search per augmenting path is slow: every search costs O(E) and may add a single edge. Hopcroft–Karp flips a whole batch per round. It finds a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths, and because the paths share no vertex, flipping all of them adds one edge for each. A round has three phases, and all three together cost only O(E).

First, direct the graph: edges outside M point from L to R, edges in M from R back to L. Second, run BFS from all free left vertices at once. Each vertex gets a layer, its distance, and q is the layer of the nearest free right vertex. Keep only layers 0 to q and the edges between neighbouring layers: a dag H whose layer-0-to-q paths are exactly the shortest augmenting paths. Third, run DFS from each free right vertex in layer q backwards through H towards layer 0, never searching from a vertex that an earlier search already discovered.

The set found is only maximal, and that is enough. After a round the shortest augmenting path is strictly longer, and once paths are about √V long few can remain. So the loop runs fewer than 2√V times: O(√V · E) in all.

Try it: press Step through Figure 25.2 and 25.3: arrows appear, layers ripple out, the graph slides into layers, then DFS runs right to left. Watch r4 skip l3 (already discovered) and r6 get stuck. Switch the order to Lowest first and Play again: the first round finds 3 paths instead of 2. Then try Big random graph and press Run: the first round finds hundreds of paths, and later rounds only a handful.

What the book says

The Hopcroft-Karp algorithm runs in O(√V E) time. HOPCROFT-KARP starts with M = ∅ and repeats: let P = {P1, …, Pk} be a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest M-augmenting paths, and set M = M ⊕ (P1 ∪ ⋯ ∪ Pk), until P is empty. Corollary 25.2 makes each round grow M, and Corollary 25.4 makes the result correct, since the algorithm stops when there are no augmenting paths.

Line 3 takes O(E) time in three phases. The first forms the directed graph GM, directing edges outside M from L to R and edges in M from R to L. The second creates a dag H by a variant of breadth-first search started from all the unmatched vertices of L. The third finds the paths with a variant of depth-first search on the transpose HT.

Each vertex of H lies in the layer given by its minimum breadth-first distance from an unmatched vertex in L. Vertices in L appear in even-numbered layers and vertices in R in odd-numbered layers. If q is the smallest distance of an unmatched vertex in R, the last layer holds the vertices of R at distance q, and vertices farther away are left out: in Figure 25.2, l7 and r8 are omitted because their distances exceed q = 3. Every path in H from layer 0 to an unmatched vertex in layer q is a shortest M-augmenting path, and every shortest one is present.

For each unmatched vertex r in layer q, the third phase runs a depth-first search from r until it reaches layer 0 or runs out of paths, and each vertex is searched from only when it is first discovered in any search. In Figure 25.3 the search from r1 finds ⟨(r1, l3), (l3, r3), (r3, l1)⟩; the search from r4 examines (r4, l3), backtracks because l3 was already discovered, and finds ⟨(r4, l5), (l5, r7), (r7, l6)⟩; the search from r6 gets stuck; and there is no search from r5, which is matched. The set of two paths is maximal but not maximum, since the graph holds three vertex-disjoint shortest paths, and the algorithm needs only a maximal set.

For the bound, Lemma 25.5 shows the shortest augmenting path gets longer after every iteration, and Lemma 25.6 shows a maximum matching has at most |M| + |V|/(q + 1) edges when the shortest augmenting path has q edges. Together these give fewer than 2√|V| iterations, and Theorem 25.8 states the O(√V E) bound. The chapter notes add that Madry later gave an algorithm that is asymptotically faster for sparse graphs.

25.2 · the stable-marriage problem

Two people who would rather run off together

Four women and four men each rank everyone on the other side, best first. We pair them off, one woman with one man. Everybody has a partner, so is everybody content? Not necessarily. If Karen would rather be with Davis than with her own partner, and Davis would rather be with Karen than with his, those two can walk away from their partners and both come out ahead.

That pair is a blocking pair: two people who are not matched to each other, but each rank the other above their current partner. A matching with no blocking pair is stable. Stable does not mean that everyone gets a first choice. It means that nobody has a reason to leave. In the machine, the yellow cells in each list are the people ranked above that person's partner, so a pair blocks exactly when each of the two stands in the other's yellow cells.

Does a stable matching always exist, and is there only one? The next machine runs an algorithm that always finds one. This one shows that there can be several: switch to three women and three men and find all three.

Try it: the matching starts as the book's unstable one. Click Karen, then Davis, and read why that pair blocks. Press Pair them up and the blocking pair disappears (Emma and Oscar are paired by the swap). Press Find blocking pairs to watch the check run line by line. Then switch to 3 women, 3 men and use the three matching buttons: each of them is stable, while three of the six possible matchings are not.

What the book says

Section 25.1 found maximum matchings in a bipartite graph. Section 25.2 starts from a complete bipartite graph, with an edge from every vertex in L to every vertex in R, and adds a different kind of information from the weights of Section 25.3: each vertex ranks the vertices on the other side. Every vertex in L has an ordered list of all the vertices in R, and vice versa, with n vertices in each set. The goal is to match each vertex of L with one of R in a stable way.

The problem takes its name from marriage, with L as a set of women and R as a set of men, who each rank everyone in the other group. The matching should be such that if a woman and a man are not matched to each other, at least one of them prefers their assigned partner. If they are not matched to each other but each prefers the other over their assigned partner, they form a blocking pair. A stable matching is a matching that has no blocking pair; if there is a blocking pair, the matching is unstable.

The book's example has four women (Wanda, Emma, Lacey and Karen) and four men (Oscar, Davis, Brent and Hank). The stable matching pairs Lacey with Brent, Wanda with Hank, Karen with Davis and Emma with Oscar, and it is unique. Karen prefers Brent and Hank to her partner Davis, but Brent prefers his partner Lacey to Karen and Hank prefers his partner Wanda, so neither forms a blocking pair. If the last two pairs were Emma with Davis and Karen with Oscar instead, Karen and Davis would be a blocking pair, because Karen prefers Davis to Oscar and Davis prefers Karen to Emma.

Stable matchings need not be unique. With three women (Monica, Phoebe and Rachel) and three men (Chandler, Joey and Ross) the book finds three. In matching 1 all the women get their first choice and all the men their last; matching 2 is the opposite; in matching 3 everyone gets their second choice. A stable matching always exists, whatever rankings are given, and the Gale-Shapley algorithm finds one. The chapter's opening names the U.S. National Resident Matching Program, which matches medical students to hospitals, as perhaps the best-known real-world example.

25.2 · the Gale–Shapley algorithm

Ask, hold, trade up

Everyone starts free. A free woman proposes to the first man on her list that she has not tried yet. If he is free, they become engaged. If he is engaged but prefers her to his fiancée, he drops the fiancée, who becomes free again, and accepts the new proposal. Otherwise he turns her down and she stays free, ready for the next name on her list. A man who has been proposed to once stays engaged for good, though he may trade up to someone he likes better.

Why does it stop? Each woman proposes to each man at most once, so there are at most n² proposals. And why is the result stable? Suppose a woman w ends up with m but would prefer m′. She proposed to m′ first, so m′ either refused her or dropped her for someone he likes better, and a man only ever moves up. So m′ does not prefer w to his final partner, and (w, m′) is never a blocking pair. This is the Gale–Shapley algorithm.

Nothing says which free woman should go next, and it turns out not to matter: every order ends with the same matching, the best partner each woman can have in any stable matching. The same matching is the worst each man can have. Let the men propose instead and the favours flip.

Try it: press Step twice for one proposal and its answer, or Next proposal for both at once, or Play. Before a step you can click any yellow circle to choose who proposes next, because line 2 allows any free woman, and the final matching will not change. Then switch to 3 women, 3 men and Play: every woman gets her first choice. Press Men propose and Play again: a different stable matching appears, with every man getting his first choice.

What the book says

A simple algorithm known as the Gale-Shapley algorithm always finds a stable matching. It has two variants that mirror each other, woman-oriented and man-oriented, and the book examines the woman-oriented one. Everyone starts out free. Engagements occur when a free woman proposes to a man. When a man is first proposed to he goes from free to engaged, and he always stays engaged, though not necessarily to the same woman. If an engaged man receives a proposal from a woman whom he prefers to his current fiancée, that engagement is broken, the other woman becomes free, and he and the new woman become engaged. A woman who is engaged temporarily stops proposing, but if she becomes free again she continues down her list. For the man-oriented version, reverse the roles of men and women.

The procedure GALE-SHAPLEY has ten lines: assign everyone as free; while some woman is free, let her propose to the first man on her list to whom she has not proposed; if he is free they become engaged; otherwise, if he ranks her higher than the woman he is engaged to, he breaks that engagement and the two become engaged; else he rejects her and she remains free; finally return the engaged pairs. The procedure allows some choice: any free woman may be selected in line 2, and it produces a stable matching regardless of the order.

On the four-woman example the book shows one possible run of nine iterations. Wanda proposes to Brent, Emma to Davis, Lacey to Brent (Brent drops Wanda), Karen to Brent (rejected) and then to Hank, Wanda to Hank (Hank drops Karen), Karen to Davis (Davis drops Emma), Emma to Hank (rejected) and finally to Oscar. Then everyone is engaged and the procedure returns the stable matching seen earlier.

Theorem 25.9 says that GALE-SHAPLEY always terminates and returns a stable matching, so a stable matching always exists. The loop makes at most n² iterations, since each woman goes through her ranking in order, and Corollary 25.10 says the algorithm can be implemented to run in O(n²) time. Theorem 25.11 says that however line 2 chooses the women, the procedure returns the same stable matching, and in it each woman has the best partner possible in any stable matching. Corollary 25.12 follows: there can be stable matchings that the procedure does not return, as in the three-woman example. Corollary 25.13 says that each man gets the worst partner possible in any stable matching.

25.3 · the assignment problem

Who should do which job?

You have n workers and n tasks, and a number for every worker and task: how effective that worker is at that task. Give every worker exactly one task and every task exactly one worker. Which assignment makes the total effectiveness as large as possible? In graph terms the workers are the vertices l of L, the tasks are the vertices r of R, every pair is an edge with a weight w(l, r), and you want a perfect matching of maximum total weight. That is the assignment problem.

The brute-force answer is to list all n! perfect matchings and keep the best. That is fine for 5 workers (120 matchings) and for 7 (5,040), but 10 workers already give 3,628,800, and 20 workers give about 2.4 × 10¹⁸. The Hungarian algorithm of the next two machines is far faster: its bound is O(n⁴), against which 20⁴ is only 160,000.

The goal can be turned around. To minimise the total instead of maximising it, the book asks in Exercise 25.3-6 how to modify the algorithm. Flip the goal below and see how the best matching changes: minimising the weights is the same as maximising their negatives.

Try it: click one cell in a row to give that worker that task. Click a cell in a column that is already taken and the task changes hands. Build a full assignment (one cell in every row and column) and read your total. Then press Try all matchings to watch the brute-force scan, and compare your total with the best. Change the size to see how fast n! grows next to n⁴, press Minimise, or draw New weights. The first weights are the top-left corner of the matrix in the book's Figure 25.4, which the Hungarian machines will solve in full.

What the book says

The chapter's opening describes an assignment problem as one where workers must be assigned to tasks in order to maximize the overall effectiveness of the assignment. For each worker and each task, the worker has some quantified effectiveness for that task. Assuming there are equal numbers of workers and tasks, the goal is to find a matching with the maximum total effectiveness.

Section 25.3 starts from a complete bipartite graph G = (V, E) with V = L ∪ R, where L and R each contain n vertices, so the graph has n² edges. Each edge (l, r) has a weight w(l, r) that represents the utility gained by matching l with r. The goal is a perfect matching M* whose total weight w(M) = Σ w(l, r) over (l, r) in M is the maximum over all perfect matchings. Finding such a maximum-weight perfect matching is called the assignment problem.

Like the stable-marriage problem, the assignment problem finds a matching that is good, but with a different definition of good: maximizing total value rather than achieving stability. You could enumerate all n! perfect matchings, but the Hungarian algorithm solves it much faster. The section proves an O(n⁴) bound, and Problem 25-2 asks you to refine the algorithm to O(n³).

The exercises ask how to modify the Hungarian algorithm to find a matching that minimizes rather than maximizes the sum of the edge weights (Exercise 25.3-6), and how to handle an assignment problem with |L| ≠ |R| (Exercise 25.3-7).

25.3 · vertex labels and the equality subgraph

A cap that a perfect matching can touch

Pin a number to every worker and every job: its label h. The only rule is that for every pair the two labels add up to at least the pair's payoff, l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r). Labels that obey it are a feasible vertex labeling. One always exists: give each worker the best payoff in its row and each job 0.

Now keep only the pairs where the labels add up to exactly the payoff. They make the equality subgraph Gh. Here is the trick. A perfect matching uses every vertex once, and every pair is worth at most its two labels, so no perfect matching can weigh more than the sum of all labels. If Gh contains a perfect matching, every one of its edges is tight, so it weighs exactly that sum: it reaches the cap, and nothing can beat it.

So you do not have to compare 5,040 matchings. You have to find labels whose equality subgraph has a perfect matching, and the book says it does not matter which labels, only that some do. The next machine changes them on purpose until that happens.

Try it: click vertices to pick a set, then press − 1 or + 1 to move all their labels. A pair whose labels fall below its payoff turns coral: the labeling is no longer feasible. Start from Default labels: Gh has no perfect matching, and the gap between the labels' sum and the matching's weight is the labels of the unmatched vertices. Try to repair it by hand, or press Book's final labels to see a perfect matching fit. Build Gh replays the double loop that finds the tight pairs.

What the book says

The setting is a complete bipartite graph G = (V, E) with V = L ∪ R, |L| = |R| = n, and a weight w(l, r) on every edge. The assignment problem asks for a perfect matching of maximum total weight. The Hungarian algorithm works not on G but on a subgraph called the equality subgraph, which changes over time and has the property that any perfect matching in it is also an optimal solution.

The equality subgraph depends on an attribute h, the label of a vertex. The labeling is feasible if l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r) for all l ∈ L and r ∈ R. A feasible labeling always exists, such as the default labeling l.h = max {w(l, r) : r ∈ R} (equation 25.1) and r.h = 0 (equation 25.2). Given a feasible labeling h, the equality subgraph Gh = (V, Eh) has the same vertices and the edges with l.h + r.h = w(l, r).

Theorem 25.14: if Gh contains a perfect matching M*, then M* is an optimal solution to the assignment problem on G. The proof writes w(M*) as the sum of l.h + r.h over its edges, which is the sum of all the labels because M* is perfect. For any perfect matching M the inequality gives w(M) ≤ the same sum (equation 25.3).

The book then stresses the freedom this gives: “the weight of any matching is always at most the sum of the vertex labels.” The algorithm is free to choose an equality subgraph, and to change which one it uses as it goes, as long as it ends with a perfect matching in some equality subgraph. If the labels are the “right” ones, a maximum-cardinality matching in the equality subgraph is also a maximum-weight perfect matching.

25.3 · the Hungarian algorithm

Nudge the labels until a perfect matching fits

Seven workers, seven jobs, and a payoff w(l, r) for every pairing. You want everyone placed and the total payoff as high as possible. The Hungarian algorithm gives every worker and every job a number, its label h, under one rule: for every pair, l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r). Only the pairs where the two labels add up to exactly the payoff count; they form the equality subgraph Gh. The start is the default of the last machine: each worker takes the best payoff in its row, each job takes 0. A greedy matching inside Gh gives the first M.

Then it loops. Search Gh for an augmenting path with a breadth-first search that starts from every unmatched worker at once. It walks from workers to jobs along unmatched edges and from jobs back to workers along matched ones. Found: flip the path, M ⊕ P, and one more pair is matched. Not found: the search ran dry. Take the forest F it grew and let δ be the smallest slack of an edge from a searched worker to an unsearched job. Lower the labels of the searched workers by δ and raise the labels of the searched jobs by δ. Edges inside the forest keep their sums and so does every matched edge, so nothing already won is lost, yet at least one new edge becomes allowed. The search carries on.

Why is the end right? The sum of all labels is always at least the weight of any perfect matching, and each label update lowers that sum while each augmentation raises the weight of M. When M is perfect in Gh the two numbers meet, and that M is optimal. There are at most n augmentations and each search costs O(n3), so the basic algorithm runs in O(n4).

Try it: press Step to walk the pseudocode line by line, Next vertex for one trip round the repeat loop, or Next phase to jump to the next big event: the queue running dry, new edges, a path. When the queue empties, watch the grid: the blue block is every edge the search could still use, and the sun-coloured cell is where δ comes from. Keep an eye on Σ labels falling and w(M) rising until they meet. Empty start begins with no matching; Random matrix gives another input.

What the book says

The Hungarian algorithm starts with any feasible vertex labeling h and any matching M in the equality subgraph Gh. It repeatedly finds an M-augmenting path P in Gh, replaces M by M ⊕ P and so grows the matching by one edge, until a perfect matching is achieved. Four questions arise: which labeling to start with (the default labeling of equations 25.1 and 25.2), which matching (“any matching, even an empty matching, but a greedy maximal matching works well”), how to find an augmenting path, and what to do when that search fails (update the labeling to bring in at least one new edge).

To find a path, the algorithm forms the directed equality subgraph GM,h: edges of M are directed from R to L and all other edges of Gh from L to R. An M-augmenting path starts at an unmatched vertex in L, ends at an unmatched vertex in R, and alternates. The search is a breadth-first search whose queue starts with all the unmatched vertices in L and which stops on first discovering an unmatched vertex in R. Each vertex needs only one predecessor, so the search grows a breadth-first forest F with the unmatched vertices of L as its roots.

If the queue empties first, the labels change to h′ so that no edge of the forest and no edge of M leaves the directed equality subgraph, while at least one edge from FL to R − FR enters it. With δ = min {l.h + r.h − w(l, r) : l ∈ FL and r ∈ R − FR}, equation (25.5) subtracts δ from l.h for vertices in FL and adds δ to r.h for vertices in FR. Lemma 25.15 shows that h′ is feasible and has these properties.

The running example has seven workers and seven jobs. It starts from a greedy matching of four edges, makes three augmentations and three label updates, with δ equal to 1, 1 and 2, and ends with weights summing to 65. That is also the sum of all the final feasible vertex labels. The book remarks that maximizing the weight of a matching and minimizing the sum of the feasible vertex labels are “duals” of each other.

The while loop of HUNGARIAN runs at most n times, since each iteration grows M by one. A call of FIND-AUGMENTING-PATH makes at most n growth steps, each guaranteed to discover at least one vertex in R, so it takes O(n3) and the whole algorithm O(n4). Exercise 25.3-5 shows that rebuilding GM,h is unnecessary, and Problem 25-2 computes δ in O(n) time, which gives O(n3).

problem 25-1 · perfect matchings in a regular bipartite graph

One walk, two halves

A bipartite graph is d-regular when every vertex has exactly d edges. Exercise 25.1-6 says such a graph can always be cut into d perfect matchings that share no edge. Colour the edges with d colours so that every colour touches every vertex exactly once: that is the same thing. Problem 25-1 asks how to find the colours fast, in Θ(E lg d) time when d is a power of 2.

Here is one way, the one parts (a) and (b) of the problem lead to. When every degree is even, a closed walk that uses every edge exactly once, an Euler tour, exists and takes O(E) time to find. Walk it and sort the edges by direction: those walked from L to R in one pile, those walked from R to L in the other. The walk enters each vertex as often as it leaves it, so every vertex has d/2 edges in each pile, and you now hold two (d/2)-regular graphs. Do the same inside each of them. After lg d rounds every graph is 1-regular, which is a perfect matching.

Each round walks every edge once, and there are lg d rounds, hence Θ(E lg d). The book states the problem and leaves the algorithm for you to find, so this recursion is one standard answer, not the book's own text.

Try it: pick d = 4 (the complete graph K4,4) or d = 2 (two separate cycles) above the picture and press Step. The dark edge is the one being walked; when the walker arrives it takes the colour of its direction. The first tour gives blue and yellow; the second round splits each of them again into four colours. At the end every colour is a perfect matching: each vertex has exactly one edge of each colour. The d = 2 graph has two components, so the tour runs once on each.

What the book says

Exercise 25.1-6 defines the setting: “In a d-regular graph, every vertex has degree d.” If G = (V, E) is bipartite with V = L ∪ R and also d-regular, then |L| = |R|. The exercise asks the reader to use Hall's theorem to prove that every d-regular bipartite graph contains a perfect matching, and then to prove that it contains d disjoint perfect matchings.

Problem 25-1 builds on that exercise in three parts. Part (a) asks for a proof that a connected, undirected graph has an Euler tour, “a cycle traversing each edge exactly once, though it may visit a vertex multiple times”, if and only if the degree of every vertex is even. Part (b) asks, for a connected graph in which every vertex has even degree, for an O(E)-time algorithm that finds an Euler tour, as in Problem 20-3(b).

Part (c) supposes that d is an exact power of 2 and asks for an algorithm that finds all d disjoint perfect matchings in a d-regular bipartite graph in Θ(E lg d) time. The book gives no solution here. The machine uses the standard idea that the first two parts point to: an Euler tour splits a regular graph into two graphs of half the degree, and the split repeats lg d times.

Labels from shortest paths

Someone hands you the best assignment M* but not the labels that prove it. Can you work them out? You need numbers with l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r) for every pair (25.6), and l.h + r.h = w(l, r) on every matched pair (25.7). Those are the labels whose equality subgraph holds M*, so M* is a perfect matching in it.

These conditions have the shape of a shortest-path problem. Let xl = l.h and xr = −r.h. Then (25.6) reads xr ≤ xl − w(l, r), and (25.7) adds xl ≤ xr + w(l, r) for matched pairs. Each is the triangle inequality v.d ≤ u.d + length of an edge (u, v). So draw an edge l → r of length −w(l, r) for every pair and an edge r → l of length w(l, r) for every matched pair. Run Bellman–Ford with every distance starting at 0. The distances obey every triangle inequality, so they are the x you want.

One thing can go wrong: a negative cycle. A cycle in this graph alternates unmatched and matched pairs, and its length is the weight of the matching minus the weight of the matching you would get by swapping along the cycle. If M* is optimal, no cycle is negative and the distances settle. If it is not, there is a negative cycle and no labels exist.

Try it: press Step: each step is one pass of Bellman–Ford over all 20 edges, and every distance that improves flashes in the margin. Cells you see are edges: each cell is l → r, and each lilac cell also r → l. When a pass changes nothing the distances are final, and the labels follow: l.h = l.d and r.h = −r.d. The grid then switches to slack: never below 0, and exactly 0 on the lilac cells. Then pick M is not optimal and watch the distances sink in every pass until the check finds the negative cycle.

What the book says

Problem 25-5 starts from a complete bipartite graph G = (V, E) with edge weights w(l, r) and a given maximum-weight perfect matching M* of G. The task is to compute a feasible vertex labeling h such that M* is a perfect matching in the equality subgraph Gh, that is, a labeling with l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r) for all l ∈ L and r ∈ R (25.6), and l.h + r.h = w(l, r) for all (l, r) ∈ M* (25.7).

Requirement (25.6) holds for all edges and the stronger requirement (25.7) holds for the edges of M*. The problem asks for an algorithm to compute h and a proof that it is correct.

The hint reads: “Use the similarity between conditions (25.6) and (25.7) and some of the properties of shortest paths proved in Chapter 22”, in particular the triangle inequality (Lemma 22.10) and the convergence property (Lemma 22.14). The book gives no solution, so the graph and the shortest-path run in the machine are one standard way to follow that hint, not the book's own text.

The chapter on one page

Chapter 25 in eight cards. Each one points at the scenes that act it out.

Pairs that never share

A matching is a set of edges in which every vertex has at most one incident edge. In a bipartite graph every edge joins L to R. A maximal matching cannot be extended; a maximum one is as large as any matching can be. GREEDY-BIPARTITE-MATCHING gives a maximal one, and the book asks you to show it is at least half the maximum. → 25.1 Matchings

Swap along the path

An M-alternating path alternates between edges outside and inside M; if both ends are free it is M-augmenting, with an odd number of edges. Taking the symmetric difference M ⊕ P makes a new matching with exactly one more edge (Lemma 25.1), and k vertex-disjoint paths add k. → 25.1 Augmenting paths

No path, no better matching

A matching is maximum if and only if no augmenting path exists (Corollary 25.4, due to Berge). So search for a path with an alternating BFS, flip it, and repeat: each search is O(E) and there are at most |V|/2 flips, so O(VE). → 25.1 Augmenting paths

Many paths at once

Hopcroft–Karp flips a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths per round. Direct the graph, run BFS from all free left vertices to get layers and the dag H, then DFS backwards from the free right vertices in layer q. Shortest paths get longer every round, so there are fewer than 2√|V| rounds: O(√V · E). → 25.1 Hopcroft–Karp

Nobody wants to leave

In the stable-marriage problem each of n women and n men ranks everyone on the other side. A blocking pair is two people, not matched to each other, who each prefer the other to their partner; a matching with none is stable. A stable matching always exists, and there can be several. → 25.2 The stable-marriage problem

Ask, hold, trade up

GALE-SHAPLEY: a free woman proposes down her list, a man keeps the best proposal he has seen. It stops after at most n² proposals with a stable matching, whatever order the free women go in. Women get the best partner any stable matching allows, and men the worst. → 25.2 The Gale–Shapley algorithm

Best total, and a cap to prove it

The assignment problem asks for a perfect matching of maximum total weight in a complete bipartite graph; listing all n! of them is hopeless. A feasible labeling has l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r), and the sum of all labels caps every matching. If the equality subgraph Gh holds a perfect matching, it reaches the cap and is optimal (Theorem 25.14). → 25.3 The assignment problem, Vertex labels

Nudge the labels

The Hungarian algorithm keeps a matching in Gh and augments by BFS. When the search runs dry it lowers the labels of the searched left vertices by δ and raises the searched right ones by δ, so a new edge enters Gh. That is O(n4), and O(n3) with Problem 25-2. Problem 25-1 splits a regular bipartite graph into perfect matchings; Problem 25-5 recovers labels from an optimal matching. → 25.3 The Hungarian algorithm, 25-1, 25-5

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

matching
A set of edges M ⊆ E in which every vertex has at most one incident edge.
bipartite graph
A graph whose vertices split into two disjoint sets L and R, with every edge joining one vertex of each.
matched / unmatched
A vertex is matched under M if some edge of M touches it, and unmatched, or free, otherwise.
maximal / maximum matching
Maximal: no edge can be added. Maximum: the most edges of any matching. Every maximum matching is maximal, not the other way round.
perfect matching
A matching under which every vertex is matched.
M-alternating path
A simple path whose edges alternate between M and E − M.
M-augmenting path
An alternating path whose first and last edges are outside M. Both ends are free, and it has an odd number of edges.
symmetric difference X ⊕ Y
The elements in X or Y but not both. M ⊕ P swaps which edges of P are in the matching.
Berge's theorem
Corollary 25.4: a matching is maximum if and only if the graph has no augmenting path for it.
GM
The directed version of the graph: edges outside M point from L to R, edges in M from R to L.
layer and the dag H
A vertex's layer is its BFS distance in GM from an unmatched left vertex. H keeps layers 0 to q and the edges between neighbouring layers.
maximal set of vertex-disjoint paths
Paths that share no vertex, to which no further such path can be added. It need not be the largest such set.
Hopcroft–Karp
Repeatedly flips a maximal set of vertex-disjoint shortest augmenting paths. It runs in O(√V · E).
stable-marriage problem
n women and n men each rank everyone on the other side; pair them so nobody has a reason to leave.
blocking pair, stable matching
A blocking pair is a woman and a man not matched to each other who each prefer the other to their partner. A stable matching has none.
Gale–Shapley algorithm
Free women propose in list order and men trade up. At most n² proposals, and it always returns a stable matching.
assignment problem
Find a perfect matching of maximum total weight in a complete bipartite graph with edge weights w(l, r).
feasible vertex labeling
A number h on every vertex with l.h + r.h ≥ w(l, r) for every pair. The default gives each left vertex its row's best weight and each right vertex 0.
equality subgraph Gh
The edges where the two labels add up exactly to the weight. A perfect matching inside it is an optimal assignment.
Hungarian algorithm
Keeps a matching in Gh, augments by BFS, and changes the labels when the search fails. O(n4) as written.
δ and the forest F
The breadth-first forest the failed search grew, and the smallest slack of an edge from a searched left vertex to an unsearched right vertex. Searched left labels drop by δ, searched right labels rise by δ.
d-regular graph
Every vertex has degree d. A d-regular bipartite graph holds d disjoint perfect matchings (Exercise 25.1-6).
duality
Maximising a matching's weight and minimising the sum of the labels are duals of each other, much as a maximum flow equals a minimum cut.

Rust words from the code panels

For readers coming from another language. Each one shows up in a listing above.

Vec<Option<usize>> as mate
mate[r] is Some(l) when r is matched to l and None when it is free. There is no −1 sentinel to forget to check, and the compiler makes you handle both cases.
lazy filter, next(), next_back()
nbrs.iter().filter(…) does no work until asked. next() takes the first free neighbour and next_back() the last, and neither scans further than it must.
VecDeque and while let
while let Some(l) = q.pop_front() tests for an empty queue and dequeues in one go. VecDeque::from([s]) starts a queue holding s.
match on an Option, early return
match m.mate_r[r] { None => return Some(…), Some(next) => q.push_back(next) } gives the free and matched cases their own arms, and a function can leave from inside the arm.
a struct that owns two Vecs
Matching { mate_l, mate_r } keeps both sides of M. &Matching lends it to the search read-only; &mut Matching lets augment overwrite entries along a path.
usize::MAX and saturating_add
A layer not reached yet is usize::MAX, the book's ∞. Adding 1 to it would overflow and panic, so saturating_add(1) stays at the maximum.
type alias, tuple results
type Mate = Vec<Option<usize>> is just a shorter name. A function can return a tuple, (dl, dr, q), and the caller unpacks it with let.
&mut out-parameters in recursion
The DFS takes &mut [bool] for the discovered flags and &mut Vec for the path. Every recursive call writes into the same buffers, so nothing is copied.
iter().flatten() and closures in filter
paths.iter().flatten() walks every edge of every path as one stream; filter(|&r| mr[r].is_none()) keeps the free right vertices.
rank tables and take_while
Inverting each preference list once makes “does he prefer her to her?” one comparison, and take_while walks the people ranked above a partner and stops at the partner.
recursion with &mut and i64::MIN
The assignment brute force builds all n! permutations in one shared Vec, pushing and popping, and starts its best total at i64::MIN, the book's −∞.
lifetimes in a struct, Result
The Hungarian state only borrows the weights, so its type carries a lifetime 'a. Computing labels from a matching returns Result, with Err carrying a message when no labeling exists.
