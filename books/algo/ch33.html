The chapter in five stops

Each stop has one number that measures how badly things are going, and a way to push it down.

  1. Learn from examplesA program sees data, forms a guess about the pattern, then uses the guess on new data. A spam filter shows how.
  2. Sort points into groupsWith no labels at all, put similar points together. Assign each point to its nearest center, move the centers, repeat. It always stops, but maybe not at the best answer.
  3. Learn whom to trustExperts give advice every day. Cut the trust in each one who is wrong. You end up with not many more than about twice the best expert's mistakes.
  4. Walk downhillTo reach a low point, look at the slope under your feet and step the other way. Step size decides whether you crawl, arrive or fly off.
  5. Fit a model to dataFitting a line to patients is one more walk downhill. Here the ground is the total squared error, and it has one lowest level, so you can't get trapped.

33 · what learning means here

A program that learns from examples

Show a program examples with the right answers, and it can form a rule that it then uses on examples it has never seen.

Six emails sit in a folder. Someone has marked each one spam or not spam. A new email arrives. Is it spam? You could write the rules by hand, or let the examples write them. Give each word a score: up when the word shows up in spam, down when it shows up in good mail. A new email's score is the sum of its words.

Those scores are a hypothesis: a guess about the pattern in the data. Reading the marked emails is the training phase. Scoring new emails is the prediction phase. Because every training email came with its answer, or label, this is supervised learning.

Take the labels away and you have unsupervised learning: the data must speak for itself, as in the next scenes. When training and prediction are mixed together, as when you learn whom to trust day by day, it is online learning.

Read the maths
spam if (sum of the word scores) > threshold
score
One number per word, learned from the training emails.
threshold
A line you choose. Totals above it count as spam.

With numbers: “money now” scores 2 + 1 = 3. With the threshold at 0, 3 is above 0, so it is flagged as spam.

Why it can work at all: a word that keeps turning up in spam is some evidence about the next email. It is only evidence, not proof, so the filter can be wrong. Its rule is only as good as the examples it saw.

Try it: press Next to read the emails one at a time and watch the bars move. Then switch to One wrong label: a single mistake in the training data changes the scores, so check whether the new emails come out the same. Drag the threshold to make the filter stricter or looser.

What the book says

The book describes three learning problems in this chapter: k-means clustering, multiplicative weights and gradient descent. An algorithm uses the data collected so far to produce a hypothesis that describes regularities or predicts new data. Machine learning typically has a training phase, then a prediction phase. In online learning, the two are intermingled.

In supervised learning, each training point has an associated output or label, and the hypothesis says how labels depend on attributes. The book's example is spam: a rule of the form “if an email has one of a set of words, then it is likely to be spam”, or a spam score per word, with a document classified as spam when its total passes a threshold. The exact scoring rule on this page (+1 per spam email, −1 per other email) is this page's toy; the book names no rule.

In unsupervised learning, the training data is unlabeled, as in clustering. A third form, reinforcement learning, takes actions in an environment and receives feedback, and it is not covered further. Sometimes the goal is causal inference rather than prediction, and fitting may have to balance fit against simplicity.

33.1 · feature vectors and similarity

How alike are two things?

Turn each thing into a list of numbers, then measure how far apart two lists are, without letting one number drown out the rest.

Amara has a grade average of 3.9 and a family income of 30,000. Which other student is most like her? Before a computer can answer, each student must become a short list of numbers. That list is a feature vector, and each number in it is an attribute.

The book measures how unlike two lists are. Subtract them number by number, square each difference, add the squares up. Now look at what that does to Amara and Chen. The grade averages differ by 1.9, which squares to 3.61. The incomes differ by 1,000, which squares to 1,000,000. The grades barely count.

So attributes are usually rescaled first. One way the book gives: stretch each attribute so its smallest value becomes 0 and its largest becomes 1. Then a big difference in grades and a big difference in income count about the same.

Read the maths
Δ(x, y) = (x₁ − y₁)² + (x₂ − y₂)² + … + (x_d − y_d)²
x, y
Two feature vectors, such as two students.
x₁, x₂
The first and second number in x. Here, grade average and income.
d
How many numbers each list has. Here, 2.
Δ
The dissimilarity: 0 means identical, and bigger means less alike.

With numbers: Amara (3.9, 30000) and Bo (3.7, 38000) give 0.2² + 8000² = 0.04 + 64,000,000.

Easy to mix up: Δ is the distance squared. The book chooses squared differences because they are conventional and convenient, and says other measures could be used instead.

Try it: press Next in Raw numbers and watch the grade part of each bar stay too small to see. The closest student is Chen, whose grades are nothing like Amara's. Then switch to Scaled 0 to 1 and step through again: the answer changes to Bo.

What the book says

The input to clustering is a set of n examples and an integer k, and the goal is to divide the examples into at most k disjoint clusters of similar examples. Each example has d attributes and is given by a d-dimensional feature vector x = (x₁, x₂, …, xd), the point in Rd that represents it. The book's Figure 33.1 clusters 49 examples, 48 state capitals plus the District of Columbia, by latitude and longitude. It also mentions stars and fragments of recorded speech.

Dissimilarity is the squared Euclidean distance (33.1), and all attribute values must be present. If any are missing, you might ignore that example, or fill in the median value.

Data often needs cleaning first. Scales can vary widely, and the book's example of a large gap is grade-point average against family income. Attributes are usually scaled so that none dominates: by a linear transform that sends the minimum to 0 and the maximum to 1, or so that each has mean 0 and unit variance. Binary attributes may need no scaling. The students and their numbers here are this page's own.

33.1 · clusterings

One number that scores a grouping

Pick a few center points. Each point joins its nearest center. Add up the squared distances: that total says how tight the groups are.

Picture eight houses on a map and two postboxes. Everyone walks to the nearest postbox. Draw a square whose side is the walk from each house, and pile up the areas of all those squares. Long walks make huge squares. Move a postbox, and the pile changes.

That pile is the number we want to shrink. Its name is the objective: the one number a method tries to make as small as it can. The postboxes are the centers, and the houses that share a center form a cluster. Choosing k centers to make the pile as small as possible is the k-means problem.

It sounds easy. It isn't: the problem is NP-hard, so nobody knows a fast way to find the very best centers. The rest of this section settles for an answer that can't be improved by small changes.

Read the maths
f(S, C) = sum over all points x of (squared distance from x to its nearest center)
S
The set of points.
C
The list of k centers. A center can be anywhere, not only on a data point.
f
The objective. Smaller means tighter groups.

With numbers: a point at (2, 4) with its nearest center at (3, 7) adds 1² + 3² = 10 to f.

Try it: press Next and watch each point add its square to the stack. Then drag a center (the diamonds) or press New centers. The machine jumps to the total. How low can you get f by hand? Try moving a center away from the middle of its points and see f jump.

What the book says

A k-clustering of a set S of n points is a decomposition into a sequence of k disjoint subsets, or clusters, whose union is S. A cluster may be empty. The book considers only clusterings defined by a sequence C of k centers and the nearest-center rule: a point may belong to a cluster only if no other cluster's center is closer. A center can be anywhere, and not necessarily a point in S.

Ties may be broken arbitrarily, except that a point never changes cluster unless the distance to its new center is strictly smaller.

The k-means problem is to find centers that minimize f(S, C), the sum of the squared distances from each point to its nearest center (33.2). The problem is NP-hard. The book knows of no polynomial-time algorithm that finds the global minimum, but a local minimum can be found. The points on this page are this page's own.

The score, one point at a time
33.1 · optimal centers, optimal clusters

Two small problems we can solve exactly

With the groups fixed, each group's best center is its average. With the centers fixed, each point should join its nearest one.

Finding centers and groups together is hard. But freeze one of them and the other becomes easy. Say four friends live at miles 1, 2, 4 and 9 of a straight road, and they must pick one meeting spot. If the price of a trip is the distance squared, where is the cheapest spot? Try a few spots and plot the total. You get a smooth bowl, and its lowest point is at mile 4.

And (1 + 2 + 4 + 9) ÷ 4 is 4. The best spot is the plain average. That point is the group's centroid, or mean: on a plane, you average the east-west numbers and the north-south numbers separately.

Now freeze the centers instead. Each point adds its own squared distance to the total, once. So it does best by joining whichever center is nearest, and what one point does can't affect the others. That is the nearest-center rule again, and now you can see why it is the right rule.

Read the maths
c = (x₁ + x₂ + … + x_m) ÷ m
c
The best center for one group: its centroid.
x₁ … x_m
The points in the group.
m
How many points the group has.

With numbers: points at 1, 2, 4, 9 give c = 16 ÷ 4 = 4. The total at c = 4 is 9 + 4 + 0 + 25 = 38. At c = 3 it is 4 + 1 + 1 + 36 = 42, which is higher.

Why the average wins: moving the center towards a point cuts that point's cost, but raises the others. Those pulls balance exactly at the average. The squares are what make it the average, rather than some other middle.

Try it: press Next in Best center to try spots along the road, then drag the last point further out. The average follows it, and the bowl slides. In Best groups, watch the total drop each time a misplaced point joins its nearest center.

What the book says

In an optimal solution, each center must be the centroid, or mean, of the points in its cluster, attribute by attribute (33.3). Theorem 33.1: given a nonempty cluster, its centroid is the unique choice of center that minimizes the sum of the squared distances to the points of the cluster. The proof treats each attribute as a convex quadratic in the center's coordinate, then takes the derivative and sets it to 0.

Theorem 33.2: given the points and the centers, a clustering minimizes the total of squared distances if and only if it assigns each point to a cluster whose center is nearest. Each point contributes exactly once to the sum, so putting it with its nearest center minimizes its contribution.

The road, the friends and their numbers are this page's own.

33.1 · Lloyd's procedure

Take turns, and the score can only fall

Let every point join its nearest center, then move every center to the middle of its group. Repeat until nothing changes.

The last scene gave us two exact answers: the best groups for fixed centers, and the best centers for fixed groups. Lloyd's procedure just takes turns. Start with a few points picked at random as centers. All points assign themselves. All centers move to their group's average. Now some points may be closer to a different center, so go again.

Watch the chart. It is a staircase going down, because each kind of step can only lower the score or leave it alone. When an assign step changes nothing, the procedure stops.

That end point is a local minimum: no single kind of step can lower the score any further. Be careful with that word, though. It does not mean best. In Bad start, two centers begin in the same clump. The score still falls at every step, the procedure still stops, and the result is still poor.

Read the maths
running time = O(T · d · k · n)
T
How many rounds it took. There is no good promise on this, only that it ends.
d, k, n
Attributes per point, centers, and points.

With numbers: here d = 2, k = 3 and n = 13. One assign step measures 13 × 3 = 39 distances, each adding 2 squares, so 78 squares a round.

Why it must stop: each round before the last strictly lowers the score. There are only so many ways to divide up the points, at most kn, and a falling score can't visit one twice. So it runs out of moves. That says nothing about how long it takes, or how good the end is.

Try it: press Next and tell the two kinds of step apart: the coloured tag says which is running. Press New random start to roll again, or drag a diamond at step 1 to place a center yourself. Then pick Bad start and compare the final f.

What the book says

Lloyd's procedure iterates two operations until the results converge. Step 1, initialize centers: pick k points independently from S at random, and assign all points to cluster S(1) to begin. Step 2, assign points to clusters by the nearest-center rule, not changing a point's cluster unless the new center is strictly closer. Step 3, stop if no change. Step 4, recompute each center as the centroid of its cluster, or the zero vector if the cluster is empty, then go to step 2.

The procedure always terminates. By Theorem 33.1, recomputing centers can't increase f, and a point is reassigned only when that strictly decreases f. So each iteration except the last strictly decreases f, and there are at most kn clusterings.

The running time is proportional to the number T of iterations. Assignment takes O(dkn) per iteration and recomputing centers takes O(dn), for O(Tdkn) overall. In practice, it sometimes suffices to stop when the percentage decrease in f falls below a threshold. The book writes the procedure as four numbered steps; the listing below sets them out as code. The thirteen points are this page's own. The chapter notes say the procedure was first proposed by Lloyd and later by Forgy, and that it is sometimes called the Lloyd-Forgy algorithm.

33.1 · only a local answer

Lloyd can stop at a bad answer

Lloyd's procedure stops when no step helps, which can be far from the best. The book's remedy: start again many times and keep the best.

Four points sit at the corners of a long, flat box. The obvious groups are left pair and right pair. But put one center on the top edge and one on the bottom. Top points join the top center, bottom points the bottom one, and each center is already at its group's average. Nothing wants to change, so Lloyd stops, with a score 16 times worse than the left-and-right answer.

It is the same on a straight line. That is the warning in the word local. Each kind of step looks only at small moves, one point or one center. Getting from the bad answer to the good one needs several points to change sides at once, and the score would rise on the way.

Nobody knows a fast way to guarantee the best answer. So the book offers a plain remedy: run Lloyd many times from different random starts, and keep the run with the lowest score. It still doesn't promise the best answer. It only makes a poor one less likely.

A recipe you will see again: pick a family of guesses described by some numbers (here, the centers). Write down one number saying how badly a guess fits (here, f). Then use a procedure that pushes that number down, at least locally. Gradient descent, later in this chapter, is one such procedure.

Try it: press Next in Flat box to see Lloyd accept a bad answer, and do the same in On a line. Then in Many restarts, watch eight random starts finish at different scores. Press New dice for eight more.

What the book says

A local minimum satisfies two properties: each cluster has an optimal center, and each point is assigned to a cluster with the closest center. These properties are necessary, but not sufficient, for optimality. Because Lloyd's procedure is guaranteed to find only a locally optimal clustering, one approach is to run it many times with different randomly chosen initial centers, taking the best result.

Exercise 33.1-2 asks for an example with n = 4 points in the plane and k = 2 where an iteration doesn't improve f, yet the clustering is not optimal. Problem 33-3 asks for an example in one dimension. The two examples here are this page's own answers to those questions.

Lloyd's algorithm illustrates an approach common to many machine-learning algorithms: define a hypothesis space in terms of parameters, define a measure of how poorly a hypothesis fits the training data, and use an optimization procedure to minimize it, at least locally. Regularization terms can be added to penalize hypotheses that are too complicated and that overfit, a topic the book doesn't pursue here.

The chapter notes add that finding an optimal clustering is NP-hard even in the plane (Mahajan et al.), and that there is an approximation algorithm with ratio 9 + ε for any ε > 0 (Kanungo et al.).

Fewer colours, nearly the same picture

Treat each pixel's colour as a point, cluster the points, and repaint each pixel with its center. The picture survives in far less space.

Each pixel of a photo stores three numbers, red, green and blue, in 24 bits. So a pixel is a point in a three-dimensional colour space. That is all Lloyd needs. Ask for 4 clusters, and you get 4 colours that stand in for all the others.

Now each pixel only has to say which of the 4 colours it uses, and that takes 2 bits. A short table called the palette maps those numbers back to real colours when the picture is opened. This is vector quantization. Some detail is lost for good, so the compression is lossy.

The book's other example clusters 49 places on a map by latitude and longitude, into 4 groups. It lists the score after each round, so you can read the same staircase from the Lloyd scene in real results from the book.

33.2 · learning from experts

Advice from people you can't yet judge

If one adviser is always right, vote with everyone who has never been wrong. Each mistake you make throws out at least half of them.

Every morning you must say whether it will rain. Eight friends each give an answer first. You know nothing about the weather and nothing about the friends. Some may be useless, or even out to fool you. Only in the evening do you find out what happened. Learning as you go like this is online learning, and the friends are the book's experts.

What can you fairly hope for? You can't beat the future. But you can compare yourself with the best expert, judged afterwards. Your mistakes minus that expert's mistakes is your regret. Low regret means that following the crowd cleverly was nearly as good as knowing from day one whom to follow.

Start with the easy case: suppose one expert is always right, but you don't know which. Keep a list of experts with a clean record, follow the majority of that list, and each evening cross off everyone who was wrong. When you are wrong, at least half of the list was wrong too, so the list at least halves. It can only halve so many times.

Read the maths
your mistakes ≤ ⌈lg n⌉
n
How many experts there are.
lg n
How many times you can halve n before reaching 1.
⌈ ⌉
Round up to a whole number.

With numbers: with n = 8, the list can go 8 → 4 → 2 → 1, so at most lg 8 = 3 mistakes. With n = 1000, at most 10.

Not a promise about the weather: nothing here assumes the weather can be predicted. The promise is only about you compared with the experts. If every expert were terrible, this guarantee would mean little.

Try it: in Worst case the outcomes are chosen to catch you out whenever the perfect expert allows it. Press Next and count your mistakes against the limit. Drag n to 16 and look again: twice the experts, only one more mistake. Random days shows that a real run can do much better than the limit.

What the book says

There are n experts and T events with outcomes 0 or 1. Before event t, each expert makes a prediction, and the learner produces a prediction from those predictions and what has been learned about the experts. Only then does the outcome appear. The goal is to minimize the total number m of mistakes. The book assumes nothing about the events or the experts: their predictions could be correlated or chosen to deceive you.

The book's fair measure is regret, m − m*, where m* is the number of mistakes made by the best expert in hindsight. Regret can be negative, although it typically isn't.

Lemma 33.3: if one of n experts always predicts correctly, an algorithm makes at most ⌈lg n⌉ mistakes. It keeps a set S of experts with no mistakes so far, predicts the majority vote of S (any prediction on a tie), and removes everyone who was wrong. Exercise 33.2-1 generalizes this: if S ever becomes empty, reset it to all the experts and continue. The friends and the rain are the book's kind of example; the runs are this page's own.

33.2 · the weighted-majority algorithm

Cut trust a little, instead of cutting people

Give every expert a weight and follow the heavier side of the vote. Whoever is wrong loses weight, so reliable voices come to dominate.

Crossing people off is too harsh when even the best expert sometimes slips: soon nobody would be left. Instead give each expert a weight, starting at 1, which measures how much you trust them. Each morning, add up the weights behind rain and the weights behind dry, and go with the heavier side.

In the evening, multiply the weight of each wrong expert by a number a bit below 1, and leave the others alone. With γ = 1/4, that number is 3/4. After four mistakes, a weight is about a third of what it was. Nobody ever hits zero, so a good expert with a bad week can recover their influence relative to the rest.

Multiplying, rather than subtracting, gives the family its name: multiplicative weights. This particular member is the weighted-majority algorithm. It never needs to know who is good. The pattern of the mistakes finds that out.

Read the maths
w = (1 − γ)^(number of mistakes so far)
w
An expert's weight: how much you trust them right now.
γ
Gamma: the share of weight lost per mistake, between 0 and 1/2.

With numbers: γ = 1/4, with 3 mistakes so far: w = (3/4)³ = 0.75 × 0.75 × 0.75 ≈ 0.42.

Why the vote gets good: each mistake costs an expert the same share, so the gap between a careful expert and a careless one keeps widening. Given time, the careless ones can't outvote the careful one.

Try it: in One good expert, Chidi is right about 90% of the time and the rest are guessing. Press Next and see Chidi's bar pull ahead. Then try Adversary, in which the outcome is always the opposite of what you say. Drag γ up to 0.5 to punish harder, and press New days for another run.

What the book says

The key idea is to use feedback to update how much trust to put in each expert, decreasing confidence in those who make more mistakes. The change in weights is done by multiplication, hence the name. WEIGHTED-MAJORITY takes the experts, the number T of events, the number n of experts, and a parameter 0 < γ ≤ 1/2.

Lines 1–2 set each initial weight to 1. Each iteration of the main loop gets the experts' predictions (line 4), computes upweight and downweight, the sums of the weights of those predicting 1 and 0 (lines 5–8), and predicts by the larger sum, breaking ties in favor of 1 (lines 9–11). The outcome is revealed in line 12, and lines 14–17 multiply each wrong expert's weight by 1 − γ.

Multiplicative-weights methods are a broader class. Outcomes and predictions can be real numbers with a loss, the update can depend on the loss, and the weights can be treated as a distribution over experts. The chapter notes credit the first machine-learning use to Littlestone's Winnow algorithm, later extended by Littlestone and Warmuth to weighted majority. Trust that shrinks with every mistake
33.2 · the bound on regret

Never much worse than the best expert

Whatever the experts and the outcomes do, your mistakes stay under about twice the best expert's, plus a small fixed extra.

Here is the surprising part. You don't know who the best expert is until the end. The outcomes may be chosen to hurt you. And still there is a ceiling on your mistakes, in terms of the mistakes of whichever expert turns out best.

The reason is a tug of war over the total weight. Every time you are wrong, at least half of the weight was on the wrong side, so a share of it vanishes. So the total shrinks fast if you make many mistakes. But the best expert's own weight shrinks only when they are wrong, and it is part of that total. The total can't fall below it, and that limits your mistakes.

Be clear about what this promises. It doesn't say you will do well. If the best expert is poor, you can be poor. And it isn't a small gap: in Adversary you go wrong every single day and the best expert about half the time. What it says is that the factor of about 2 is all you pay. Picking one expert at random by weight cuts that bound in half.

Read the maths
m ≤ 2(1 + γ) m* + (2 ln n) / γ
m
Your mistakes.
m*
The mistakes of the best expert, judged at the end.
n
How many experts there are.
ln n
The natural logarithm of n. It grows very slowly: ln 20 is about 3.
γ
The share of weight lost per mistake, at most 1/2.

With numbers: the book's case has 20 experts, 1000 events and a best expert with 50 errors. With γ = 1/4: 2 × 1.25 × 50 + (2 × 3.00) ÷ 0.25 = 125 + 24 = at most 149 errors.

Why randomness helps: an adversary who sees your weights can always pick the outcome that the heavier side got wrong. But if you follow a single expert picked at random by weight, then the adversary can't know which side you are on. It can only make you wrong with a chance equal to the share of weight on the losing side.

What the book says

Theorem 33.4: for every expert Ei and every event T′ ≤ T, m(T′) ≤ 2(1 + γ)mi(T′) + (2 ln n)/γ (33.5). The proof uses the potential W(t), the sum of all the weights. On a mistake W(t + 1) ≤ (1 − γ/2)W(t), otherwise it doesn't increase, and W always exceeds any one expert's weight (1 − γ)mi. Taking logarithms and bounding them gives the theorem. Corollary 33.5 compares against the best expert at the end.

Choosing γ = √(ln n / m*), when that is at most 1/2, gives m ≤ 2m* + 4√(m* ln n). With γ = 1/2, the algorithm makes at most 3 times the best expert's errors, plus 4 ln n. With T = 1000, n = 20 and a best expert with 50 errors, γ = 1/4 gives at most 149 errors, a success rate of at least 85%.

Randomization decreases the bound by a factor of 2. Slope: how much the height changes per step

Take a tiny step right and see how your height changes. That ratio is the slope, and its sign says which way is down.

You stand on a hilly road in thick fog. You can't see the valley, only the ground under your feet. How do you know which way is down? Take a small step to the right and measure how your height changed. If you dropped, right is downhill. If you rose, left is.

Divide the change in height by the length of the step. That number is the slope. On a real curve, the answer depends a little on how long your step was, so make the step shorter and shorter. The ratio settles on one number. That number is the slope at that spot, and school maths calls it the derivative.

The sign is what counts. A positive slope means the ground rises to the right, so downhill is left. A negative slope means downhill is right. A slope of zero means flat ground: a valley floor, or a hilltop.

33.3 · unconstrained gradient descent

Step against the slope, and repeat

To get low, check the slope where you stand and step the other way. You reach a low point, though maybe not the lowest one.

The ball stands at x = 0.6. The slope there is about −0.38, a gentle fall to the right. So it moves right. How far? In proportion to the slope, multiplied by a setting called the step size. Steep ground means a big move, and gentle ground means a small one. Then it looks again, because the ground has changed.

That loop is gradient descent. The word descent means going down, and the gradient is the slope, as in the warm-up. As the ball nears the valley floor, the slope shrinks, so the moves shrink too, and the ball settles there. A flat spot like that, lower than everything near it, is a local minimum.

Now the catch. The curve's true lowest point, the global minimum, is over on the left. The ball can't know that. The slope says only what is under its feet. From x = 0.6 every small step leads to the valley on the right, so that is where it stays. So gradient descent looks for a local answer, and it can miss the best one.

33.3 · the procedure

Too small crawls, too big bounces

Step size decides everything: too small crawls, about right arrives, too big bounces, and far too big flies off.

Take the simplest possible bowl, f(x) = x², with its lowest point at x = 0. Its slope at x is 2x, so one step of gradient descent sends x to x − γ · 2x. In words, it multiplies x by (1 − 2γ). All four behaviours are in that one number.

With γ = 0.03, x shrinks by only 6% a step. That is safe, and very slow. With γ = 0.25, x halves each step, and the moves get smaller as the ground flattens, as in the book's Figure 33.4. With γ = 0.9, x flips sign and shrinks only a little: it overshoots, but lands closer each time. With γ = 1.1, x flips and grows. Each landing is farther out and steeper, so it runs away.

One more surprise in the book's procedure: it doesn't return the last point. It returns the average of all the points it visited, called x-avg. The book admits that you might prefer the last point in many circumstances. Its analysis uses the average because that is what its proof can handle, as a later scene shows.

33.3 · convex functions

Bowls have no false bottoms

Stretch a string between any two points on a curve. If the curve never rises above it, the only valley is the true lowest one.

Draw a bowl on paper. Pick any two points on it and stretch a string between them. The string stays on or above the bowl, wherever you pick the points. Now try that on a curve with two valleys, with the string running from one valley floor to the other. The hump between them pokes up through the string.

That is the whole test. A curve that passes for every pair of points is called convex. It describes a shape you can see, and it is the property the book's analysis relies on. A convex function is bowl-like all over, in as many directions as it has.

Why care? Because on a convex function, the trap from before can't happen. Any local minimum is also a global minimum. A walker who reaches a valley floor is at the lowest level there is. So the book studies how close gradient descent can get on convex functions. On anything else, a local answer is all it offers.

33.3 · analysis for convex functions

The price of accuracy

With the right step size on a bowl, the average gets close to the lowest value. Halving the error limit takes four times the steps.

On a convex bowl, gradient descent has no false bottom to fall into. So now the question is how close it gets, and how fast. The book's answer needs two numbers about the problem. R is how far the start is from the lowest point. L is a limit on how steep the ground gets along the walk.

Given those and a budget of T steps, the theorem names a step size. It then promises that the average of the visited points is within a known height of the true lowest value. The step size isn't a free choice here: more steps means a smaller γ, so that you creep in more carefully.

Why trust the average and not the last point? Because the proof can only control the steps as a group. Think of a savings account. The balance is the squared distance to the bottom. In a step where you are still high up, the balance falls a lot, and that pays for your height. So the height left over, added to the change in balance, is never above a small fixed fee. Add up all T steps and the balances in between cancel out.

33.3 · not knowing R and L

Line search: try step sizes along the way down

If you can't work out the step size in advance, pick the direction first, then try longer steps until going further stops helping.

The theorem wants R, the distance to a lowest point you don't yet know. So where does a real step size come from? One answer is to find a new one at each step. You already know which way is down, so the only choice left is how far.

Imagine standing on a hillside and looking along the slope. Try a small step and see where it would land. Double it. Double it again. Each time, ask whether landing there is lower. As soon as doubling doesn't help, you have overshot the low point on this line. So the best length is near there, and you can narrow it down by halving the gap, the same trick as binary search.

This is line search, because you search along one straight line. It costs extra evaluations of the function at each step. In return you don't need to know R or L, and the step can differ from one move to the next. The book says that not having a fixed step size can actually help in practice.

33.3 · constrained gradient descent

When some answers aren't allowed

If the answer must stay inside a region, take the usual step, and if that leaves the region, jump to the nearest point inside.

Often not every answer is allowed. A park has a fence, and the lowest ground lies outside it. You want the lowest point inside the fence. This is constrained minimization. The fenced area is a convex body: a shape where the straight line between any two of its points stays inside. A disk or a box qualifies, and a crescent does not.

The fix is almost too simple. Take the normal gradient step. If you end up outside, go to the closest point inside. That jump is called projection. Nothing else in the procedure changes.

Won't that jump undo your progress? The book's key lemma says no. When the region is convex, moving to the closest inside point can't take you further from any point inside. That includes the best allowed point. So the whole proof carries over, and the same limit on the error holds. The book calls this somewhat surprising.

33.3 · applications: linear systems

An equation hidden in a bowl

To solve an equation, build a bowl whose slope is zero exactly where the equation holds. Then walk down it.

Solve 3x = 12. You know the answer is 4, and the book admits that using gradient descent here is roundabout and inefficient. But look how it works. Rewrite the equation as 3x − 12 = 0. Now find a curve whose slope is 3x − 12: that curve is ½·3x² − 12x, a bowl. Its lowest point is where the slope is zero, and that is the solution.

The same trick works with many unknowns at once. For a set of linear equations Ax = b, there is a many-dimensional bowl whose gradient is Ax − b. So each step only has to multiply by A and see how far off you are. When the gradient reaches zero, the equations hold.

The usual way to solve these, Gaussian elimination, takes time that grows with the cube of the number of unknowns. For huge systems where a close answer will do, walking downhill can be faster. There is a condition, though: the bowl must really be a bowl. The book's word for this condition on A is positive-semidefinite. Without it, the walk can run away.

What the book says

Chapter 28's Gaussian elimination solves Ax = b in Θ(n³) time, which might be prohibitively expensive for large matrices. If an approximate solution is acceptable, you can use gradient descent. In n dimensions the analog is f(x) = ½xᵀAx − bᵀx, whose gradient is Ax − b. So if A is positive-semidefinite, gradient descent can find a point where Ax ≈ b, and if R and L are not too large, it is faster than Gaussian elimination.

33.3 · gradient descent in machine learning

The ball is now a line

To fit a line through data, treat the line's two numbers as your position and total squared error as your height. Then walk downhill.

The book's example is predicting heart disease. Say you have six patients, and for each, the number of close relatives with heart disease and how severe the patient's disease is. A straight line through those points would let you guess the severity for a new patient.

A line is fixed by two numbers, called weights: w₀, its height at zero, and w₁, how steeply it climbs. For any line, each patient has an error: the line's guess minus the true value. Square the errors and add them up. That total is the loss function. It is the same idea of an objective as the score f for clusters: one number saying how badly things fit.

Here comes the shift in thinking. The patients don't change. What you move is the weights. Think of (w₀, w₁) as a position on a map, and the loss as the height there. That landscape is a convex bowl, so there are no false bottoms, and gradient descent can find its way to the best line. Finding that line is called linear regression.

What the book says

The book's example: for each of m patients, n attributes such as age, height, blood pressure and number of close family members with heart disease, and a label for the severity of heart disease. It models the relationship as linear: f(x) = w₀ + Σ wjxj (33.32). The error is e(i) = f(x(i)) − y(i), and the objective is the sum of their squares (33.33), typically called the loss function. The loss is convex, as a sum of squares of linear functions, so gradient descent applies. If features are normalized, the weights tend to have a natural interpretation: better predictors get larger weights. The computed weights form a model, used to predict the label of a new patient. Each entry of the gradient is linear in its weight, and the gradient can be computed in O(nm) time (Exercise 33.3-5). Compared with the exact method of Chapter 28, which inverts a matrix, gradient descent is typically much faster.

Regularization can be achieved by a constraint such as ‖w‖ ≤ B, which controls the complexity of the model. Projection is by scaling: w = w′B/‖w′‖ when w′ is outside. Then L = O(B) and R = O(B), so the accuracy after T iterations is O(B²/√T). The page uses one attribute so that the line can be drawn; the six patients, their numbers and γ are its own.

Learning from one example at a time

Instead of reading all the data before each step, pick one example at random and step at once. Each step is rough but cheap.

The patient chart has twelve points. A full gradient reads all twelve to make one step. If you had a million patients, that would be a million readings per step. And in some settings the data comes one point at a time anyway, so you can't read it all first.

So use only one example. Pick a point at random and compute just that point's slope, as though it were the whole data set. That gives a rough guess at the gradient, which the book calls a stochastic estimate, a word that means “involving chance”. Take a small step on that guess, and go on to the next.

A single step can make things worse for the data as a whole, since it serves only one point. You can see the wobble in the chart. But the wobbles lean downhill. Per example read, the rough method can get ahead early on, since it has already moved many times while the full method has moved once.

problem 33-1 · Newton's method

A cousin that looks for zero

To find where a curve crosses zero, follow the line touching it down to zero, and repeat. Near a good root, this is very quick.

Gradient descent hunts for a low point. Newton's method hunts for a root, a place where the curve's height is exactly 0. It also moves by repeating a small rule, using the slope. Draw the straight line that touches the curve where you are. Slide down it to where it crosses zero, and use that spot as the next guess.

Try it on x² − 2, whose root is √2. Starting from x = 3, the guesses go 1.83, 1.46, 1.415, 1.41421. Once it is close, each step about doubles the count of correct digits. The book calls this quadratic convergence, since the error is roughly squared at each step.

The book then sets a trap as an exercise, f(x) = (x − 3)² from x = 3.5. Here the root is also the minimizer, so the curve just touches zero, with slope 0 there. Run it in the machine: Newton's error only halves each step, and the speed-up is gone. Newton's assumptions rule this case out, because they ask that the slope isn't zero anywhere in the interval you work in.

Read the maths
x⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = x⁽ᵗ⁾ − f(x⁽ᵗ⁾) / f′(x⁽ᵗ⁾)
f(x)
The height where you are: how far from zero.
f′(x)
The slope there. Height ÷ slope is how far you must slide along the tangent.

With numbers: f(x) = x² − 2 at x = 3: the height is 7 and the slope is 2 × 3 = 6, so the next guess is 3 − 7 ÷ 6 ≈ 1.833.

The chapter on one page

The key ideas, in order.

Learning from data

Supervised learning starts from labeled examples and produces a hypothesis that predicts labels. Unsupervised learning has no labels, as in clustering. Online learning mixes training and prediction.

The k-means score

f adds up the squared distance from every point to its nearest center. Finding the centers with the lowest possible f is NP-hard, so we settle for a local minimum.

Lloyd's two steps

Assign each point to its nearest center. Move each center to the mean of its group. Neither step can raise f, so the procedure always stops, in O(Tdkn) time for T rounds.

A local answer only

Lloyd's result depends on the start, and it can be far from the best. One remedy from the book: run it many times from different random starts and keep the best.

Weights that shrink

WEIGHTED-MAJORITY follows the heavier side of a weighted vote and multiplies each wrong expert's weight by 1 − γ. Nobody is ever thrown out.

Regret stays bounded

Your mistakes are at most 2(1 + γ) times the best expert's, plus 2 ln n / γ. With the book's 20 experts, 1000 events and a best of 50 errors, γ = 1/4 gives at most 149.

Step against the slope

Gradient descent repeats x = x − γ · gradient. Too small a γ crawls. Too large a γ overshoots and can run away. On a bumpy curve it finds only a local minimum.

Convex means no false bottoms

A straight string between two points of a convex function never dips under the curve. So every local minimum is also a global one.

The price of accuracy

With γ = R/(L√T), the averaged answer is within RL/√T of the minimum. To halve the error bound, run four times as many steps.

Projection keeps you inside

For a constraint, take the same step, then jump to the closest allowed point. If the allowed region is convex, that jump never takes you further from the best allowed point. The bound stays the same.

Many problems are downhill walks

Solving Ax = b, and fitting a line by least squares, both reduce to minimizing a convex function. A limit on the size of the weights just adds a projection.

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

training phase

prediction phase

label
The right answer attached to a training example, such as “spam”.
supervised learning
Learning from examples that each come with a label.
unsupervised learning
Learning from data with no labels at all.
online learning
Learning where training and prediction are mixed: you act, see the result, and learn as you go.
feature vector
The list of numbers that stands for one example, such as a student's grade average and income.
attribute
One number in a feature vector: one measured property of the example.
dissimilarity
How unlike two feature vectors are. In this chapter, the squared distance between them.
objective / loss function
The one number a method tries to make as small as it can. It measures how badly things fit.
center
A point that stands for a group. Each data point joins its nearest one.
cluster
A group of points, here all the points that share a nearest center.
k-means problem
Choose k centers so that the total squared distance from each point to its nearest center is as small as possible.
centroid
The mean of a group of points, found one attribute at a time. It is the best center for that group.
Lloyd's procedure
The method for k-means: assign each point to its nearest center, move each center to its group's mean, and repeat until nothing changes.
local minimum
A point that is lowest among its near neighbours. There may be something lower further away.
palette
The short table of colours used by a compressed picture. Each pixel stores only a position in it.
vector quantization
Replacing each point by the nearest of a few chosen points, so that fewer distinct values have to be stored.
online
Said of a problem where you must act on each piece of input before seeing the next.
expert
Anything that makes a prediction for you. It need not be good at it.
regret
Your mistakes minus the mistakes of the best expert, judged at the end.
multiplicative weights
A family of methods that keep a weight per option and change it by multiplying.
weighted-majority algorithm
Follow the heavier side of a weighted vote, and multiply each wrong expert's weight by 1 − γ.
slope
How much the height changes per unit moved to the right, at one spot on a curve.
derivative
The school-maths name for the slope of a curve at a point, written f′(x).
gradient
The list of slopes, one for each direction. As an arrow, it points the way the function rises fastest.

The number γ that scales each move in gradient descent. It is a setting you choose, not something computed from the data.
gradient descent
Repeatedly move against the gradient, by an amount proportional to it, to reach a low point.
global minimum
The lowest value a function reaches anywhere.

The average of the points gradient descent visited, leaving out the final one. It is what the book's procedure returns.
contour lines
Lines on a map that join points of the same height.
convex
Bowl-like: a straight string between any two points of the curve never dips below the curve. A local minimum of a convex function is also a global one.

Choosing the step size at each step, by trying sizes along the downhill direction.
constrained
Said of a minimization where the answer must stay inside an allowed region.

Moving a point to the closest point of a region. A point already inside doesn't move.

The model's guess for an example minus that example's true label.
linear regression
Finding the straight-line relationship that minimizes the total squared error on the data.

Involving chance. A stochastic gradient uses a randomly chosen example instead of all of them.

Find a root by repeatedly sliding down the tangent line to where it crosses zero.

A value of x at which f(x) = 0.

When each error is about a constant times the square of the error before, so that the correct digits roughly double each step.
