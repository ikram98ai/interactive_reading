Quicksort

Quicksort picks one element, the pivot, moves everything smaller to its left and everything bigger to its right, and then sorts the two sides the same way. Its worst case is Θ(n²), as slow as insertion sort, yet with a randomly chosen pivot its expected time is Θ(n lg n) on any input of distinct numbers, with small constants: often the best practical choice.

“Combine by doing nothing: because the two subarrays are already sorted, no work is needed to combine them.” — Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest & Stein

7.1 · description of quicksort

Pick a pivot, and it never moves again

Merge sort does its hard work at the end, when it merges. Quicksort does it at the start. It picks one element, the pivot, and rearranges the subarray so that everything no bigger than the pivot sits to its left and everything no smaller sits to its right. From that moment the pivot is in its final sorted place. The two sides are then sorted the same way, recursively, and they never need to touch again.

In divide-and-conquer terms: divide by partitioning A[p : r] into a low side A[p : q − 1] and a high side A[q + 1 : r] around the pivot A[q]; conquer by calling QUICKSORT on each side; combine by doing nothing. Either side may be empty, and a subarray with fewer than two elements (p ≥ r) is already sorted. The first call is QUICKSORT(A, 1, n).

All of this happens inside the one array: quicksort sorts in place. Its worst case is Θ(n²), yet it is often the best practical choice, because its expected running time is Θ(n lg n) when the elements are distinct, with small constant factors.

What the book says

Quicksort has a worst-case running time of Θ(n²) on an input array of n numbers. Despite this, it is often the best practical choice for sorting because it is remarkably efficient on average: its expected running time is Θ(n lg n) when all numbers are distinct, and the constant factors hidden in the Θ(n lg n) notation are small. Unlike merge sort, it sorts in place, and it works well even in virtual-memory environments.

Quicksort, like merge sort, applies divide-and-conquer. Divide by partitioning (rearranging) A[p : r] into two possibly empty subarrays A[p : q − 1] (the low side) and A[q + 1 : r] (the high side), such that each element of the low side is less than or equal to the pivot A[q], which is in turn less than or equal to each element of the high side; computing q is part of partitioning. Conquer by calling quicksort recursively on both subarrays.

“Combine by doing nothing: because the two subarrays are already sorted, no work is needed to combine them.” Every element of A[p : q − 1] is sorted and at most A[q], every element of A[q + 1 : r] is sorted and at least A[q], so the whole subarray “cannot help but be sorted!” To sort an entire n-element array A[1 : n], the initial call is QUICKSORT(A, 1, n).

The analysis assumes distinct values. A footnote shows how to enforce that at the cost of Θ(n) extra space and constant overhead: replace each A[i] by the pair (A[i], i), comparing indices when values tie. Section 7.2 discusses performance informally, 7.3 presents a randomized version, and 7.4 analyzes it precisely.

7.1 · partitioning the array

One sweep, four regions

PARTITION does all of quicksort's real work, in one left-to-right sweep. It takes the last element as the pivot x, then walks an index j across everything else. Each element it meets is either small (≤ x) or big (> x). A small one is swapped forward to join the low side. A big one is left where it is, and the high side simply grows behind it.

At every moment the subarray is in four regions: A[p : i] holds values ≤ x, A[i + 1 : j − 1] holds values > x, A[j : r − 1] has not been looked at, and A[r] is x. That statement is the loop invariant. Each iteration keeps it true in one of two ways (Figure 7.3): if A[j] > x, only j moves; if A[j] ≤ x, i moves up, A[i] and A[j] trade places, then j moves. When j reaches r, one last exchange puts the pivot between the two sides, and PARTITION returns its index q.

The loop runs exactly r − p times with one comparison each, plus constant work around it, so partitioning n = r − p + 1 elements takes Θ(n) time and needs no second array: it works in place.

Try it: press Step to walk through Figure 7.1 one line at a time, or Play. Mint cells are the low side, blue the high side, yellow the pivot, white still unknown. At the start of every iteration (line 3) the three checks at the bottom test the invariant on the real array. Try Exercise 7.1-1 (the book's 12-element exercise) and Random. In Rust, i becomes lo = i + 1, because a usize cannot start at −1.

What the book says

The key to the algorithm is the PARTITION procedure, which rearranges the subarray A[p : r] in place and returns the index of the dividing point between the two sides of the partition. It always selects the element x = A[r] as the pivot. As it runs, each element falls into exactly one of four regions, some of which may be empty (Figure 7.2).

The loop invariant: at the beginning of each iteration of the loop of lines 3–6, for any array index k, (1) if p ≤ k ≤ i, then A[k] ≤ x; (2) if i + 1 ≤ k ≤ j − 1, then A[k] > x; (3) if k = r, then A[k] = x.

Initialization: before the first iteration, i = p − 1 and j = p, so no values lie in the first two regions and those conditions hold trivially; line 1 makes the third true. Maintenance: if A[j] > x, the only action is to increment j; if A[j] ≤ x, the loop increments i, swaps A[i] and A[j], then increments j (Figure 7.3). Termination: the loop makes exactly r − p iterations and ends with j = r, so the unexamined region is empty and every value is in the low side, the high side, or the singleton set holding the pivot.

The final two lines swap the pivot with the leftmost element greater than x, moving it into its correct place, and return its new index. In Figure 7.1 the value 2 is “swapped with itself” and put into the low side. The output satisfies a slightly stronger condition than the divide step asks for: after line 3 of QUICKSORT, A[q] is strictly less than every element of A[q + 1 : r]. Exercise 7.1-3 asks for the argument that PARTITION on n = r − p + 1 elements runs in Θ(n) time.

7.2 · worst-case and best-case partitioning

A staircase or a block

How fast quicksort runs depends on one thing: how evenly PARTITION splits. In the worst case every split is as lopsided as it can be, n − 1 elements on one side and none on the other. Each level of recursion then removes a single pivot, still pays Θ(n) to partition what is left, and there are about n levels: T(n) = T(n − 1) + Θ(n), an arithmetic series that sums to Θ(n²).

The cruel part is which input does it. PARTITION always takes the last element as the pivot, so on an array that is already sorted the pivot is the largest element every time, and the high side is always empty. Insertion sort finishes that same input in O(n). In the best case each split is as even as possible, both sides at most n/2: T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n), which is Θ(n lg n), as fast as merge sort.

Depth costs memory too. Quicksort sorts in place, but every pending call holds a small frame on the stack, so the extra space grows with the depth of the recursion: Θ(n) in the worst case.

Try it: press Step to run both inputs one level of the recursion tree at a time, or Play. Each row is a level and each bar a call, as wide as its subarray, so a row's width is that level's partitioning cost and the whole shape's area is the total work. The bars at the top are the array itself. Raise n to 64: the sorted staircase reaches 63 levels while the best case stops at 6. Switch the right side to Shuffled (press it again for a new shuffle) and see how close a random order comes to the best case.

What the book says

The running time of quicksort depends on how balanced each partitioning is, which in turn depends on which elements are used as pivots. If the two sides are about the same size, quicksort runs asymptotically as fast as merge sort; if the partitioning is unbalanced, it can run asymptotically as slowly as insertion sort.

Although quicksort sorts in place, the memory it uses aside from the array is not constant: each recursive call requires a constant amount of space on the runtime stack, so quicksort requires space proportional to the maximum depth of the recursion, which could be as bad as Θ(n) in the worst case.

Worst case: partitioning produces one subproblem with n − 1 elements and one with 0. Partitioning costs Θ(n) and T(0) = Θ(1), so T(n) = T(n − 1) + T(0) + Θ(n) = T(n − 1) + Θ(n). Summing the costs level by level gives an arithmetic series, Θ(n²). “The worst-case running time of quicksort is therefore no better than that of insertion sort.” Moreover, it occurs when the input array is already completely sorted, a situation in which insertion sort runs in O(n) time.

Best case: in the most even possible split, PARTITION produces two subproblems, each of size no more than n/2, giving T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n). By case 2 of the master theorem, T(n) = Θ(n lg n).

7.2 · balanced partitioning

Lopsided, and still n lg n

Worst and best are the two extremes. What about a split that is lopsided but steady: PARTITION always puts 9/10 of the elements on one side and 1/10 on the other? The recurrence is T(n) = T(9n/10) + T(n/10) + Θ(n). That sounds bad. It is in fact O(n lg n).

Look at the recursion tree (Figure 7.4), with the Θ(n) replaced by n. The subproblems on one level never hold more than n elements between them, so no level costs more than n. The thin side runs out first, at depth log10 n, and down to there every level costs exactly n. The fat side shrinks by 9/10 per level and stops at depth log10/9 n. That is still Θ(lg n), only with a bigger constant: at most n per level, times Θ(lg n) levels.

Any split of constant proportionality works the same way, 99 to 1 included: the tree is Θ(lg n) deep and each level costs O(n), so the ratio changes only the constant hidden in the O. What really hurts is a split that is not a proportion at all, like the 0 to n − 1 of the sorted input.

Try it: press Play to grow the tree. The top rows are its first three levels with their sizes, as in Figure 7.4. Below them is every level at once: each row's width is that level's cost, so the shape's area is the total work, and the dashed outline is the even 1-to-1 tree at the same scale. Drag split to 99 to 1, then raise n to a million: the tree gets much deeper, yet ÷ n lg n stays put for a fixed split. Change only the split and that constant moves.

What the book says

As the analyses in Section 7.4 will show, the average-case running time of quicksort is much closer to the best case than to the worst case. To see why, suppose the partitioning always produces a 9-to-1 proportional split, “which at first blush seems quite unbalanced.” The recurrence is T(n) = T(9n/10) + T(n/10) + Θ(n).

Figure 7.4 shows its recursion tree, with the Θ(n) driving function replaced by n, which doesn't affect the asymptotic solution (Exercise 4.7-1). Every level of the tree has cost n until the recursion reaches a base case at depth log10 n = Θ(lg n); after that, the levels cost at most n. The recursion terminates at depth log10/9 n = Θ(lg n).

So with a 9-to-1 split at every level, “which intuitively seems highly unbalanced,” quicksort runs in O(n lg n) time, asymptotically the same as a split down the middle. Even a 99-to-1 split gives O(n lg n). Any split of constant proportionality yields a recursion tree of depth Θ(lg n) with O(n) cost per level; the ratio affects only the constant hidden in the O-notation.

Exercise 7.2-5 generalizes: with splits in proportion α to β at every level, where α + β = 1 and 0 < α ≤ β < 1, the minimum depth of a leaf is approximately log1/α n and the maximum depth approximately log1/β n.

7.2 · intuition for the average case

A bad split hides inside a good one

To talk about an “average” input you have to say what inputs look like. Quicksort only ever compares elements, so what matters is their relative order, not their values. The book assumes the elements are distinct and every permutation is equally likely. Then PARTITION does not split the same way at every level: about 80% of the time it splits at least as evenly as 9 to 1, and about 20% of the time worse (Exercise 7.2-6). The good and bad splits land at random places in the recursion tree.

The book's thought experiment: let bad and good splits take turns. At the root, partitioning n elements costs n and leaves sizes 0 and n − 1, the worst case. At the next level the n − 1 elements split evenly. Two levels cost Θ(n) + Θ(n − 1) = Θ(n) and leave pieces of size 0, (n − 1)/2 − 1 and (n − 1)/2, no bigger than one good split leaves. The bad split's cost is absorbed into the good split's, so the total stays O(n lg n), only with a slightly larger constant.

The machine draws the recursion as rows of bars. Each row is one level, and each bar is one call's subarray, drawn under its own positions in the array. A mint bar is a good split, a coral one a bad split, and the lilac cell is the pivot, which is now in its final place. The number at the end of a row counts that level's comparisons. It can never be more than n.

Try it: press Play on Random input and look for the coral bars: they are scattered, mostly near the bottom. Switch to Bad, good, bad…: every other row is coral, and the tree is nearly twice as deep as Always good. Compare the comparison counts: about 150 against 94, and both far below the 435 of the worst case. New input draws another input of the same kind.

What the book says

To develop a notion of the expected behavior you must assume something about how inputs are distributed. “Because quicksort determines the sorted order using only comparisons between input elements, its behavior depends on the relative ordering of the values in the array elements given as the input, not on the particular values in the array.” As in the analysis of the hiring problem (Section 5.2), assume that all permutations of the input numbers are equally likely and that the elements are distinct.

On a random input “the partitioning is highly unlikely to happen in the same way at every level”. Exercise 7.2-6 asks you to show that about 80% of the time PARTITION produces a split at least as balanced as 9 to 1, and about 20% of the time a less balanced one. In the average case PARTITION produces a mix of good and bad splits, and “the good and bad splits are distributed randomly throughout the tree.”

For intuition, suppose good and bad splits alternate levels, the good ones best-case and the bad ones worst-case (Figure 7.5). At the root the cost is n and the subarrays have sizes n − 1 and 0. The subarray of size n − 1 then splits into sizes (n − 1)/2 − 1 and (n − 1)/2, taking the base-case cost of the empty subarray as 1. The combined cost is Θ(n) + Θ(n − 1) = Θ(n). “This situation is at most a constant factor worse than that in Figure 7.5(b)”, where one level of partitioning produces two subarrays of size (n − 1)/2 at cost Θ(n). “Yet this latter situation is balanced!”

The Θ(n − 1) cost of the bad split can be absorbed into the Θ(n) cost of the good split. When levels alternate between good and bad splits, the running time is like that for good splits alone: “still O(n lg n), but with a slightly larger constant hidden by the O-notation.” Section 7.4.2 analyzes the randomized version rigorously.

7.3 · a randomized version of quicksort

No input is the bad one

The average-case story assumed that every input order is equally likely. Real inputs don't behave that way. Bank transactions arrive almost sorted (Exercise 7.2-4), and on a sorted array PARTITION picks the largest element as the pivot every time: the worst case. An adversary who knows your pivot rule can always hand you that input.

So stop letting the input choose the pivot. RANDOMIZED-PARTITION picks an index i = RANDOM(p, r), with every element of A[p : r] equally likely, swaps A[i] into A[r], and then runs the ordinary PARTITION. RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT calls it in place of PARTITION, and nothing else changes. Section 5.3 shuffled the whole input first. Picking a random pivot does the same job and makes the analysis simpler.

The running time is now a random variable that depends on the algorithm's own coin flips, not on the input. When the elements are distinct, its distribution is the same for every input order, whether sorted, reversed or shuffled. A slow run is still possible, but no input can force one.

Try it: the adversary's input is Sorted. With pivot A[r], press Play: each call peels off just one element, and the run costs 120 comparisons. Switch to random pivot and Play: RANDOM picks a cell, the pick is swapped to the end, and PARTITION splits around it. Then press Run 500: the counts form a hump around 51, far left of the coral QUICKSORT line. Try Reversed and Shuffled. The coral line moves, the hump doesn't.

What the book says

The chapter's plan: when all elements are distinct, the randomized algorithm has a good expected running time and “no particular input elicits its worst-case behavior.” Problem 7-2 covers the case where elements may be equal.

The average-case exploration assumed all permutations of the input are equally likely. “This assumption does not always hold”, as in the almost-sorted input of Exercise 7.2-4. Section 5.3 showed that judicious randomization can give good expected performance over all inputs. “For quicksort, randomization yields a fast and practical algorithm. Many software libraries provide a randomized version of quicksort as their algorithm of choice for sorting large data sets.”

RANDOMIZED-HIRE-ASSISTANT explicitly permuted its input. Quicksort could do the same, but “a different randomization technique yields a simpler analysis”: instead of always using A[r] as the pivot, choose the pivot randomly from A[p : r], each element with equal probability, and exchange it with A[r] before partitioning. “Because the pivot is chosen randomly, we expect the split of the input array to be reasonably well balanced on average.”

“The changes to PARTITION and QUICKSORT are small.” RANDOMIZED-PARTITION swaps before partitioning, and RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT calls RANDOMIZED-PARTITION instead of PARTITION. Exercise 7.3-1 asks why we analyze the expected running time of a randomized algorithm and not its worst case. Exercise 7.3-2 asks how many calls to RANDOM are made in the worst case and in the best case.

7.4.1 · worst-case analysis

The worst split sits at the ends

Section 7.2 showed one bad input that costs Θ(n2). To call that the worst case, you have to prove that nothing is worse. This proof covers QUICKSORT and RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT alike. PARTITION leaves two subproblems of sizes q and n − 1 − q, and the worst-case time T(n) takes the worst q: T(n) = max { T(q) + T(n − 1 − q) : 0 ≤ q ≤ n − 1 } + Θ(n).

Guess T(n) ≤ cn2 and substitute it for both halves (the substitution method). What is left to bound is the largest value of q2 + (n − 1 − q)2. It equals (n − 1)2 + 2q(q − (n − 1)), and the second term is never positive, so the maximum is (n − 1)2, reached at q = 0 and q = n − 1. The most lopsided split is the costliest.

That leaves T(n) ≤ c(n − 1)2 + Θ(n) = cn2 − c(2n − 1) + Θ(n). The guess survives if c(2n − 1), the room the guess leaves, covers the Θ(n) term. Pick c big enough and it always does. So T(n) = O(n2), and with the sorted-input example the worst case is Θ(n2).

Try it: in max over q, press Play to scan every split. The bar at the bottom shows the split, and the dots trace a bowl whose two rims, q = 0 and q = n − 1, are the highest. Drag n: the shape stays the same. Then open c large enough and press Play with c = 0.6: the guess fails at n = 1 and 2 and holds from 3 on. Raise c to 1 and every n passes. Raise d, the Θ(n) constant, and you need a bigger c.

What the book says

Section 7.4 begins with a worst-case analysis, “which applies to either QUICKSORT or RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT”. Section 7.2 showed that a worst-case split at every level gives Θ(n2), which intuitively is the worst case; now the book proves it. “We’ll use the substitution method (see Section 4.3) to show that the running time of quicksort is O(n²).” With T(n) the worst-case time on an input of size n, and PARTITION producing two subproblems of total size n − 1, the recurrence is (7.1): T(n) = max {T(q) + T(n − 1 − q) : 0 ≤ q ≤ n − 1} + Θ(n).

Guess T(n) ≤ cn2 for some constant c > 0. Substituting gives T(n) ≤ c · max {q2 + (n − 1 − q)2 : 0 ≤ q ≤ n − 1} + Θ(n). For q = 0, 1, …, n − 1, q2 + (n − 1 − q)2 = (n − 1)2 + 2q(q − (n − 1)) ≤ (n − 1)2, “because q ≤ n − 1 implies that 2q(q − (n − 1)) ≤ 0. Thus every term in the maximization is bounded by (n − 1)².”

Then T(n) ≤ c(n − 1)2 + Θ(n) ≤ cn2 − c(2n − 1) + Θ(n) ≤ cn2, “by picking the constant c large enough that the c(2n − 1) term dominates the Θ(n) term.” So T(n) = O(n2). “Section 7.2 showed a specific case where quicksort takes Ω(n²) time: when partitioning is maximally unbalanced. Thus, the worst-case running time of quicksort is Θ(n²).”

Exercise 7.4-1 asks for the matching lower bound of the recurrence, Ω(n2). Exercise 7.4-3 asks you to show that q2 + (n − q − 1)2 achieves its maximum over q = 0, 1, …, n − 1 when q = 0 or q = n − 1, which is what the first mode of the machine shows.

7.4.2 · expected running time

Count the pairs, not the steps

Every element comparison quicksort makes happens in one line, line 4 of PARTITION, and outside that loop each of the at most n PARTITION calls does O(1) work. So the running time is O(n + X), where X is the number of element comparisons (Lemma 7.1). To bound the expected time, bound E[X]. Name the elements by their sorted order, z1 < z2 < … < zn, and let Zij = {zi, …, zj}.

When are zi and zj compared? Only when one of them is the pivot. All of Zij stays together in one subarray until the first pivot is chosen from it. If that pivot is zi or zj, the two are compared, and only once, because a pivot never takes part again. If it is anything in between, they land on opposite sides and are never compared (Lemma 7.2). Each of the j − i + 1 elements is equally likely to come first, so Pr{compared} = 2/(j − i + 1) (Lemma 7.3).

X is a sum of indicator random variables, one per pair, so by linearity of expectation E[X] = Σi<j 2/(j − i + 1). Neighbors are always compared and far-apart pairs rarely. With k = j − i, each inner sum is less than Σk≤n 2/k = O(lg n), so E[X] = O(n lg n) (Theorem 7.4). Add the Θ(n lg n) best case and the expected time is Θ(n lg n).

Run 1000 turns the left grid into how often each pair was compared, and it matches the formula on the right.

What the book says

QUICKSORT and RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT differ only in how they pick pivots, so the analysis uses QUICKSORT and PARTITION with pivots chosen randomly. Lemma 7.1: the running time of QUICKSORT on an n-element array is O(n + X), X being the number of element comparisons. Each PARTITION call picks a pivot that is never included in later calls, so there are at most n calls; each takes O(1) time plus time proportional to its for-loop iterations, each of which makes one comparison in line 4. “Note that we are counting the number of times that array elements are compared, not comparisons of indices.” “We assume throughout that the values of the elements being sorted are distinct.”

Lemma 7.2: zi is compared with zj (i < j) if and only if one of them is chosen as a pivot before any other element of Zij. “Moreover, no two elements are ever compared twice.” The example: the numbers 1 through 10 with first pivot 7, which splits them into {1, …, 6} and {8, 9, 10}. “The values 7 and 9 are compared because 7 is the first item from Z7,9 to be chosen as a pivot. In contrast, 2 and 9 are never compared because the first pivot element chosen from Z2,9 is 7.”

Lemma 7.3: the probability that zi and zj are compared is 2/(j − i + 1). The elements of Zij stay together until some x ∈ Zij is chosen as pivot, each equally likely since the pivot is uniform, and |Zij| = j − i + 1. The probability is a sum of two terms “where the second line follows from the first because the two events are mutually exclusive.”

Theorem 7.4: “The expected running time of RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT on an input of n distinct elements is O(n lg n).” With Xij = I{zi is compared with zj}, X = Σi=1n−1 Σj=i+1n Xij. Linearity of expectation and Lemma 5.1 give E[X] = Σ Σ 2/(j − i + 1) = Σi Σk=1n−i 2/(k + 1) < Σi Σk=1n 2/k = Σi O(lg n) = O(n lg n), using the harmonic series bound (A.9). With the Θ(n lg n) best case, the expected running time is Θ(n lg n).

problem 7-1 · hoare partition correctness

Two scouts walking toward each other

The PARTITION of Section 7.1 is not the first one. C. A. R. Hoare's original takes the first element, x = A[p], as the pivot and sends two scouts in from the ends. j walks left until it stands on an element ≤ x; i walks right until it stands on one ≥ x. Each one has found an element on the wrong side, so a single exchange fixes two of them. When the scouts meet or cross, j is the dividing line: this is HOARE-PARTITION.

Its promise is weaker than PARTITION's. Every element of A[p : j] is ≤ every element of A[j + 1 : r], but the pivot is not in its final place; it ends up inside one of the two sides. So quicksort recurses on A[p : j] and A[j + 1 : r], and it still makes progress because p ≤ j < r: neither side is empty.

Why do the scouts never run off the array? On the first pass i stops at p at once, because A[p] = x. After an exchange, A[i] ≤ x and A[j] ≥ x, and those two elements act as sentinels: the next scan of j must stop at or before the old i, and i at or before the old j. The practical win is fewer exchanges, and on equal keys a split down the middle instead of PARTITION's n − 1 to 0.

What the book says

The version of PARTITION given in the chapter is not the original partitioning algorithm. The original, due to C. A. R. Hoare, is HOARE-PARTITION(A, p, r): x = A[p], i = p − 1, j = r + 1, then a while loop that decrements j until A[j] ≤ x, increments i until A[i] ≥ x, and exchanges the two if i < j, else returns j. Part (a) asks you to demonstrate it on A = ⟨13, 19, 9, 5, 12, 8, 7, 4, 11, 2, 6, 21⟩, showing the array and the indices i and j after each iteration of the while loop in lines 4–13.

Part (b) asks how the PARTITION procedure of Section 7.1 differs from HOARE-PARTITION when all elements in A[p : r] are equal, and for a practical advantage of HOARE-PARTITION over PARTITION for use in quicksort.

Assuming the subarray holds at least two elements, parts (c)–(e) ask you to prove that the indices i and j never access an element of A outside A[p : r]; that the procedure returns a value j with p ≤ j < r; and that when it terminates, every element of A[p : j] is less than or equal to every element of A[j + 1 : r].

PARTITION separates the pivot value (originally in A[r]) from the two partitions it forms. HOARE-PARTITION, on the other hand, always places the pivot value (originally in A[p]) into one of the two partitions A[p : j] and A[j + 1 : r], and since p ≤ j < r, neither partition is empty. Part (f) asks you to rewrite QUICKSORT to use HOARE-PARTITION.

From the chapter notes: “Quicksort was invented by Hoare, and his version of PARTITION appears in Problem 7-1.” Bentley attributes the PARTITION procedure of Section 7.1 to N. Lomuto.

HOARE-PARTITION(A, 1, 12)
problem 7-2 · quicksort with equal element values

A bag of identical marbles

Sorting a bag of identical marbles should take no work at all. Quicksort with PARTITION takes the most. PARTITION sends every element with A[j] ≤ x to the low side, and when every key equals the pivot that is all of them: the split is n − 1 to 0, every time. Random pivots can't help, because every pivot is the same value. Randomized quicksort, Θ(n lg n) expected on distinct keys, becomes Θ(n²) on equal ones, and it gets close to that whenever a few values repeat many times.

The fix is a three-way partition, the book's PARTITION′. It splits A[p : r] into keys less than the pivot, keys equal to it, and keys greater, and returns the two ends q and t of the middle block. The middle block is already in its final place, so QUICKSORT′ recurses only on A[p : q − 1] and A[t + 1 : r]: the parts whose elements are not known to be equal. All keys equal? One pass, and nothing is left.

PARTITION′ keeps four regions: < x in A[p : q − 1], = x in A[q : k − 1], not yet seen in A[k : t], and > x in A[t + 1 : r]. Each step looks at A[k] once and either moves k right or t left, so the unseen region shrinks by one every time: Θ(r − p), like PARTITION.

Try it: press Play on Two-way with 3 distinct keys and see how many elements are left to sort. Then switch to Three-way and play the same array: the mint block of keys equal to the pivot is finished. Drag distinct keys down to 1 and compare the bars, which count a whole sort of 240 keys: 28,680 comparisons against 240. At 12 values the gap narrows but stays about fourfold, because each value still appears some 20 times. Only with (nearly) distinct keys do the two cost the same, as the last line of the Rust program's output shows.

What the book says

The analysis of the expected running time of randomized quicksort in Section 7.4.2 assumes that all element values are distinct; Problem 7-2 examines what happens when they are not. Part (a) asks for randomized quicksort's running time when all element values are equal, and Exercise 7.1-2 asks what value of q PARTITION returns in that case.

PARTITION returns an index q such that each element of A[p : q − 1] is less than or equal to A[q] and each element of A[q + 1 : r] is greater than A[q]. Part (b) asks for PARTITION′(A, p, r), which permutes the elements of A[p : r] and returns two indices q and t, where p ≤ q ≤ t ≤ r, such that all elements of A[q : t] are equal, each element of A[p : q − 1] is less than A[q], and each element of A[t + 1 : r] is greater than A[q]. Like PARTITION, it should take Θ(r − p) time.

Part (c) asks for RANDOMIZED-PARTITION′, which calls PARTITION′, and a procedure QUICKSORT′(A, p, r) that calls RANDOMIZED-PARTITION′ and “recurses only on partitions where elements are not known to be equal to each other.” Part (d) asks you to use QUICKSORT′ to adjust the analysis of Section 7.4.2 so that it no longer assumes distinct elements.

The chapter's first footnote adds that distinctness can be enforced at the cost of Θ(n) additional space by turning each value A[i] into the pair (A[i], i), with ties broken by index. “There are also more practical variants of quicksort that work well when elements are not distinct.”

One pass, two ways to split
problem 7-5 · stack depth for quicksort

The pile of unfinished calls

Every call that has started but not returned keeps a note on the call stack: its parameters and where to resume. Quicksort's second recursive call is the last thing it does, so it needs no note at all: set p = q + 1 and go around a while loop instead. That is tail-recursion elimination, and the result is the book's TRE-QUICKSORT, with one recursive call left, on the low side.

That one call is the trouble. On a sorted array, PARTITION's pivot A[r] is the largest key, the low side holds all the other n − 1, and each call has to open the next one before it can loop. All n calls are open at once: the stack depth is Θ(n). Θ(n²) time is merely slow; a call stack a million frames deep can overflow and crash the program.

The fix for part (c): partition, then make the recursive call on the smaller side and loop on the larger one. A recursive call then gets at most half of its caller's elements, so at most about lg n calls can be open at once, whatever the input. The work is the same as before, just in a different order, so the O(n lg n) expected time with random pivots stays.

Try it: with Sorted input, press Play and watch the memory strip under the machine grow one tre_quicksort frame per key, while the chart climbs to n. Then switch to Smaller side first and play again: the pile never gets past two frames. Try Reversed too, and read the at n = 1024 tile for each.

What the book says

The QUICKSORT procedure of Section 7.1 makes two recursive calls to itself: after it calls PARTITION, it sorts the low side recursively and then the high side. “The second recursive call in QUICKSORT is not really necessary, because the procedure can instead use an iterative control structure.” “This transformation technique, called tail-recursion elimination, is provided automatically by good compilers.” Applying it turns QUICKSORT into TRE-QUICKSORT, and part (a) asks you to argue that TRE-QUICKSORT(A, 1, n) correctly sorts A[1 : n].

Compilers usually execute recursive procedures with a stack holding the pertinent information for each call, including its parameter values. The most recent call is at the top and the initial call at the bottom; a call pushes its information and a return pops it. Since array parameters are represented by pointers, each call needs O(1) stack space. “The stack depth is the maximum amount of stack space used at any time during a computation.”

Part (b) asks for a scenario in which TRE-QUICKSORT's stack depth is Θ(n) on an n-element input array. Part (c) asks you to modify TRE-QUICKSORT so that the worst-case stack depth is Θ(lg n), while keeping the O(n lg n) expected running time.

Section 7.2 makes the same point about QUICKSORT itself: although it sorts in place, the memory it uses besides the array is not constant, because each recursive call takes a constant amount of space on the runtime stack. It needs space proportional to the maximum depth of the recursion, which could be as bad as Θ(n) in the worst case.

How deep does the stack get?
problem 7-6 · median-of-3 partition

Ask three, trust the middle one

A random pivot is as likely to be the smallest key as the median. The median-of-3 method hedges: draw three elements at random and partition around the middle one of the three. The extremes are now impossible: the smallest key could only be the middle of three if two of them were smaller still. So with distinct keys z1 < ⋯ < zn, p1 = pn = 0.

The pivot is zi exactly when the three draws are zi, one of the i − 1 smaller keys and one of the n − i larger ones. Out of the C(n, 3) equally likely sets that gives pi = 6(i − 1)(n − i) / (n(n − 1)(n − 2)): a hump in the middle instead of the random pivot's flat 1/n. For large n the true median comes up about 3/2 times as often, and a good split (n/3 ≤ i ≤ 2n/3) has probability close to ∫ 6t(1 − t) dt over the middle third, 13/27 ≈ 0.48 instead of 1/3: about 1.44 times as likely.

Part (d) is the sobering one. Even if every pivot were the exact median, quicksort would still do Ω(n lg n) work, so a smarter pivot can only make good splits more common. It changes the constant factor, never the growth rate. In exchange it costs three random draws and a tiny sort per partition.

Try it: press Pick to draw three keys, sort them and send the middle one to the histogram. The first draw on its own is a uniformly random pivot, so the same draws feed the grey bars too. Press Run 3000 a few times: the blue bars settle onto the dots of the exact formula, and the tiles compare hitting the median and landing a good split. Drag n and watch the hump keep its shape.

What the book says

One way to improve RANDOMIZED-QUICKSORT is to partition around a pivot chosen more carefully than by picking a random element from the subarray. “A common approach is the median-of-3 method: choose the pivot as the median (middle element) of a set of 3 elements randomly selected from the subarray.” The problem assumes that the n elements of A[p : r] are distinct and that n ≥ 3, writes their sorted order as z1, z2, …, zn, and defines pi = Pr{x = zi} for the median-of-3 pivot x.

Part (a) asks for an exact formula for pi as a function of n and i for i = 2, 3, …, n − 1, observing that p1 = pn = 0. Part (b) asks by what amount the method increases the likelihood of choosing the median x = z⌊(n+1)/2⌋ compared with the ordinary implementation, as the limiting ratio when n → ∞.

Part (c) defines a “good” split to mean choosing the pivot as x = zi with n/3 ≤ i ≤ 2n/3, asks by what amount the method increases the likelihood of a good split, and hints to approximate the sum by an integral. Part (d) asks you to argue that in the Ω(n lg n) running time of quicksort, the median-of-3 method affects only the constant factor.

Exercise 7.4-6 considers the same change to PARTITION, randomly picking three elements of A[p : r] and partitioning about their median, and asks for the approximate probability of getting worse than an α-to-(1 − α) split, as a function of α in the range 0 < α < 1/2.

The chapter on one page

Chapter 7 in eight cards. Each one points at the scenes that act it out.

Pivot first, then recurse

Partition A[p : r] around a pivot A[q], sort the low side and the high side recursively, and combine by doing nothing. Each pivot lands in its final place and never moves again. → 7.1 Quicksort

One sweep, four regions

PARTITION takes x = A[r] and walks j left to right, keeping ≤ x, > x, unknown and x as its loop invariant. r − p comparisons, Θ(n) time, no second array. → 7.1 Partitioning

Sorted input is the worst case

A 0 to n − 1 split at every level gives T(n) = T(n − 1) + Θ(n) = Θ(n²), on exactly the input where insertion sort takes O(n). Even splits give 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(n lg n). → 7.2 Worst and best

Any constant proportion is fine

A 9-to-1 or even 99-to-1 split still makes a tree of depth Θ(lg n) with at most n cost per level: O(n lg n). Bad splits mixed between good ones only raise the constant. → 7.2 Balanced splits, Average case

Let the pivot be random

RANDOMIZED-PARTITION swaps a random element of A[p : r] into A[r] before partitioning, so no particular input brings out the worst case, not even a sorted one. → 7.3 Randomized

Counting comparisons

The worst case is Θ(n²) by substitution, the maximum sitting at q = 0 or n − 1. With random pivots zi and zj meet with probability 2/(j − i + 1), so E[X] = O(n lg n). → 7.4.1 Worst-case bound, 7.4.2 Expected time

Other ways to partition

Hoare's original partition runs two indices toward each other. Equal keys make PARTITION quadratic; a three-way PARTITION′ that groups the keys equal to the pivot fixes that. → 7-1 Hoare partition, 7-2 Equal keys

Mind the stack and the pivot

Recursion depth is memory: TRE-QUICKSORT can still need Θ(n) stack, unless it recurses on the smaller side. A median-of-3 pivot makes good splits likelier but changes only the constant factor. → 7-5 Stack depth, 7-6 Median of 3

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

quicksort
A divide-and-conquer sort: partition around a pivot, then sort both sides the same way. Θ(n²) worst case, Θ(n lg n) expected.
pivot
The element a partition is built around. After partitioning it sits in its final sorted place.
PARTITION
The procedure that rearranges A[p : r] around the pivot A[r] and returns the pivot's new index q.
low side, high side
A[p : q − 1], whose values are at most the pivot, and A[q + 1 : r], whose values are at least the pivot. Either may be empty.
combine
In quicksort, nothing: once both sides are sorted, the whole subarray is sorted.
in place
Sorting inside the input array, with only a constant number of elements stored outside it at any time.
loop invariant
A statement true before every iteration. For PARTITION: the four regions ≤ x, > x, unknown and x.
worst-case partitioning
A split into n − 1 elements and 0 at every level. It gives Θ(n²) time and Θ(n) recursion depth.
best-case partitioning
The most even split, both sides at most n/2: Θ(n lg n), like merge sort.
balanced partitioning
A split of constant proportion, such as 9 to 1. The tree stays Θ(lg n) deep, so the time stays O(n lg n).
recursion tree
A picture of every call as a node labelled with its subproblem size; adding up each level's cost gives the running time.
good and bad splits
On random inputs PARTITION mixes balanced and lopsided splits. A bad split followed by a good one costs about as much as one good split.
randomized quicksort
Quicksort that picks its pivot uniformly at random from A[p : r]. Its expected time is O(n lg n) on every input of distinct values.
RANDOM(p, r)
Returns an integer from p to r, each equally likely. RANDOMIZED-PARTITION uses it to choose the pivot.
expected running time
The average over the algorithm's own random choices, for a fixed input.
element comparisons X
The number of times line 4 of PARTITION runs. Quicksort's running time is O(n + X) (Lemma 7.1).
Zij
The values zi < … < zj between two elements in sorted order. zi and zj are compared only if one of them is the first pivot chosen from Zij.
substitution method
Guess a bound, such as T(n) ≤ cn², and prove it by induction. Section 7.4.1 uses it for the worst case.
HOARE-PARTITION
Hoare's original partition: two indices move toward each other from the ends and swap pairs that are on the wrong sides.
three-way partition
PARTITION′ splits into <, = and > the pivot, so equal keys are finished in one pass.
tail-recursion elimination
Replacing the last recursive call with a loop, as TRE-QUICKSORT does. The other call still recurses, so the stack can still grow Θ(n) deep.
stack depth
The most stack space in use at any moment, here the deepest chain of pending calls.
median-of-3
Choose three random elements and partition around the middle one: a pivot near the median becomes more likely.
