The chapter in six stops

Each stop asks the same question: what did the method get, what was the true best, and what was promised?

  1. A way to keep scoreDivide what you got by the true best. A method's promise is a limit on that number, for every possible input.
  2. Prove it without knowing the bestFind a floor the best can't go below. Then show your answer is at most twice the floor. Vertex cover shows this in one picture.
  3. Tours, and a wallA tree gives a floor for a salesperson's tour, and a tour within 2. Without one sensible rule about distances, no fixed promise can exist unless P = NP.
  4. Greedy with a slowly growing limitAlways take the set that covers the most. Its limit grows with the input, but only like a logarithm.
  5. Coins and fractionsFlipping a coin per variable satisfies 7 of every 8 clauses on average. Allowing fractions in a cover, then rounding, costs at most double.
  6. Choose your own accuracyFor subset sum you set the allowed error yourself, and pay for it in time. The lists stay short, and the answer stays close.

35 · performance ratios

A score for close enough

Divide what you got by the true best. A method's promise is a limit on that score, for every possible input.

A city wants cameras at street corners, so that every street can be seen from at least one end. The fewest that can do it on this map is 3. A quick method returns 6. Is that good? It used twice as many as needed. Now suppose the method can prove it never needs more than twice as many, on any map. That is useful on a map far too large to solve exactly.

The problems in this chapter are NP-complete: nobody knows a fast way to find the exact best. The book lists three ways around that. If your inputs are small, a slow exact method may be fine. Your inputs may be a special case with a fast method. Or you can accept a near-best answer, fast. A method of that third kind, with a proof of how near, is an approximation algorithm.

Its score is the approximation ratio. A truck that wants the heaviest load is the opposite kind of problem: bigger is better. If the best load is 307 pounds and the method loads 302, the score is 307 ÷ 302, about 1.02. Either way, the score is never below 1, and 1 means perfect.

Read the maths
max(C ÷ C*, C* ÷ C) ≤ ρ(n)
C
The cost of the answer the method returned.
C*
The cost of the true best answer.
max
Take the larger of the two fractions. One is at least 1 and the other at most 1, so this works for both kinds of problem.
ρ(n)
The promise, a limit that may depend on the input size n. It must hold for every input.

With numbers: C = 6 and C* = 3 give max(2, 0.5) = 2. C = 302 and C* = 307 give max(0.98, 1.02) = 1.02.

A common mix-up: "2-approximation" does not mean it usually gets twice the best. It means it can never get worse than twice. The promise is a ceiling. Where a real answer lands under that ceiling is a separate question, and this page keeps asking it.

Try it: press Next to work out the score, one step at a time. Then drag the method got past the shaded zone, and see the promise break. Switch to Bigger is better to see the same score for a maximization problem.

What the book says

Many problems of practical significance are NP-complete, yet too important to abandon. The book gives three options: if the actual inputs are small, an exponential algorithm might be fast enough; you might isolate special cases solvable in polynomial time; or you can find a near-optimal solution in polynomial time, either in the worst case or the expected case. An algorithm that returns near-optimal solutions is an approximation algorithm.

An algorithm has an approximation ratio of ρ(n) if, for any input of size n, the cost C of its solution is within a factor of ρ(n) of the cost C* of an optimal solution: max(C/C*, C*/C) ≤ ρ(n). Such an algorithm is a ρ(n)-approximation algorithm. All solutions are assumed to have positive cost, so the ratios are well defined.

The ratio is never less than 1. A 1-approximation algorithm produces an optimal solution, and one with a large ratio may return a solution much worse than optimal. For many problems, polynomial-time algorithms with small constant ratios are known. For others, such as set cover, the best known ratios grow with n.

The 3 and 6 are the book's vertex-cover figure. The 302 and 307 are its subset-sum example. The cameras are this page's way of telling the first one, and the shaded zone uses the promises of Theorem 35.1 and of ε = 0.40 in Section 35.5.

35 · approximation schemes

Paying for accuracy

Some methods let you choose how close you want to get. The more accuracy you ask for, the longer they run.

Imagine a method with a dial. Set it to 0.5 and you are promised an answer within 1.5 times the best. Set it to 0.01 and you are promised within 1.01 times. The dial is called ε, the Greek letter epsilon: the extra error you are willing to accept. A method with this dial is an approximation scheme.

Everything turns on what a smaller ε costs. The book gives two made-up running times to show the difference. In the first, ε sits in the power of n, so halving it squares the time. That can still count as polynomial, because for any one fixed ε the power is a fixed number. It is a polynomial-time approximation scheme.

In the second, ε sits only in the multiplier, so halving it multiplies the time by 4, however large n is. That is a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme. You will build a real one for subset sum at the end of this chapter.

Read the maths
O(n^(2/ε)) against O((1/ε)² n³)
n
The size of the input.
ε
The allowed extra error. The promise is a ratio of at most 1 + ε.
O(…)
How the running time grows, ignoring a constant multiplier.

With numbers: take n = 20 and halve ε from 1/4 to 1/8. The first goes from 208 to 2016. The second goes from 16 × 8,000 to 64 × 8,000, which is 4 times as much.

Why the position of ε matters: a multiplier grows the time by a fixed factor when you halve ε. A power doubles the exponent, and squaring a large number makes it enormous.

Try it: press Next to halve ε again and again, and watch the two lines part. The vertical axis counts powers of 10, so each grid line is many times the one below. Drag n to see that only the first line cares how big the input is when ε shrinks.

What the book says

Some polynomial-time approximation algorithms achieve increasingly better ratios by using more computation time, so you can trade computation time for the quality of the approximation. An approximation scheme takes as input an instance and a value ε > 0 such that, for any fixed ε, the scheme is a (1 + ε)-approximation algorithm.

A polynomial-time approximation scheme runs in time polynomial in the size n of its input for any fixed ε > 0. Its running time can increase very rapidly as ε decreases, and might be, for example, O(n2/ε).

A fully polynomial-time approximation scheme has a running time polynomial in both 1/ε and n, for example O((1/ε)2n3). With such a scheme, any constant-factor decrease in ε comes with a corresponding constant-factor increase in the running time.

Both running times are the book's illustrations of what a scheme might cost, not costs of specific algorithms. This machine counts them as exact step counts, leaving out the constants that O hides. The time in years is this page's own arithmetic, at a billion steps a second.

35.1 · the vertex-cover problem

Take both ends

To touch every line with few points: pick any line, take both of its ends, and cross off everything those two touch.

Back to the cameras. The map has 7 corners and 8 streets. A camera on a corner sees every street leaving that corner, and every street must be seen. A set of corners that does this is a vertex cover: a set of vertices that touches every edge. The fewest possible is called an optimal vertex cover, and finding it is NP-complete.

The fast method is almost too plain. Pick any street that nobody watches yet. You don't know which end is the better one, so put a camera on both. Cross off every street those two cameras can see. Repeat until no streets are left.

It wastes cameras, and it doesn't try to be clever. On this map it uses 6 where 3 would do: exactly twice. Pick streets in a luckier order and it uses only 4. The next scene shows why it can never use more than twice the best, whatever the map and whatever the order.

Why the result is a cover: the loop stops only when no street is left. A street is crossed off only when a camera can see it. So every street is seen.

Try it: press Next to step through the book's picks: one change per step. Then choose A lucky order, or press Shuffle the edges, and run it again. The ceiling stays at 6 while the real answer moves.

What the book says

A vertex cover of an undirected graph G = (V, E) is a subset V′ of V such that if (u, v) is an edge of G, then either u or v is in V′, or both. Its size is the number of vertices in it. The vertex-cover problem is to find one of minimum size, and it is the optimization version of an NP-complete decision problem.

APPROX-VERTEX-COVER starts with C empty and E′ a copy of the edge set. While E′ is not empty, it takes an arbitrary edge (u, v) of E′, adds u and v to C, and removes from E′ every edge incident on either. Its running time is O(V + E) using adjacency lists.

Figure 35.1 runs it on a graph with 7 vertices and 8 edges. The chosen edges are (b, c), (e, f) and (d, g), and the cover is b, c, d, e, f, g. The optimal cover contains only three vertices: b, d and e.

The lucky order and the shuffles are this page's own runs of the same algorithm, which the book allows because line 4 takes an arbitrary edge. The cameras are this page's story.

Picking edges
35.1 · why it can never exceed twice the best

The proof in one picture

The picked streets share no corner, so any answer needs one camera per picked street, and the method used exactly two.

Here is the puzzle. How can you prove an answer is at most twice the best, when you never find out what the best is? You find a number the best can't go below. A value like that is a lower bound. Think of it as a floor under the best.

Look only at the streets the method picked. They share no corners, because each pick crossed off everything touching its two ends. Any set of cameras at all, including the very best set, must watch each of those streets. And one camera can't watch two of them, since they have no corner in common. So even the best set needs at least one camera per picked street. That count is the floor.

The method put two cameras on each picked street, and none anywhere else. So its answer is exactly 2 × the floor. The floor is at most the best. Put those together and the answer is at most 2 × the best. You never had to know the best.

Read the maths
|C| = 2|A| ≤ 2|C*|
A
The set of edges the method picked. |A| means how many.
C
The cover it returned. |C| is its size.
C*
A best possible cover. Nobody needs to know which.

With numbers: the book's run picks |A| = 3 edges. So any cover has at least 3 vertices, the method has 2 × 3 = 6, and 6 ≤ 2 × |C*|.

Why this pattern matters: compare your answer with a floor you can see, not with a best you can't. The rest of the chapter uses this same move, with a different floor each time. A set of edges like A, with no shared vertices and no room for another, is a maximal matching.

Try it: press Next through the book's picks. Then choose A lucky order. The floor drops to 2, under the true best of 3. A floor doesn't have to be tight to do its job.

What the book says

Theorem 35.1: APPROX-VERTEX-COVER is a polynomial-time 2-approximation algorithm.

Let A denote the set of edges that line 4 picked. To cover the edges in A, any vertex cover, and in particular an optimal cover C*, must include at least one endpoint of each edge in A. No two edges in A share an endpoint, since once an edge is picked, all other edges incident on its endpoints are deleted. So |C*| ≥ |A|. Each execution of line 4 picks an edge for which neither endpoint is already in C, so |C| = 2|A|. Together, |C| = 2|A| ≤ 2|C*|.

You might wonder how you can prove this when you don't even know the size of an optimal vertex cover. Instead, you find a lower bound on the size. The set A is a maximal matching: a matching to which no edges can be added and still have a matching. “The approximation ratio comes from relating the size of the solution returned to the lower bound.”

35.1 · what the 2 does and doesn't say

A ceiling, not a forecast

A 2-approximation can't do worse than double. It doesn't usually do double. Where it lands depends on the input.

Hearing "2-approximation", most people picture a method that gives you twice the best. That reading is wrong. Think of an elevator rated for 10 people. The rating is a limit it must never go past. It tells you nothing about how many people ride it on a normal Tuesday.

The book proves one thing here: the answer is never above 2 times the best, on any input. It doesn't say how often answers land near 2, and the book's own example lands exactly on 2. So here is this page's own experiment, not from the book. It makes random maps of 8 corners, runs the method with a random pick order, and finds the true best by trying all 256 subsets.

The result is honest in both directions. On maps crowded with streets, the method lands far under the ceiling. On sparse maps it often hits the ceiling exactly. There the picked streets stand alone, and the best set really does need only one camera each.

With numbers: a map where the method used 6 and the best is 4 scores 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5. That is not "wrong by a factor of 2". It is 50% more than needed.

Keep both halves: the ceiling is real, and some inputs reach it. The ceiling is also not the typical result on many inputs. A promise covers the worst case only, and that is why it can hold for every input.

Try it: press Next to see five maps one at a time, then 400 at once. Drag street chance from 10% to 90%, and watch the pile slide away from the ceiling. New random maps runs the experiment again with fresh maps.

What the book says

By the book's definition, a ρ(n)-approximation algorithm has its cost within a factor of ρ(n) of optimal for any input of size n. Theorem 35.1 gives ρ = 2 for APPROX-VERTEX-COVER. That is a statement about every input, so it limits the worst case. The book makes no statement about typical performance of this algorithm.

On the book's own example (Figure 35.1), the returned cover has 6 vertices and the optimal cover has 3, a ratio of exactly 2. So the bound is reached there. The chapter notes record that the vertex-cover problem has been studied extensively, with Hochbaum listing 16 different approximation algorithms, and that all the approximation ratios are at least 2 − o(1).

The random maps, the street chance, the averages and the 400-map chart are this page's own experiment, on tiny graphs of 8 vertices. They show what happens on those graphs and nothing more. They are not a claim from the book.

35.2.1 · the traveling-salesperson problem with the triangle inequality

Tree, walk, skip

Connect the cities with the cheapest tree, walk around that tree, then cut out every repeat visit. What is left is a good tour.

A salesperson starts in city a, must visit 8 cities once each, and has to come home. Which order is shortest? That round trip is a tour, and finding the shortest one is the traveling-salesperson problem. With 8 cities you can try every order. With 80 you can't, and no fast exact method is known.

Here distance is plain straight-line distance. That gives you a useful rule: going straight from one city to another is never longer than going through a third. This is the triangle inequality. Everything below depends on it.

The method has three moves. First build a minimum spanning tree: the cheapest set of roads that connects all the cities. A tree isn't a round trip, so second, walk around it, using every road twice, once each way. That full walk visits everything but repeats itself. So third, go through the visits and skip any city you have already been to, going straight to the next new one. The order you end up with is the tree's preorder, and it is a tour.

Read the maths
c(u, w) ≤ c(u, v) + c(v, w)
c(u, w)
The cost of going straight from city u to city w.
v
Any third city you might stop at along the way.

With numbers: from c to h is √5, about 2.24. Going through b costs √2 + √5, about 3.65. So skipping b can only save distance.

A common mix-up: the tree is not the salesperson's route, and it's not a tour. It has no way home. It is only the raw material: its cost becomes the floor in the next scene.

Try it: press Next and follow the bars on the right. The tree grows one road at a time, the walk doubles it, and each skip brings the cost down. Then choose Random cities to run it on a map nobody prepared.

What the book says

The input is a complete undirected graph with a nonnegative integer cost on each edge, and the goal is a hamiltonian cycle (a tour) of minimum cost. A cost function satisfies the triangle inequality if, for all vertices u, v, w, c(u, w) ≤ c(u, v) + c(v, w). Points in the plane with euclidean distance satisfy it. The problem is NP-complete even with this requirement.

APPROX-TSP-TOUR selects a root, computes a minimum spanning tree T from it using MST-PRIM, lists the vertices in the order a preorder tree walk first visits them, and returns that hamiltonian cycle. Even with a simple MST-PRIM, its running time is Θ(V2).

In Figure 35.2, the full walk visits a, b, c, b, h, b, a, d, e, f, e, g, e, d, a. The preorder is a, b, c, h, d, e, f, g. The tour costs approximately 19.074, and an optimal tour approximately 14.715, which is about 23% shorter.

The city coordinates are read from that figure, and the page's script reproduces both of the book's totals. The random maps are this page's own.

35.2.1 · why the tour stays within 2

A chain of three small facts

The tree costs no more than the best tour, the walk doubles the tree, and skipping never adds cost. So the tour stays within double.

The same move as vertex cover: find a floor. This time the floor is the tree. Take the best tour, whatever it is, and delete one road. What remains still connects every city, so it is a spanning tree. The cheapest spanning tree can't cost more than that one. So the tree you built costs no more than the best tour, even though you never saw the best tour.

Then the rest is arithmetic. The full walk uses each road twice, so it costs exactly 2 × the tree. Each skip replaces two legs with one direct leg, and the triangle inequality says that can't cost more. So the tour costs at most the walk, which is 2 × the tree, which is at most 2 × the best.

Take away the triangle inequality and that last link breaks, as Skip a stop shows. And like every promise in this chapter, 2 is a limit for the worst case. Many maps shows where tours actually landed on this page's random cities. The book adds a warning: this method is usually not the best practical choice, because other methods typically do much better.

Read the maths
c(H) ≤ c(W) = 2c(T) ≤ 2c(H*)
T
The minimum spanning tree.
W
The full walk around T.
H
The tour the method returns.
H*

With numbers: on the book's map, 19.074 ≤ 23.786 = 2 × 11.893 ≤ 2 × 14.715 = 29.43.

Try it: press Next to add one link at a time. In Skip a stop, drag the direct road's cost above 7 and see the shortcut become a detour. In Many maps, see 300 random maps against the ceiling.

What the book says

Theorem 35.2: when the triangle inequality holds, APPROX-TSP-TOUR is a polynomial-time 2-approximation algorithm for the traveling-salesperson problem.

Deleting any edge from a tour yields a spanning tree, and each edge cost is nonnegative, so c(T) ≤ c(H*). A full walk W traverses every edge of T exactly twice, so c(W) = 2c(T), giving c(W) ≤ 2c(H*). By the triangle inequality, deleting a visit to any vertex from W does not increase the cost. Keeping only the first visit to each vertex gives the same ordering as a preorder walk, so c(H) ≤ c(W), and c(H) ≤ 2c(H*).

“There are other approximation algorithms that typically perform much better in practice.” The chapter notes credit the algorithm to Rosenkrantz, Stearns and Lewis. Christofides gave a 3/2-approximation with the triangle inequality, and Arora and Mitchell showed a polynomial-time approximation scheme when the points lie in the euclidean plane.

35.2.2 · the general traveling-salesperson problem

When no promise can exist

Without the triangle rule, any fixed promise would answer a question nobody can answer fast. So none can exist, unless a famous open question falls.

Drop the rule, so costs can be anything. How good a promise can you now make? The answer is striking: no fixed number at all, not 2, not a million. The one exception is if P = NP, a famous open question.

The argument is a trap. Suppose someone hands you a fast tour method with a promise of ρ, say 2. Take any network and ask: is there a loop that goes through every point exactly once, using only its own lines? That loop is a hamiltonian cycle, and deciding whether one exists is NP-complete. Turn the network into a tour problem. Lines that exist cost 1. Lines that don't exist cost so much that one of them ruins a tour.

If a loop exists, the best tour costs 5 on a 5-point network, and the method must return something within 2 × 5 = 10. Every tour using a fake line costs at least 15. So it has to return the loop itself. If no loop exists, every tour costs at least 15. So reading the method's cost tells you the answer. A fast tour method would answer an NP-complete question fast.

Read the maths
c(u, v) = 1 if (u, v) is in E, otherwise ρ|V| + 1
E
The lines the network really has.
|V|
The number of points.
ρ
The promise the imagined method claims.

With numbers: |V| = 5 and ρ = 2 give a fake line cost of 2 × 5 + 1 = 11. A tour with even one fake line costs at least 11 + 4 = 15, which is more than 2 × 5 = 10.

Why any ρ fails: whatever ρ is claimed, the fake lines are priced from it, so they are always too dear to fit under the promise. A larger promise only widens the gap.

Try it: press Next for a network that has a loop. Then choose No loop and watch every dot jump past the line. Drag ρ as high as it goes: the gap never closes.

What the book says

Theorem 35.3: if P ≠ NP, then for any constant ρ ≥ 1, there is no polynomial-time approximation algorithm with approximation ratio ρ for the general traveling-salesperson problem.

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose such an algorithm A exists, with ρ an integer, rounding up if needed. Given an instance G of the hamiltonian-cycle problem, form the complete graph G′ on V, with c(u, v) = 1 if (u, v) is in E and ρ|V| + 1 otherwise. If G has a hamiltonian cycle, (G′, c) has a tour of cost |V|. If not, any tour uses an edge not in E and costs at least (ρ|V| + 1) + (|V| − 1) = ρ|V| + |V|, more than ρ|V|.

So A must return the hamiltonian cycle if one exists, and otherwise returns a tour of cost more than ρ|V|. Using A solves the hamiltonian-cycle problem in polynomial time.

This is a general technique: given an NP-hard decision problem X, produce in polynomial time a minimization problem Y whose “yes” instances have value at most k and whose “no” instances have value greater than ρk. Unless P = NP, Y then has no polynomial-time ρ-approximation algorithm. The chapter notes credit Theorem 35.3 to Sahni and Gonzalez.

The book gives this as an argument, not as numbered code: the listing writes it as a short procedure. The two networks are this page's own. Method A doesn't exist, so the machine acts it out with brute force, and returns a cheapest tour.

35.3 · the set-covering problem

Always the biggest bite

To cover everything with few sets, keep taking the set that covers the most of what is still uncovered.

You must form a committee for a project that needs 12 skills. Six people are available, and each has some of the skills. You want as few people as possible, with every skill held by at least one member. That is the set cover problem, and its decision version is NP-complete. Each dot in the picture is a skill, and each outline is a person: S1 to S6.

The obvious method is greedy. Count how many still-missing skills each person would add. Take whoever adds the most. Count again, because what each person adds has changed. Repeat until no skill is missing.

Watch the first choice. S1 has the most skills, 6, so it goes first. But S1 isn't in the best committee at all, which is S3, S4, S5. An early grab can be a real mistake, and greedy never takes one back. It ends with 4 people where 3 would do.

What to count: the size of a cover is how many sets it uses, not how many elements. Every cover contains all 12 elements, so that number would never differ.

Try it: press Next and watch the bars change as each pick makes the others worth less. At the last round two people tie. Change Tie goes to and run it again: the book allows either.

What the book says

An instance (X, F) consists of a finite set X and a family F of subsets of X, such that every element of X belongs to at least one subset in F. The problem is to find a minimum-size subfamily C of F whose members cover all of X. Its decision version is NP-complete, and the problem models many resource-allocation problems, such as forming a committee that has a set of needed skills.

GREEDY-SET-COVER picks, at each stage, the set S that covers the greatest number of remaining uncovered elements, breaking ties arbitrarily. It runs in O(|X| |F| (|X| + |F|)) time, and Exercise 35.3-3 asks for a linear-time version.

In Figure 35.3, X consists of 12 points. A minimum-size cover is S3, S4, S5, with size 3. The greedy algorithm produces a cover of size 4 by selecting either S1, S4, S5 and S3, or S1, S4, S5 and S6, in order.

35.3 · analysis of the greedy method

The logarithm limit

Each pick removes at least a fixed share of what remains, so what remains shrinks fast. That limits greedy to a logarithm times the best.

Say the best committee has 3 people, and 12 skills are missing. Those 3 people between them hold all 12. So at least one of them holds 12 ÷ 3 = 4 or more. Greedy takes whoever adds the most, so its pick adds at least 4. That leaves at most two-thirds of the missing skills.

The same reasoning works every round, since the best 3 can still cover whatever is left. So each round, what's left shrinks to at most two-thirds of itself. Things that keep shrinking by a fixed share reach zero fast. The number of rounds grows with the logarithm of the starting size. That is a very slowly growing number. It is 3 for 12 elements and 14 for a million.

Unlike the limit of 2 in earlier scenes, this limit grows with the input. Is that real, or only a weak proof? Greedy's trap is a family of inputs, built by this page, where two rows cover everything, yet greedy keeps taking blocks that look bigger. Its score really does grow as the input grows.

What the book says

Theorem 35.4: GREEDY-SET-COVER is a polynomial-time O(lg X)-approximation algorithm.

Let C* be an optimal cover and k = |C*|. Since C* also covers each subset Ui, at least one of its sets covers at least |Ui|/k new elements, so the set greedy picks does too, giving |Ui+1| ≤ |Ui|(1 − 1/k). Iterating, |Ui| ≤ |X|(1 − 1/k)i.

Since 1 + x ≤ ex, (1 − 1/k)k ≤ 1/e. Taking i = ck with c = ⌈ln |X|⌉ gives |X|(1 − 1/k)ck < 1, so |C| ≤ |C*| ⌈ln X⌉.

The book calls this a logarithmic approximation ratio: as the instance gets larger, the approximate solution may grow relative to an optimal one, but because the logarithm grows slowly, the algorithm may nonetheless give useful results.

The book states this bound and doesn't build an input that shows the growth. Greedy's trap is this page's own family: two rows of 2k − 1 elements, and blocks of width 1, 2, 4, … that tempt greedy away. It shows the ratio growing on those inputs only.

35.4 · a randomized approximation algorithm for MAX-3-CNF satisfiability

Flip a coin for each variable

Set every switch at random. Each three-way wish fails only 1 time in 8, so on average 7 of every 8 are granted.

Picture 10 guests and 4 light switches. Each guest makes three wishes, like "switch 1 on, or switch 2 off, or switch 3 on". A guest is happy if at least one wish comes true. You want as many happy guests as possible. In the book's words, the switches are variables, a wish is a literal, a guest is a clause, and the problem is MAX-3-CNF.

Now the surprise: don't think at all. Flip a fair coin for each switch. A guest's three wishes are about three different switches, so the coins behave independently. The guest is unhappy only if all three wishes fail, and that takes three coins landing the wrong way. That chance is 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8.

So each guest is happy 7 times out of 8, whatever the other guests want. Adding that up over 10 guests gives an expected 8.75 happy guests. Nobody can do better than all 10. So on average, the coins come within a factor of 10 ÷ 8.75 = 8/7 of the best.

Read the maths
E[Y] = 7m/8, so the ratio is at most 8/7
m
The number of clauses.
Y

E[Y]
The average of Y over all the ways the coins can land.

With numbers: m = 10 gives E[Y] = 70 ÷ 8 = 8.75, and m ÷ E[Y] = 10 ÷ 8.75 = 8/7, about 1.14.

The promise is about the average. One run of coins can land under 7/8, or over it. Unlike the earlier ceilings, this is a promise about the average of many runs, not about each single run.

Try it: start in One clause and step through all 8 ways three coins can land. Then open Many flips, and watch the average settle on 7/8. Try All 8 clauses: this formula can never satisfy more than 7 of 8, so the coins always get the very best.

What the book says

A randomized algorithm has an approximation ratio of ρ(n) if, for any input of size n, the expected cost C of its solution is within a factor of ρ(n) of the cost C* of an optimal solution. MAX-3-CNF satisfiability asks for an assignment that maximizes the number of clauses evaluating to 1. Each clause has exactly three distinct literals, and the book further assumes no clause contains both a variable and its negation.

Theorem 35.5: given n variables and m clauses, independently setting each variable to 1 with probability 1/2 and to 0 with probability 1/2 is a randomized 8/7-approximation algorithm.

A clause is not satisfied only if all three of its literals are set to 0, with probability (1/2)3 = 1/8, so it is satisfied with probability 7/8. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of satisfied clauses is 7m/8. Since m is an upper bound on the number satisfied, the ratio is at most m/(7m/8) = 8/7.

The book states this in words, so the listing writes it as a short procedure. Both formulas, the guests and the coin runs are this page's own. The true best comes from trying every setting.

35.4 · approximating weighted vertex cover using linear programming

Allow fractions, then round

Solve an easier version where half a camera is allowed. That cost is a floor. Round up every half, and you pay at most double.

Now corners have prices: 4, 3, 5, 2, 6. You want every street watched for the least money, not with the fewest cameras. The take-both-ends method ignores price, so it can buy something ruinous. Cheap hub shows it: it pays 10 where 1 would do.

Write the problem with numbers. Give each corner a value x: 1 if it gets a camera, 0 if not. Each street needs the x values of its two ends to add up to at least 1. Minimize the total price. With whole numbers only, that is as hard as before. So break the rule, and let x be any fraction from 0 to 1. That easier version is called a relaxation, and it is a linear program, which can be solved in polynomial time.

Half a camera means nothing in real life, so this answer is only a calculation. But it's a useful one. Every real cover obeys the looser rules, so the relaxed cost is a floor under the true best. Then comes the rounding: any corner with x of ½ or more gets a real camera.

What the book says

The minimum-weight vertex-cover problem gives each vertex a positive weight w(v), and asks for a cover of minimum total weight. The algorithm of Section 35.1 won't work here, because its solution could be far from optimal for the weighted problem.

35.5 · an exponential-time exact algorithm

Every possible load

To find the heaviest load that fits, keep a list of every total you can make. Each new box can double that list.

A truck can carry at most t pounds. You have a few boxes of different weights. How heavy a load can it take without going over? This is the optimization version of subset sum, and the decision version is NP-complete.

Here is the exact method. Start with a list holding only 0: the empty truck. For each box, copy the list, add the box's weight to every copy, and merge the two lists in sorted order. Drop duplicates, and anything over the limit, since a load already too heavy can only get heavier. At the end, the largest number in the list is the answer.

It works, and the answer is exactly right. The cost is the list. Each box can double its length, so 16 boxes can mean tens of thousands of entries, and 60 boxes could be more than any computer holds. The exception: if the weights or t are small numbers, the list can't get long, because it holds only distinct whole numbers up to t.

Read the maths
Pᵢ = Pᵢ₋₁ ∪ (Pᵢ₋₁ + xᵢ)
Pᵢ
Every total you can make using the first i boxes.
xᵢ
The weight of box i.
∪
Put two sets together: leave the box off, or put it on.

With numbers: boxes 1, 4, 5. P₁ = 0, 1. P₂ = 0, 1, 4, 5. P₃ = 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10.

Try it: press Next through the book's three boxes. Drag t down and see the cut at work. Then open Sixteen boxes and watch the list size follow the doubling line. The chart's vertical axis doubles at each step up.

What the book says

An instance is a pair (S, t), with S a set of positive integers and t a positive integer. The optimization problem seeks a subset whose sum is as large as possible but not larger than t. For example, a truck can carry no more than t pounds, and n boxes are to be loaded.

EXACT-SUBSET-SUM iteratively computes Li, the sorted list of sums of all subsets of the first i numbers that do not exceed t, using MERGE-LISTS, which merges two sorted lists and removes duplicates in O(|L| + |L′|) time. Once a subset's sum exceeds t, no superset of it can be optimal.

The length of Li can be as much as 2i, so it is an exponential-time algorithm in general, although it is polynomial in the special cases in which t is polynomial in |S| or all the numbers in S are bounded by a polynomial in |S|.

The boxes 1, 4, 5 are the book's example. The default limit of 10 keeps every total, and other limits are this page's own. The sixteen boxes are this page's own too, drawn at random between 1,000 and 9,999, with t set to half their total.

35.5 · trimming a list

Drop the near-duplicates

If two totals are nearly equal, you only need the smaller one. It can stand in for the other, and the list gets much shorter.

Suppose your list has 20, 21 and 22. You want an answer that's close, not exact. If a load of 22 is possible and you remember only 20, you are never off by more than 10%. So keep 20, and trim the other two.

The value kept is the representative of the ones dropped. It must be no larger than them, because a smaller load always stays under the limit if the larger one did. And it must be within a small factor, written 1 + δ. With δ = 0.1, a value can stand in for anything up to 10% bigger.

The method is one pass through the sorted list. Keep the first value. For each next value, ask whether the last value kept can stand in for it. If yes, drop it. If no, keep it, and it becomes the new stand-in.

Read the maths
y ÷ (1 + δ) ≤ z ≤ y
y
A value removed from the list.
z
The value kept to represent it.
δ
The Greek letter delta: how far off a representative may be, as a fraction. It is between 0 and 1.

With numbers: δ = 0.1, y = 22, z = 20. 22 ÷ 1.1 = 20, so 20 ≤ 20 ≤ 22 holds, and 20 can represent 22. But 23 ÷ 1.1 is about 20.9, more than 20, so 23 stays.

Why the list ends up short: each value kept is more than 1 + δ times the one before it. Values that grow by a fixed share each time reach any limit quickly. So the length depends on the logarithm of the largest value, not on how many values came in.

Try it: press Next. The shaded band shows what the current stand-in can cover. Drag δ to 0.01 and nothing is dropped. Drag it to 0.5 and most of the list goes.

What the book says

The key to a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme is to “trim” each list Li after it is created: if two values in L are close to each other, there is no need to maintain both of them explicitly. Given a trimming parameter δ with 0 < δ < 1, remove as many elements as possible so that every removed y has a remaining z with y/(1 + δ) ≤ z ≤ y. You can think of such a z as “representing” y in the new list.

For example, with δ = 0.1 and L = ⟨10, 11, 12, 15, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 29⟩, trimming gives ⟨10, 12, 15, 20, 23, 29⟩. The deleted 11 is represented by 10, the deleted 21 and 22 by 20, and the deleted 24 by 23.

TRIM runs in Θ(m) time on a sorted list of length m. A number is appended only if it is the first element of L or if it cannot be represented by the most recent number placed into the new list.

35.5 · a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme

You choose the accuracy

Trim after every box. The lists stay short, the answer stays close, and you decide how close, paying only a modest price for more.

Put the last two scenes together. Run the exact method, but trim the list after each box. Every total you lose has a representative not far below it. So when you finish, the largest number left can't be far below the true best.

One catch: errors pile up. A representative of a representative is further off, and that can happen once per box. So instead of trimming by your full allowance ε, trim by a small share of it: ε ÷ 2n, where n is the number of boxes. The book proves those small errors, compounded over n boxes, stay within 1 + ε.

This is the dial from the start of the chapter, made real. The limit on the list length grows with 1/ε, not with 2 to the power of n. So asking for twice the accuracy raises that limit by a fixed factor, however large the input. That is a fully polynomial-time scheme: a hard problem, where you buy as much accuracy as you need.

APPROX-SUBSET-SUM takes S, n, t and an approximation parameter ε with 0 < ε < 1. It repeats MERGE-LISTS, then TRIM with parameter ε/2n, then removes elements greater than t, and returns the largest value z* in Ln. The example has S = ⟨104, 102, 201, 101⟩, t = 308 and ε = 0.40, so δ = 0.05, and it finishes with L4 = ⟨0, 101, 201, 302⟩. The procedure returns 302, “which is well within” 40% of the optimal answer 307 = 104 + 102 + 101. In fact, it is within 2%.

Theorem 35.7: APPROX-SUBSET-SUM is a fully polynomial-time approximation scheme. For every y in Pi at most t, some z in Li has y/(1 + ε/2n)i ≤ z ≤ y. So y*/z* ≤ (1 + ε/2n)n, and that is at most eε/2 ≤ 1 + ε/2 + (ε/2)2 ≤ 1 + ε.

After trimming, successive elements differ by a factor of more than 1 + ε/2n. So each list holds at most log1+ε/2n t + 2 elements, which is less than 3n ln t/ε + 2. This is polynomial in the input size and in 1/ε.

Bins and machines

Two more simple rules, each with the same trick: a floor anyone can compute, and an answer that stays within twice that floor.

You have objects of different sizes and bins of size 1. What is the fewest bins that can hold everything? The first-fit rule takes each object in turn, and puts it in the first bin with room. If no bin has room, it opens a new one.

The floor here is easy: add up all the sizes. That total, rounded up, is a number of bins you can't get below. And first-fit can't waste much. At most one of its bins can end up half full or less, because a later bin's objects would have fit in that one.

Machines work the same way. Whenever a machine is idle, give it any job not yet started. The finishing time of the whole schedule is its makespan. Nothing can finish before the longest job does, or before the average machine's load is done. Each of those is a floor, and greedy stays within the sum of the two.

Read the maths

S
The total size of all the objects. The best is at least ⌈S⌉.
pₖ
How long job k takes.
m
How many machines there are.
Cmax
The makespan: when the last job finishes.

With numbers: the book's jobs are 2, 12, 4, 5 on 2 machines. 23 ÷ 2 + 12 = 23.5. A greedy schedule takes 14, and the best takes 12.

Every method, on one scale

The chapter on one page

The key ideas, in order.

A score for close

The approximation ratio is the larger of got ÷ best and best ÷ got, for the worst input. It is never below 1, and 1 means always optimal.

A floor makes the proof

You don't need the best to compare with it. Find a floor the best can't go below, then bound your answer by a multiple of that floor.

Vertex cover: 2

Pick an edge and take both ends. The picked edges share no vertex, so any cover needs one vertex per picked edge. You took two.

A ceiling, not a forecast

A 2-approximation can be anywhere from exactly right to exactly double. The theorem only rules out worse than double. The book's own figures land on 2, 1.30, 1.33 and 1.02.

Tours: 2, with a condition

When going direct never costs more than going via a third stop, a minimum spanning tree walked and shortcut gives a tour within 2 of the best.

Without that condition: no limit

A tour method with any constant ratio would answer the hamiltonian-cycle question fast. So none exists unless P = NP.

Greedy covers within a log

Taking the set that covers the most new elements gives a cover at most ⌈ln |X|⌉ times the best. The limit grows with size, but slowly.

Coins, on average

A clause of three literals fails only if all three are false, a 1 in 8 chance. So random settings satisfy 7/8 of the clauses on average, and the ratio is 8/7.

Relax, then round

Allowing fractions gives a floor you can compute. Rounding each x at ½ keeps every edge covered, and at most doubles the cost.

Trim the lists

Removing near-duplicates keeps subset-sum lists short. Trimming by ε/2n keeps the answer within 1 + ε of the best, in time polynomial in n, lg t and 1/ε.

That was the last stop. You sorted a hand of cards near the start of this book. You end it with problems nobody knows how to solve fast, and an honest way to work with them: say what you got, and prove how far it can be from the best.
