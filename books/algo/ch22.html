Single-Source Shortest Paths

A map app finds the cheapest route by starting every distance at ∞ and tightening it, one edge at a time, until nothing improves. This chapter shows that one move, relaxation, and the orders in which to do it: Bellman-Ford copes with negative weights, a sweep in topological order handles dags, and Dijkstra's greedy rule handles nonnegative ones.

22 · shortest paths, weights and optimal substructure

The cheapest way there

A road map is a graph: intersections are vertices, road segments are edges, and every edge carries a number, its weight (a distance, a travel time, a toll). The weight of a route is the sum of its edges, and the shortest-path weight δ(s, v) is the smallest sum over every route from s to v, or ∞ if there is no route at all. Your GPS does not list every route. It leans on a fact: a shortest path is made of shortest paths. Cut out any stretch of a cheapest route and that stretch is itself a cheapest route between its ends, because a cheaper stretch would have made the whole trip cheaper.

Weights need not be distances. They can be profits and losses, and then some are negative. That is fine until a negative-weight cycle is reachable: go around it once more and the trip gets cheaper again, forever, so δ = −∞ and no shortest path exists. A cycle that weighs zero or more never helps, so a shortest path can be taken without cycles: at most |V| − 1 edges.

To report the route and not just its weight, every vertex remembers a predecessor v.π, the vertex before it on the way from s. Follow π backward and you walk a shortest path home. Together the π edges form a shortest-paths tree, and it need not be unique.

Try it: in Weights, click a vertex and watch its walk from s add up on the tape. Press One more lap on d (a cycle that costs), then slide w(d, c) down to −7 and press it again: the same cycle now pays, and c and d turn coral like e, f and g. In Tree, click z, press Print path, then Other tree. In Variants, compare the four problems.

What the book says

The input to a shortest-paths problem is a weighted, directed graph G = (V, E) with a weight function w : E → ℝ. The weight w(p) of a path p = ⟨v0, v1, …, vk⟩ is the sum of the weights of its edges. The shortest-path weight δ(u, v) is the minimum of w(p) over paths from u to v when there is a path, and ∞ otherwise; a shortest path is any path with w(p) = δ(u, v). Edge weights can stand for time, cost, penalties, loss, or any other quantity that accumulates linearly along a path and that you want to minimize. Breadth-first search is a shortest-paths algorithm for unweighted graphs, where every edge has unit weight.

The chapter solves the single-source problem. It also gives the variants: single-destination (reverse every edge), single-pair (solve single-source from u; no known algorithm for the pair is asymptotically faster in the worst case) and all-pairs (run single-source from every vertex, though Chapter 23 often does better).

Lemma 22.1 is titled “Subpaths of shortest paths are shortest paths.” Its proof is cut and paste: if a cheaper subpath existed, splicing it in would give a path from v0 to vk lighter than p, a contradiction. Optimal substructure is a key indicator that dynamic programming or the greedy method might apply: Dijkstra's algorithm is greedy, and Floyd-Warshall, which finds shortest paths between every pair, is dynamic programming.

If no negative-weight cycle is reachable from s, then δ(s, v) is well defined for every v, even when it is negative. If one is reachable, no path to a vertex on or beyond the cycle can be shortest, and δ(s, v) = −∞. In Figure 22.1, δ(s, a) = 3, δ(s, b) = 3 + (−4) = −1, δ(s, c) = 5 and δ(s, d) = 11, since the cycle ⟨c, d, c⟩ weighs 6 + (−3) = 3 > 0. The cycle ⟨e, f, e⟩ weighs 3 + (−6) = −3 < 0, so e, f and g have δ = −∞. Vertices h, i, j form a negative cycle too, but s cannot reach them, so their δ is ∞. Dijkstra's algorithm assumes nonnegative weights; Bellman-Ford allows negative ones and typically detects a negative cycle.

A shortest path cannot contain a negative-weight cycle, nor a positive-weight one (removing it lowers the weight), and a 0-weight cycle can be removed without changing the weight. So shortest paths can be assumed simple, with at most |V| − 1 edges. To represent them, each vertex keeps a predecessor v.π, as in breadth-first search, and PRINT-PATH from Section 20.2 prints the path. The predecessor subgraph of the π values is, at termination, a shortest-paths tree: a rooted tree containing a shortest path from s to every vertex reachable from s. Shortest paths and their trees are not necessarily unique; Figure 22.2 shows two trees with the same root.

22 · relaxation and initialization

Tighten the guess

You do not know the cheapest way to anywhere yet, so every vertex starts with an honest guess: it is unreachable (v.d = ∞), except the source, which is 0 away from itself. That guess is a promise that the true weight is at most this big, an upper bound called the shortest-path estimate. Initialization sets every d to ∞, every predecessor π to NIL, and s.d to 0.

After that there is exactly one move. Pick an edge (u, v) and ask: is the trip to v through u cheaper than what v knows? That is u.d + w(u, v) against v.d. If it is cheaper, lower v.d and remember u as v's predecessor. If not, nothing changes. This is relaxing the edge, and it is the only way an estimate or a predecessor ever changes.

Every algorithm in this chapter is the same recipe: initialize, then relax edges. They differ only in how many times each edge is relaxed and in what order: Bellman-Ford relaxes every edge |V| − 1 times, while Dijkstra's algorithm and the dag algorithm relax each edge exactly once.

Try it: in One edge, drag v.d to 9 and press Relax, then drag it to 6 and press again: one case tightens, the other changes nothing. In Whole graph, press Initialize, then click edges of the graph, such as s → t and then t → x. Try an edge whose tail is still ∞, and relax the same edge twice.

What the book says

For each vertex v, the algorithms maintain an attribute v.d, which is an upper bound on the weight of a shortest path from the source s to v. We call v.d a shortest-path estimate. The Θ(V)-time procedure INITIALIZE-SINGLE-SOURCE leaves v.π = NIL for all v, s.d = 0 and v.d = ∞ for v ∈ V − {s}.

Relaxing an edge (u, v) consists of testing whether going through u improves the shortest path to v found so far and, if so, updating v.d and v.π. RELAX takes O(1) time. Figure 22.3 shows two examples with w(u, v) = 2 and u.d = 5: when v.d = 9 it decreases to 7, and when v.d = 6 it is unchanged. Relaxation is the only means by which shortest-path estimates and predecessors change.

A footnote explains the name: it may seem strange to use “relaxation” for an operation that tightens an upper bound, but the term is historical. A relaxation step can be viewed as relaxing the constraint v.d ≤ u.d + w(u, v); if it already holds there is no pressure to satisfy it, so the constraint is relaxed.

The section states, without proof until Section 22.5, six properties: the triangle inequality, the upper-bound property (v.d ≥ δ(s, v) always, and once it reaches δ it never changes), the no-path property, the convergence property, the path-relaxation property and the predecessor-subgraph property. It also fixes the arithmetic with infinities: for any real number a ≠ −∞, a + ∞ = ∞ + a = ∞, and for any real a ≠ ∞, a + (−∞) = (−∞) + a = −∞. All the algorithms assume the graph is stored as adjacency lists with each edge's weight, so each edge's weight is found in O(1) time.

22.1 · the Bellman-Ford algorithm

Relax everything, again and again

Some roads in this graph have negative weights, so a cautious plan that finishes the closest vertex first is not safe. Bellman-Ford does the opposite of clever: it relaxes every edge, in one fixed order, and then does the whole round again. One round is a pass. After the pass, estimates near the source are right, and the right numbers spread outward one edge per pass.

Why does |V| − 1 passes always do it? A shortest path is simple, so it has at most |V| − 1 edges. Pass 1 relaxes its first edge, pass 2 its second, and so on, in the right order, no matter what else gets relaxed in between. After the last pass every estimate equals δ(s, v). Then one more look at every edge asks: can anything still be relaxed? If so, the weights cannot be trusted and the procedure returns FALSE; if not, it returns TRUE.

The price is |V| − 1 passes over |E| edges: O(VE) when the graph has at least about as many edges as vertices.

Try it: press Step a few times and watch the edge order on the right: edges leaving a vertex that is still ∞ change nothing. Press Pass to finish a pass, then Play. Switch to Lucky order (edges listed along the shortest path s → y → x → t → z) and Play again: one pass does all the work and passes 2 to 4 change nothing.

What the book says

The Bellman-Ford algorithm solves the single-source shortest-paths problem in the general case in which edge weights may be negative. It returns a boolean value indicating whether there is a negative-weight cycle that is reachable from the source. If there is such a cycle, the algorithm indicates that no solution exists; if not, it produces the shortest paths and their weights. It progressively decreases an estimate v.d until it achieves δ(s, v), and returns TRUE if and only if the graph contains no negative-weight cycles reachable from the source.

Figure 22.4 runs the algorithm on a graph with 5 vertices. After initialization, the algorithm makes |V| − 1 passes over the edges; each pass is one iteration of the for loop of lines 2–4 and relaxes each edge once. In this example each pass relaxes the edges in the order (t, x), (t, y), (t, z), (x, t), (y, x), (y, z), (z, x), (z, s), (s, t), (s, y). Lines 5–8 then check for a negative-weight cycle and return the appropriate boolean value.

The running time is O(V2 + VE) with adjacency lists: the initialization takes Θ(V), each of the |V| − 1 passes takes Θ(V + E) (examining |V| adjacency lists to find the |E| edges), and the check takes O(V + E). Fewer than |V| − 1 passes sometimes suffice (Exercise 22.1-3), which is why the bound is O, not Θ. When |E| = Ω(V) it is O(VE); Exercise 22.1-5 asks for O(VE) even when |E| = o(V).

Lemma 22.2: if no negative-weight cycle is reachable from s, then after the |V| − 1 iterations v.d = δ(s, v) for every reachable v. The proof uses the path-relaxation property: a shortest path has at most |V| − 1 edges, and among the edges relaxed in the ith iteration is (vi−1, vi). Corollary 22.3: there is a path from s to v if and only if the procedure ends with v.d < ∞.

Theorem 22.4 (correctness): with no negative-weight cycle reachable from s, the algorithm returns TRUE, v.d = δ(s, v) for all v, and the predecessor subgraph is a shortest-paths tree. At termination, by the triangle inequality, v.d = δ(s, v) ≤ δ(s, u) + w(u, v) = u.d + w(u, v), so no test in line 6 returns FALSE. If a negative-weight cycle is reachable, the procedure returns FALSE.

22.1 · negative-weight cycles

When the numbers never stop falling

Put a negative-weight cycle where the source can reach it and shortest paths stop existing: every lap around the cycle makes some estimate smaller again. Bellman-Ford feels this as numbers that keep falling. A shortest path needs at most |V| − 1 edges, so if the estimates were ever going to settle they would have settled by the end of pass |V| − 1. When the cycle is there, they have not.

That is exactly what the final loop tests. It looks at every edge once more, and if any edge can still be relaxed, v.d > u.d + w(u, v), the procedure returns FALSE. The book shows that a negative cycle always trips this alarm by adding up the inequalities around the cycle: the distances cancel and leave 0 ≤ w(c) < 0, which is absurd. And in a graph without such a cycle the alarm never rings.

Knowing there is a cycle is not the same as holding it. One way to find it: do one more pass that relaxes the edges that still fail, remember the last vertex x it changed, and follow π backward. After |V| steps x is surely on the cycle, and one more walk around lists it. The book leaves finding the cycle as an exercise, so the second listing is a sketch of this idea, not the book's code.

Try it: with w(c, a) = 1 press Play and watch the table: every pass the numbers fall again. After the check says FALSE, the extra pass, the walk back along π and the cycle in coral follow. Then drag w(c, a) up to 3: the cycle now weighs +1, the passes settle, and the answer is TRUE.

What the book says

If a negative-weight cycle is reachable from the source, shortest-path weights are not well defined. Typically, the book says, the algorithm can detect and report its existence. Bellman-Ford does so with lines 5–7: after the |V| − 1 passes it checks every edge, and returns FALSE if any edge (u, v) still has v.d > u.d + w(u, v); otherwise it returns TRUE.

Theorem 22.4: if G contains a negative-weight cycle reachable from s, the algorithm returns FALSE. Proof by contradiction. Let the cycle be c = ⟨v0, v1, …, vk⟩ with v0 = vk, so that Σ w(vi−1, vi) < 0. Assume the algorithm returns TRUE. Then vi.d ≤ vi−1.d + w(vi−1, vi) for every i. Summing around the cycle, each vertex appears once on each side, so the d terms cancel (they are finite by Corollary 22.3), leaving 0 ≤ Σ w(vi−1, vi), which contradicts the cycle's negative weight.

Two of the chapter's exercises build on this. Exercise 22.1-4 asks for a modification that sets v.d to −∞ for every vertex with a negative-weight cycle on some path from the source. Exercise 22.1-7 asks for an efficient algorithm that lists the vertices of one negative-weight cycle, with a proof that it is correct. The procedure in the second listing is one possible answer in that spirit, checked here by running it, not taken from the book.

22.2 · single-source shortest paths in directed acyclic graphs

Relax in dependency order

A house goes up in a fixed order: foundation, then walls, then roof. A task can only start once the tasks that lead into it are done. A weighted dag is that picture: vertices are milestones, edges are tasks, and an edge's weight is how long its task takes. A dag has no cycles, so shortest paths are always well defined here, even when some weights are negative.

That order is the whole trick. First line the vertices up in topological order, so that every edge points to the right. Then sweep once from left to right and relax every edge that leaves the vertex you stand on. When you arrive at a vertex, every edge into it has already been relaxed, so its estimate cannot drop again. Each edge is relaxed exactly once, so the whole run costs Θ(V + E): no priority queue, no repeated passes.

Look at r: it stands before the source s in the order, so nothing ever reaches it and its estimate stays at ∞. Then try the second mode: negate every weight and the shortest path becomes the longest one. That is how a critical path in a PERT chart is found.

Try it: press Step once and watch the vertices slide into topological order, then step through a few vertices and watch the d labels fall as edges are relaxed. Play finishes Figure 22.5; the mint path is the shortest one to z. Switch to Longest path and Play again: same code, weights negated, and the path s, t, x, z of length 10 lights up. In Rust, watch how relax treats None as ∞.

What the book says

Shortest paths are always well defined in a weighted dag because “no negative-weight cycles can exist”, even with negative edges. Relaxing the edges in the order of a topological sort of the vertices computes shortest paths from a single source in Θ(V + E) time.

DAG-SHORTEST-PATHS topologically sorts the dag (line 1, Θ(V + E) by Section 20.4), calls INITIALIZE-SINGLE-SOURCE (line 2, Θ(V)), and then makes one pass over the vertices in topologically sorted order (lines 3–5), relaxing each edge that leaves the vertex. The loop of lines 4–5 relaxes each edge exactly once, an aggregate analysis, and each relaxation takes Θ(1), so the total is Θ(V + E), linear in the size of an adjacency-list representation.

Theorem 22.5: at termination v.d = δ(s, v) for all v, and the predecessor subgraph is a shortest-paths tree. The proof splits in two. A vertex that cannot be reached from s keeps d = ∞ by the no-path property. For a reachable vertex, processing in topological order relaxes the edges of a shortest path in the order they appear on it, so the path-relaxation property gives the result.

An application is finding critical paths in PERT chart analysis. A job consists of tasks, some of which must finish before others start. A dag models this: edge weights are task durations, and a critical path is a longest path through the dag. Its weight is a lower bound on the total time, even if as many tasks as possible run simultaneously. You can find one by negating the edge weights and running DAG-SHORTEST-PATHS, or by replacing ∞ with −∞ in line 2 of INITIALIZE-SINGLE-SOURCE and > with < in RELAX.

22.3 · Dijkstra's algorithm

The wave that never goes back

Drop a stone at the source. A wave spreads along the edges, and an edge's weight is how long the wave needs to cross it. The first time a wave reaches a vertex, a new wave starts from there. Breadth-first search is this picture when every edge takes one unit of time. With real weights, the vertex reached after the fewest edges is not always the one reached first, so a plain first-in-first-out queue no longer picks the right vertex.

Dijkstra's algorithm keeps a set S of vertices whose shortest-path weight is already final, and a min-priority queue Q of all the others, keyed by their estimate d. Each round it takes the vertex u in Q with the smallest d, moves it into S, and relaxes every edge that leaves u. That is a greedy choice: u is the closest vertex not yet finished, and because no weight is negative, nothing farther away can reach it sooner.

Every vertex leaves Q exactly once, so the loop runs |V| times. A relaxation can lower a neighbour's d, and then DECREASE-KEY lets that neighbour jump forward in the queue. Edges that lead back into S, such as t to y, are relaxed too, but they can never win.

Try it: press Step and follow one round on Figure 22.6: EXTRACT-MIN takes the top row of Q, the vertex turns black and joins S, then each edge leaving it is tested and a smaller d makes a row jump up the queue. Play runs it out. Pick Source z to run the same graph from another vertex. In Rust, the memory strip shows the heap's real buffer: std has no DECREASE-KEY, so an improved vertex is pushed again and its old entry stays behind as a grey, stale copy.

What the book says

Dijkstra's algorithm solves the single-source shortest-paths problem on a weighted, directed graph, but it requires nonnegative weights: w(u, v) ≥ 0 for every edge. With a good implementation its running time is lower than Bellman-Ford's. It generalizes breadth-first search to weighted graphs: a wave emanates from the source, and the first time it arrives at a vertex, a new wave emanates from that vertex. In a weighted graph the time to traverse an edge is the edge's weight, and a shortest path might not have the fewest edges.

The algorithm maintains a set S of vertices whose final shortest-path weights from s have been determined. It repeatedly selects the vertex u in V − S with the minimum shortest-path estimate, adds u to S, and relaxes all edges leaving u. DIJKSTRA replaces breadth-first search's queue by a min-priority queue Q of vertices keyed by their d values.

Line 1 initializes d and π, line 2 sets S = ∅, and lines 3–5 put every vertex into Q. The algorithm maintains the invariant Q = V − S at the start of each iteration of the while loop of lines 6–12. Line 7 extracts u, line 8 adds it to S, and lines 9–12 relax each edge (u, v) leaving u; whenever a relaxation changes v.d and v.π, the call to DECREASE-KEY updates the queue. No vertex is inserted after line 5 and each is extracted exactly once, so the while loop iterates exactly |V| times.

Because the algorithm always chooses the lightest, or closest, vertex in V − S, it uses a greedy strategy. Greedy strategies do not always yield optimal results, but Theorem 22.6 shows that Dijkstra's algorithm does compute shortest paths, which the next scene examines. 22.3 · Dijkstra's running time and why negative edges break it

Counting the queue, breaking the proof

Dijkstra's algorithm is a loop around a priority queue, so its running time is the price of that queue. Every vertex is inserted once and extracted once. Every edge is looked at once, and each look can lower one estimate, which costs one DECREASE-KEY. The three jobs cost different amounts in different queues: a plain array finds its minimum by scanning all V slots, a binary heap restructures itself in about lg V steps, and a Fibonacci heap makes DECREASE-KEY cost O(1) amortized.

That gives O(V²) for the array, O((V + E) lg V) for the binary heap and O(V lg V + E) for the Fibonacci heap. Which one wins depends on how dense the graph is, so the first mode lets you run the real algorithm on random graphs of any size and watch the three prices grow. The book's bound counts every edge as a DECREASE-KEY; real runs make far fewer.

The other half of the story is the proof. Dijkstra is right when no weight is negative, because the vertex it finishes next cannot be reached sooner by any detour. One negative edge lets a detour win. The second mode is a four-vertex graph where a finished vertex is improved after the fact, and the vertex downstream of it keeps the stale value.

Try it: in Queue cost, press Play and watch three bars grow, then drag edges per vertex up: the dashed worst-case outline of the binary heap jumps past the array's. In Negative edge, press Play with w(b, a) = −2: when b is finished it lowers a, which was already final, and c ends at 4 although the true distance is 3. Slide the weight up to 2 and play again. In Rust, the lazy-deletion check turns the late improvement into a stale entry that is thrown away.

What the book says

Dijkstra's algorithm calls three priority-queue operations: INSERT (line 5) and EXTRACT-MIN (line 7) once per vertex, and DECREASE-KEY (line 12). Each vertex joins S exactly once, so each edge in an adjacency list is examined exactly once, and DECREASE-KEY is called at most |E| times. The book calls this an aggregate analysis.

The running time depends on the implementation of Q. A simple one stores v.d in the vth entry of an array: INSERT and DECREASE-KEY take O(1), EXTRACT-MIN takes O(V) because it searches the whole array, for a total of O(V² + E) = O(V²). If the graph is sufficiently sparse, in particular E = o(V²/lg V), a binary min-heap that maps between vertices and heap elements does better: EXTRACT-MIN and DECREASE-KEY take O(lg V), building the heap takes O(V), and the total is O((V + E) lg V), which is O(E lg V) when |E| = Ω(V).

With a Fibonacci heap the time becomes O(V lg V + E): each of the |V| EXTRACT-MIN operations costs O(lg V) amortized, and each DECREASE-KEY, at most |E| of them, costs O(1) amortized. The book notes that Fibonacci heaps were historically motivated by the observation that Dijkstra's algorithm typically makes many more DECREASE-KEY calls than EXTRACT-MIN calls.

Theorem 22.6 shows that the algorithm, run with a nonnegative weight function, ends with u.d = δ(s, u) for every vertex. The proof is by induction on the iterations of the while loop: when u is extracted, take the first vertex y of a shortest path to u that is outside S, and its predecessor x in S. Because all weights are nonnegative, δ(s, y) ≤ δ(s, u); EXTRACT-MIN gives u.d ≤ y.d; and the convergence property gives y.d = δ(s, y), which squeezes u.d = δ(s, u). Exercise 22.3-2 asks for a graph with negative edges on which the algorithm fails and for the step of the proof that then breaks. The graph in the second mode is this page's own example.

22.4 · systems of difference constraints

Pairs that must stay close

You are scheduling five events. The glue takes 2 hours to set before the part may go on, so the part's time x2 must be at least x1 + 2. Every rule of this kind bounds the gap between two events: xj − xi ≤ b. A pile of such rules is a system of difference constraints, and a feasible solution is any vector x that satisfies all of them at once. You do not need the best one, any one will do.

Two things are worth noticing. Only differences matter, so if x is a solution, sliding every unknown by the same amount gives another one. And if every constant b is zero or positive, there is nothing to solve: put all the unknowns at the same value and every difference is 0. The puzzle appears only when some b is negative.

Then it can even be impossible. x1 − x2 ≤ 0 together with x2 − x1 ≤ −1 says that x1 is at most x2 and also at least one more, and adding the two inequalities gives 0 ≤ −1. How to find a solution, or to prove there is none, is the next scene's job. Here you check candidates by hand.

Try it: drag the five tokens along the number line, or click one and press the arrow keys, until no row is red. Or press Book's solution. Step runs the feasibility test one constraint at a time and stops at the first violated one, while the red rows show all of them. Press Shift all ▶ and see that nothing turns red. In With a conflict, try as hard as you like: one row is always red. In All b ≥ 0, press All equal.

What the book says

In the general linear-programming problem the input is an m × n matrix A, an m-vector b and an n-vector c, and the goal is a vector x of n elements that maximizes the objective function Σ cixi subject to the m constraints Ax ≤ b. The simplex algorithm, the most popular method, does not always run in polynomial time, though other algorithms do. Casting a problem as a polynomial-sized linear program immediately gives a polynomial-time algorithm, and faster algorithms exist for many special cases; single-pair shortest paths and maximum flow are special cases of linear programming.

Sometimes the objective function does not matter and it is enough to find any feasible solution, or to learn that none exists. In a system of difference constraints each row of A contains one 1 and one −1, with all other entries 0, so the m constraints on n unknowns each have the form xj − xi ≤ bk, where i ≠ j.

The book's example has five unknowns and eight constraints, (22.2) to (22.9). One solution is x = (−5, −3, 0, −1, −4), and x′ = (0, 2, 5, 4, 1) is another, with each component 5 larger. Lemma 22.8 says this is no coincidence: if x solves Ax ≤ b then so does x + d for any constant d, because (xj + d) − (xi + d) = xj − xi.

Such systems arise when the unknowns are times at which events occur, and each constraint says that at least, or at most, a certain amount of time must pass between two events, for example jobs in the assembly of a product. If every bk ≥ 0, a feasible solution is trivial: set all the unknowns equal. The problem is interesting only if at least one bk < 0. The added constraint in the second mode, and the third mode's system with its negative constants made positive, are this page's own illustrations.

22.4 · the constraint graph

Every inequality is a road

A rule such as x2 − x1 ≤ 3 says that unknown 2 may be at most 3 bigger than unknown 1. Draw it as a road from v1 to v2 that costs 3. Now take shortest distances d from any starting point: a detour never beats the shortest route, so d(v2) ≤ d(v1) + 3. That is the very same sentence as the rule, so the distances satisfy every rule at once.

Every unknown becomes a vertex and every rule an edge: the constraint graph. Shortest paths need a source that reaches everyone, so the graph gets one extra vertex v0 with a weight-0 edge to each of the others. Run Bellman–Ford from v0. If it returns TRUE, then xi = δ(v0, vi) solves the system, and so does the same list shifted by any constant, because the rules only look at differences. With n unknowns and m rules the graph has n + 1 vertices and n + m edges.

If it returns FALSE, the rules contradict each other. Add up the rules around a negative cycle: every unknown is added once and subtracted once, so the left side is 0 and the right side is the cycle's weight, which is below 0. A cycle of weight −1 turns the rules into 0 ≤ −1.

Try it: press Step eight times to turn the eight rules into edges, once more for v0, and keep stepping through the passes. The last step reads x off the vertices. Then pick Tighten (22.6) to ≤ 3 and run again: a rule that was exactly tight now closes a negative cycle, and the check fails. In Rust, d is a Vec<Option<i64>> where None is ∞.

What the book says

In a system of difference constraints, each constraint is a simple linear inequality xj − xi ≤ bk. The book views such a system A x ≤ b from a graph-theoretic point of view: the corresponding constraint graph is a weighted, directed graph with a vertex vi for each unknown xi plus an additional vertex v0. It has an edge (vi, vj) of weight bk for each constraint xj − xi ≤ bk, and an edge (v0, vi) of weight 0 for each unknown. The extra vertex guarantees that some vertex can reach all the others.

Theorem 22.9: if the constraint graph contains no negative-weight cycles, then x = (δ(v0, v1), …, δ(v0, vn)) is a feasible solution. The proof uses the triangle inequality, δ(v0, vj) ≤ δ(v0, vi) + w(vi, vj), on every edge. If the graph does contain a negative-weight cycle, there is no feasible solution: summing the constraints that the cycle corresponds to, each unknown is added once and subtracted once, the left-hand sides total 0, the right-hand sides total the weight of the cycle, and 0 ≤ w(c) < 0 is a contradiction. The vertex v0 cannot be on the cycle because it has no entering edges.

Because the constraint graph has edges from v0 to every other vertex, any negative-weight cycle is reachable from it. So if Bellman–Ford returns TRUE, the shortest-path weights give a feasible solution; if it returns FALSE, there is none. Figure 22.8 shows the constraint graph for the system (22.2)–(22.9), whose feasible solution is x = (−5, −3, 0, −1, −4). By Lemma 22.8, adding a constant d to every component gives another solution, for instance (0, 2, 5, 4, 1).

A system of m constraints on n unknowns produces a graph with n + 1 vertices and n + m edges, so Bellman–Ford solves it in O((n + 1)(n + m)) = O(n² + nm) time. Exercise 22.4-5 asks for a modification that runs in O(nm) time, even if m is much less than n.

Rules to roads to a solution
22.5 · triangle inequality, upper-bound and path-relaxation properties

Promises that relaxation keeps

Every algorithm in this chapter does one thing to distances: it relaxes edges, in some order, some number of times. Six promises survive any such sequence, and every correctness proof leans on them. Think of d(v) as a price tag that only ever goes down. It starts at ∞ (or 0 for the source) and is only ever replaced by the weight of a real path, so it can never dip below the true distance δ(s, v): the upper-bound property. Once it touches δ it is stuck there. A vertex that no path reaches keeps ∞ for ever: the no-path property.

The true distances obey the triangle inequality: δ(s, v) ≤ δ(s, u) + w(u, v), because going through u never beats the best route. So if u is already exact and you relax an edge (u, v) that lies on a shortest path, v becomes exact at once: the convergence property. Chain it along a shortest path: relax its edges in order, with any other relaxations mixed in between, and its last vertex is exact: the path-relaxation property. A shortest path has at most |V| − 1 edges, which is why that many passes of Bellman–Ford are enough.

Meanwhile the π pointers always form a tree rooted at the source. Once every d is exact, that tree is a shortest-paths tree: the predecessor-subgraph property. The machine checks all six against the true δ after every relaxation.

Try it: click any edge to relax it, in any order you like, and watch the six tiles keep holding while the bars close in on δ. Press Next path edge to relax s → y → x → t → z one edge at a time, or Run demo to do it with other relaxations mixed in between: when the fourth path edge is done, z is exact. Relax (q, y): q is unreachable, so nothing can change. In Rust, the same checks are assert! calls after 300 random relaxations.

What the book says

The chapter first states the properties without proof and Section 22.5 then proves them: the triangle inequality (Lemma 22.10), the upper-bound property (Lemma 22.11), the no-path property (Corollary 22.12), the convergence property (Lemma 22.14), the path-relaxation property (Lemma 22.15) and the predecessor-subgraph property (Lemma 22.17). The last five assume that the graph is initialized by INITIALIZE-SINGLE-SOURCE and that estimates and predecessors change only through relaxation steps.

The triangle inequality holds because a shortest path from s to v has no more weight than the particular path that takes a shortest path to u and then the edge (u, v). The upper-bound property, v.d ≥ δ(s, v), is proved by induction over the relaxation steps; a relaxed v.d equals u.d + w(u, v), which is at least δ(s, u) + w(u, v), which is at least δ(s, v). Once v.d reaches that lower bound it cannot decrease any further, and relaxation steps do not increase d values, so it never changes.

If u.d = δ(s, u) at any time before edge (u, v) of a shortest path is relaxed, then v.d = δ(s, v) at all times afterward. The path-relaxation property follows by induction along the path p = ⟨v0, v1, …, vk⟩, and it holds “regardless of any other relaxation steps that occur”, even when they are intermixed with relaxations of the edges of p. Lemma 22.2 uses it for Bellman–Ford: each of the |V| − 1 passes relaxes every edge, and a shortest path has at most |V| − 1 edges.

Lemma 22.16 shows that, assuming no negative-weight cycle is reachable from s, the predecessor subgraph forms a rooted tree with root s, and any sequence of relaxation steps maintains that. The proof shows that a cycle in it would force a negative-weight cycle, and that there is a unique simple path from s to each vertex of the subgraph. Lemma 22.17: after any sequence of relaxations that produces v.d = δ(s, v) for all v, the predecessor subgraph is a shortest-paths tree rooted at s.

Relax anything, in any order
problem 22-1 · Yen's improvement to Bellman-Ford

Sweep right, then sweep left

Bellman–Ford says nothing about the order in which a pass relaxes its edges, and the order matters. If the edges of a long shortest path are stored back to front, every pass fixes just one more vertex of it, and you need all |V| − 1 passes. Yen's improvement picks an order that never wastes a pass that way.

Number the vertices v1 … v|V| in any order and stand them on a line. Edges that jump to the right form Ef, edges that jump to the left form Eb. A pass is two sweeps: walk v1 → v|V| relaxing the Ef edges that leave each vertex, then walk back v|V| → v1 relaxing the Eb edges. Each sweep is a walk through an acyclic graph in topological order, so a whole run of path edges pointing one way is relaxed in a single sweep. A pass therefore advances any shortest path by a rightward run and a leftward run, and ⌈|V|/2⌉ passes are enough.

That halves the passes but not the asymptotics: a pass still touches every edge, so the running time stays O(VE) with a smaller constant. The machine's shortest path zig-zags (v1 → v4 → v2 → v5 → v3 → v6), turning at every step: the worst case for both.

Try it: press Play with Bellman–Ford: with the path stored backwards each pass makes one more vertex exact, five passes. Press Reorder edges and play again: the count depends on the luck of the list. Then pick Yen's order and play: after the third pass every distance is exact, whatever the edge list looks like. The tiles remember both results. In Rust, partition splits the edges and (0..n).rev() is the backward sweep.

What the book says

Problem 22-1 starts from the observation that the Bellman-Ford algorithm “does not specify the order in which to relax edges in each pass.” Before the first pass, assign an arbitrary linear order v1, v2, …, v|V| to the vertices and partition the edge set into Ef = {(vi, vj) : i < j} and Eb = {(vi, vj) : i > j}, assuming that the graph has no self-loops so that every edge belongs to one of them. Define Gf = (V, Ef) and Gb = (V, Eb).

Part (a) asks to prove that Gf is acyclic with topological sort ⟨v1, …, v|V|⟩ and that Gb is acyclic with topological sort ⟨v|V|, …, v1⟩. Each pass of the modified algorithm first visits each vertex in the order v1, …, v|V|, relaxing the edges of Ef that leave the vertex, and then visits each vertex in the order v|V|, …, v1, relaxing the edges of Eb that leave the vertex.

Part (b) asks to prove that if the graph contains no negative-weight cycles reachable from the source s, then after only ⌈|V|/2⌉ passes over the edges, v.d = δ(s, v) for all vertices v. Part (c) asks whether this scheme improves the asymptotic running time of the Bellman-Ford algorithm.

Same graph, two orders
problem 22-3 · arbitrage

Money that grows in a circle

One US dollar buys 64 Indian rupees, one rupee buys 1.8 Japanese yen, and one yen buys 0.009 dollars. Go once around that loop and 1 dollar becomes 64 × 1.8 × 0.009 = 1.0368 dollars: a profit of 3.68% for nothing. That is arbitrage. With n currencies and a table R of exchange rates, the question is whether some loop of exchanges multiplies money by more than 1.

Shortest paths add, they don't multiply, so take logarithms. A product of rates exceeds 1 exactly when the sum of the numbers −ln R[i, j] around the loop is below 0. Give each pair of currencies an edge of weight −ln R[i, j] and an arbitrage loop is a negative-weight cycle. Bellman–Ford finds one: after |V| − 1 passes any edge that can still be relaxed proves a negative cycle exists, and walking the π pointers back lands on it, which also prints the sequence of currencies.

While no such loop exists the same distances are useful in themselves: −d(c) is the logarithm of the best amount of currency c that one dollar can become. One floating-point precaution: rounding noise can fake a tiny negative cycle, so the code only counts improvements larger than a small epsilon.

Try it: drag the JPY → USD rate. At 0.0087 and above the loop makes money, at 0.0086 and below it loses (break-even is 1 ÷ 115.2 ≈ 0.00868). Switch the edge labels to Weights −ln R and see the products turn into sums. Press Play: two passes, then the check pass still finds an edge to relax, and walking back along π reveals the loop. In Rust, the memory strip shows why Vec<Vec<f64>> is a buffer of row pointers.

What the book says

“Arbitrage is the use of discrepancies in currency exchange rates to transform one unit of a currency into more than one unit of the same currency.” In the book's example one U.S. dollar buys 64 Indian rupees, one Indian rupee buys 1.8 Japanese yen, and one Japanese yen buys 0.009 U.S. dollars, so a trader who converts around the loop turns 1 dollar into 64 × 1.8 × 0.009 = 1.0368 dollars, a profit of 3.68%.

The problem gives n currencies c1, c2, …, cn and an n × n table R of exchange rates, such that 1 unit of currency ci buys R[i, j] units of currency cj. Part (a) asks for an efficient algorithm to determine whether there exists a sequence of currencies ⟨ci1, ci2, …, cik⟩ such that R[i1, i2] · R[i2, i3] ⋯ R[ik−1, ik] · R[ik, i1] > 1, and to analyze its running time. Part (b) asks for an efficient algorithm that prints such a sequence if one exists, again with its running time.

The chapter's own tool for such questions is the Bellman-Ford algorithm, which returns TRUE if and only if the graph contains no negative-weight cycles that are reachable from the source. The step from products of rates to sums of weights, and the whole machine on this page, are this page's own illustration and not the book's text.

The chapter on one page

Chapter 22 in eight cards. Each one points at the scenes that act it out.

Cheapest, not fewest

Each edge has a weight, a path weighs the sum of its edges, and δ(s, v) is the least weight of any path from s to v: ∞ when there is no path, −∞ when a negative-weight cycle lies on some path. A shortest path needs no cycle, so it has at most |V| − 1 edges, and each vertex's predecessor v.π traces it back, forming a shortest-paths tree that need not be unique. → 22 Shortest paths

One move: relax

Every vertex keeps an estimate v.d, an upper bound on δ(s, v), starting at ∞ (0 for the source). Relaxing an edge (u, v) tests v.d > u.d + w(u, v) and, if true, lowers v.d and sets v.π = u, in O(1). It is the only way the estimates and predecessors change; the algorithms differ in how often and in what order they relax. → 22 Relaxation

Bellman-Ford: everything, again

Relax every edge, in any fixed order, |V| − 1 times; one more look at every edge then returns FALSE if something can still be relaxed, TRUE if not. Negative weights are fine, the answer is right unless a negative cycle is reachable, and the cost is O(VE). A negative cycle is the one thing the estimates cannot settle, and following π backward from a vertex it keeps changing finds the cycle. → 22.1 Bellman-Ford, 22.1 Negative cycles

In a dag, one sweep

Sort the vertices topologically, then relax each vertex's out-edges once, in that order. Every edge into a vertex is relaxed before its turn, so its estimate is final: Θ(V + E), with negative weights allowed because no cycle exists. Negating the weights turns it into a longest-path search, which is how a critical path in a PERT chart is found. → 22.2 DAGs

Dijkstra: the closest first

With no negative weights, keep a set S of finished vertices and a min-priority queue of the rest keyed by d. Extract the closest, add it to S, relax its out-edges. The cost depends on the queue: O(V2) with an array, O((V + E) lg V) with a binary heap, O(V lg V + E) with a Fibonacci heap. One negative edge can improve a vertex after it is finished, and the proof, and the answer, fail. → 22.3 Dijkstra, 22.3 Dijkstra's cost

Inequalities are roads

A system of difference constraints xj − xi ≤ b becomes a graph with an edge of weight b from vi to vj and an extra source v0 with weight-0 edges to all. No negative cycle means xi = δ(v0, vi) is a feasible solution; a negative cycle means none exists. Bellman-Ford decides it in O(n2 + nm). → 22.4 Difference constraints, 22.4 Constraint graph

Promises relaxation keeps

Six properties hold after any sequence of relaxations: the triangle inequality, the upper bound (v.d ≥ δ(s, v), and once equal it never changes), no path means ∞ for ever, convergence, path-relaxation (relax a shortest path's edges in order, with other relaxations in between, and its end is exact), and the predecessor subgraph becoming a shortest-paths tree. → 22.5 Why it works

Words to know

The book's words from this chapter, in plain language.

weight function w, path weight w(p)
A number on every edge, possibly negative. The weight of a path is the sum of the weights of its edges.
shortest-path weight δ(s, v)
The least weight of any path from s to v. It is ∞ if there is no path, and −∞ if a negative-weight cycle lies on some path from s to v.
negative-weight cycle
A cycle whose edges add up to less than 0. Go around it again and the walk gets lighter, so no shortest path exists to anything beyond it.
single-source, single-pair, single-destination, all-pairs
One source to every vertex (this chapter); one pair; every vertex to one destination (reverse the edges); every pair (Chapter 23).
optimal substructure
A shortest path contains shortest paths: every subpath of a shortest path is itself a shortest path (Lemma 22.1).
predecessor v.π
The vertex just before v on the best path found so far, or NIL. Following π backward from v walks a path to the source.
shortest-paths tree
A rooted tree holding a shortest path from the source to every reachable vertex. When the algorithms finish, the π edges form one, and it is not necessarily unique.
shortest-path estimate v.d
An upper bound on the weight of a shortest path from s to v. It starts at ∞ and only ever goes down.
relaxation
Testing whether going through u improves the path to v and, if so, lowering v.d and setting v.π = u. The only operation that changes estimates.
INITIALIZE-SINGLE-SOURCE
Sets every d to ∞, every π to NIL and s.d to 0. Every algorithm in the chapter starts with it.
pass
One round in which Bellman-Ford relaxes every edge once. It makes |V| − 1 of them, then checks.
Bellman-Ford
The algorithm that allows negative weights and reports, with TRUE or FALSE, whether a negative-weight cycle is reachable from the source. Time O(VE).
topological order
A line-up of a dag's vertices in which every edge points to the right. Relaxing in this order solves the dag in Θ(V + E).
critical path
A longest path through a dag of tasks: the least time the whole job can take. Negate the weights and run the shortest-path code to find it.
Dijkstra's algorithm, set S
For nonnegative weights: repeatedly move the vertex with the smallest estimate from the queue into S, the set whose weights are final, and relax its out-edges. A greedy algorithm.
min-priority queue, DECREASE-KEY
A structure that hands back the smallest key; DECREASE-KEY lowers one key when a relaxation improves it. Its implementation sets Dijkstra's running time.
system of difference constraints
A set of inequalities each of the form xj − xi ≤ b. A feasible solution satisfies all of them at once.
constraint graph
One vertex per unknown, one edge of weight b from vi to vj per constraint, plus a source v0 with weight-0 edges to all of them.
triangle inequality
For every edge, δ(s, v) ≤ δ(s, u) + w(u, v): going through u never beats the best route.
upper-bound property
Always v.d ≥ δ(s, v), and once v.d reaches δ(s, v) it never changes.
path-relaxation property
Relax the edges of a shortest path in order, with any other relaxations mixed in between, and its last vertex ends with d = δ.
Ef, Eb (Yen)
With the vertices in a fixed order, the edges that go forward and the edges that go backward. Relaxed in alternating sweeps, they need only ⌈|V|/2⌉ passes.
arbitrage
Turning one unit of a currency into more than one by a loop of exchanges. With weights −ln R[i, j] it is a negative-weight cycle.

Rust words from the code panels

For readers coming from another language. Each one shows up in a listing above.

i64::MAX / 2 as ∞
A sentinel for the book's ∞: half of the largest i64 leaves room to add a weight without overflowing. It still needs a guard, d[u] != INF, because INF + w with a negative w would look like a real distance.
Option<i64> as ∞, let … else
None is ∞ and Some(x) a distance, so the compiler makes you decide what ∞ + w means. let Some(du) = d[u] else { return false }; says that an unreached u changes nothing.
two &mut slices, a bool result
relax(d: &mut [i64], pi: &mut [Option<usize>], …) -> bool borrows two different Vecs mutably at once, which is allowed, and answers "did the estimate decrease?" in the return value.
tuples as edges
Vec<(usize, usize, i64)> is the edge list, and for &(u, v, w) in edges copies each tuple out of the borrow and splits it into names in one pattern.
Option or Result as TRUE / FALSE
A return type such as Option<Vec<i64>> or Result<Vec<i64>, Vec<usize>> carries the verdict and the data together: Some or Ok holds the distances, None or Err says no, and may carry the cycle. A trailing ? hands a None upward.
moving a Vec into a call
negative_cycle(n, edges, d, pi) takes d and pi by value: ownership moves into the callee, which is fine because the caller is about to return. Chasing parents uses pi[x].unwrap(), safe because a relaxed vertex always has one.
windows, map, collect, sum, partition, rev
Iterator adapters stand in for loops: windows(2) yields neighbouring pairs of a path, map(..).collect() builds a new Vec, .sum() or .product() folds numbers into one, partition splits the edges into two Vecs by a closure, and (0..n).rev() sweeps backward.
BinaryHeap with Reverse
std's heap pops the largest item, so pushing Reverse((d, v)) makes a min-queue. There is no DECREASE-KEY: push the improved entry again and skip a stale one (d too big) when it comes out, which is called lazy deletion.
VecDeque
A queue with O(1) push_back and pop_front, used to peel vertices of in-degree 0 off a dag and produce a topological order.
assert! and assert_eq!
Checks that panic with a message when false. They turn the properties of Section 22.5 into running tests after every random relaxation, and compare two algorithms' answers.
f64::INFINITY, ln and an epsilon
Floats have a real ∞ and a natural logarithm, x.ln(), so a product of rates becomes a sum of weights. Rounding noise can fake a tiny negative cycle, so an improvement counts only if it beats a small EPS.
a seeded RNG struct, closures
A few lines of xorshift in a struct Rng give repeatable "random" graphs with no crate. A closure such as |i, j| -rates[i][j].ln() borrows its surroundings and is passed or called like a function.
